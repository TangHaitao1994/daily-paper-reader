Abstract 

Spoken Dialogue Models (SDMs) typically share a speech-token space for generation and synthesis, leading to long autoregressive se-quences and costly inference. Lower-rate speech tokenizers shorten sequences but may introduce compression–quality trade-offs and costly replacement without breaking this cou-pling. To address this limitation, we propose CoSTA, a compatibility-preserving post-hoc framework that decouples generation and syn-thesis token spaces while retaining the original speech tokenizer and decoder. CoSTA extends the generation token space with compressed representations via mean-initialized vocabulary extension, pronunciation adaptation, and dia-logue adaptation, followed by deterministic ex-pansion back to the original speech-token space before synthesis. On bilingual speech-dialogue benchmarks, CoSTA achieves approximately 

1.7× expanded speech-token generation speed, lower full-response latency, and higher through-put while preserving response quality. Analy-ses show more sustained interaction under lim-ited budgets and a practical trade-off among compression, decoding efficiency, and qual-ity. Further experiments demonstrate cross-architecture transfer and complementarity to low-rate tokenization. 1

1 Introduction 

Spoken dialogue models (SDMs) enable end-to-end speech interaction by modeling speech input and generating text tokens together with speech tokens, which are then synthesized into waveforms by a speech decoder (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2025a; KimiTeam et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Existing SDMs typically use the same token space for autoregressive genera-tion and speech synthesis, tightly coupling genera-tion with the representation required by the speech

These speech-token sequences are of-ten overly long, increasing autoregressive genera-tion cost, complicating text-speech alignment, and making long-context speech modeling more diffi-cult (Shen et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024). In practical interaction, this inefficiency slows responses and consumes generation budgets faster, limiting sus-tained interaction and spoken-response duration. One solution is to design speech tokenizers with lower token rates (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025c; Guo et al., 2026). However, aggressive rate reduction may introduce a compression–quality trade-off, and replacing the tokenizer of an existing SDM may require retraining or adapting the tokenizer, paired speech decoder, and SDM together. More fundamentally, this route still couples generation and synthesis to the same representation. We there-fore ask a different question: Can we decouple the token space used for autoregressive generation from that required for speech synthesis, while pre-serving the original speech tokenizer and decoder? 

Speech-token sequences produced by existing tokenizers exhibit substantial redundancy and can be losslessly compressed using BPE (Gage, 1994; Sennrich et al., 2016; Radford et al., 2019). Across 1arXiv:2610.21143v1 [cs.CL] 15 Oct 2026 diverse tokenizer, data, and language settings, we uncover an empirical scaling regularity: BPE com-pression shortens speech-token sequences with in-creasing vocabulary size, following an approximate log-linear trend on held-out speech. This persis-tent compressibility suggests a route to more effi-cient generation without altering synthesis: using a compact autoregressive representation while deter-ministically recovering the original speech-token sequence required by the decoder. This insight motivates generation–synthesis token-space decoupling and CoSTA, short for Co mpressed Speech Token Adaptation, acompatibility-preserving post-hoc token-space adaptation framework for existing large-scale SDMs. As illustrated in Figure 1, CoSTA extends the autoregressive generation token space with compressed representations while preserving the original synthesis interface. CoSTA adapts the SDM to generate these compressed representations through vocabulary extension with mean initial-ization, pronunciation adaptation, and dialogue adaptation. At inference time, generated com-pressed units are deterministically expanded back to the original speech-token representations before synthesis, reducing autoregressive speech-token steps while leaving the original speech tokenizer and decoder unchanged. We validate CoSTA on two representative SDM architectures. In experiments with Step-Audio 2 mini (Wu et al., 2025), CoSTA achieves 1.736 ×

and 1.659 × relative expanded speech-token gener-ation speed in Chinese and English, respectively, while preserving response quality according to both automatic and human evaluations. These gains further translate into lower full-response latency, higher throughput, and more sustained interaction under limited budgets. Vocabulary scaling further reveals a practical trade-off among compression, decoding efficiency, and response quality. Exper-iments on a different SDM architecture further demonstrate cross-architecture transfer and com-plementarity to low-rate speech tokenization. From scaling regularity to generation–synthesis token-space decoupling. The original speech-token space produces long sequences for autore-gressive generation. The empirical scaling reg-ularity reveals persistent compressibility: larger BPE vocabularies yield shorter speech-token se-quences, motivating their use for autoregressive generation. However, the speech decoder still re-quires the original token space. This motivates 

generation–synthesis token-space decoupling : the SDM generates compact BPE sequences in an ex-tended token space, which are deterministically expanded back to the original space for synthesis. We instantiate this paradigm through the post-hoc adaptation framework CoSTA in Section 3. 

3 Method 

Section 2 motivates generation–synthesis token-space decoupling. Realizing this decoupling in an existing SDM, however, requires adapting the model while preserving compatibility with its orig-inal tokenizer–decoder pipeline. We therefore pro-pose CoSTA, a compatibility-preserving post-hoc token-space adaptation framework. CoSTA adapts the SDM to generate compressed sequences in the extended token space Z, while deterministic expan-sion π(z) = s restores the original decoder-facing representation in S before waveform synthesis. As shown in Figure 3, CoSTA consists of three steps: (1) vocabulary extension with mean-based initialization, (2) pronunciation adaptation on com-pressed TTS data, and (3) dialogue adaptation on compressed speech-to-speech dialogue data. At in-ference time, only a lightweight deterministic BPE expansion is inserted before the unchanged speech decoder. We detail each step below.

3.2 Pronunciation Adaptation 

After vocabulary extension, the SDM has an ex-tended generation token space but has not yet learned to use the added BPE units reliably. We therefore perform pronunciation adaptation on compressed TTS data. Following interleaved-generation SDMs such as Step-Audio 2 mini (Wu et al., 2025), each target sequence contains both text and speech tokens. We apply BPE compres-sion only to speech tokens and leave text tokens unchanged. Each example consists of a text prompt p and a target response sequence y, where y contains interleaved text tokens and original speech tokens. 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup Implementation. We use the 8B Step-Audio 2 mini (Wu et al., 2025) as the SDM for its strong open-source support, simple architecture, and com-petitive dialogue performance; it uses S3Tokenizer V2 at 25 Hz. For pronunciation adaptation, we sam-ple 300 hours each of Chinese and English speech from Emilia (He et al., 2024) and convert them to a unified timbre with Seed-VC (Liu, 2024). For dia-logue adaptation, we follow OpenS2S (Wang et al., 2025a) to construct approximately 50K speech-dialogue examples per language, with assistant speech synthesized by CosyVoice 2 (Du et al., 2024b) using a consistent voice. We learn the BPE vocabulary from dialogue speech-token se-quences and use 80K added units, supported by the vocabulary-size trade-off analysis in Section 4.4. Vocabulary extension adds only embedding and LM-head rows, about 6% of the original model parameters. We train CoSTA-ZH, CoSTA-EN, and CoSTA-MIX for Chinese, English, and bilingual adaptation; further training details and data statis-tics are provided in Appendices A and B.1. 

Baselines and benchmarks. We compare against the original Step-Audio 2 mini and Vanilla-FT-ZH/EN/MIX, which use the same adaptation data and training configuration while retaining the original speech tokens. This controls for training-data scale and isolates the effect of BPE-compressed token adaptation. Qwen2.5-Omni (Xu et al., 2025a), Kimi-Audio (KimiTeam et al., 2025), and GLM-4-Voice (Zeng et al., 2024) serve as external quality references. We evaluate on 12 datasets from URO-Bench (Yan et al., 2025), covering aligned Chinese and English settings and spanning summarization, mathematical and common-sense reasoning, open-ended dialogue, and real-world conversation, providing broad coverage of realistic spoken-dialogue scenarios.

4.3 Ablation Study 

Table 4 shows that the adaptation components play complementary roles. Timbre conversion mainly benefits response quality, while progressive adapta-tion modestly improves compressed-token genera-tion. Pronunciation adaptation further strengthens compression and text-speech alignment, supporting reliable generation in the extended token space. Di-alogue adaptation has the largest effect: removing it causes G-Eval to drop sharply from 72.27 to 24.35, indicating that learning compressed pronunciation alone is insufficient to recover spoken-dialogue ca-pability. Appendix C.8 further analyzes the as-sociated compressed-token behavior and response failures. Figures 6 and 8 further show that mean initialization provides faster convergence and more stable gradients than the alternatives; detailed ini-tialization strategies are described in Appendix C.2.

5 Related Work 

Spoken dialogue models. Existing SDMs dif-fer in how text and speech generation are coupled. Think–Talk architectures use separate Transformer backbones (Fang et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025b; Fang et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025a,b; Team, 2026; Cui et al., 2026; Défossez et al., 2024; Team et al., 2025a); parallel-generation architectures use multiple output heads (KimiTeam et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Xie and Wu, 2024a,b; Team et al., 2025b); and interleaved-generation architectures model interleaved text–speech se-quences (Zhang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025b; Zeng et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2025). Despite these dif-ferences, the generation token space is typically coupled to the space expected by the speech de-coder. CoSTA instead decouples generation and synthesis through compatibility-preserving post-hoc adaptation, enabling compact, losslessly ex-pandable speech-token generation while retaining the original speech tokenizer and decoder. 

Discrete speech tokens. Discrete speech tokens include acoustic tokens from codec models (Zeghi-dour et al., 2022; Défossez et al., 2023; Kumar et al., 2023; Ji et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2024) and semantic tokens from discriminative (Du et al., 2024a,b, 2025) or self-supervised speech mod-els (Baevski et al., 2020a,b; Hsu et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022; Chung et al., 2021). Lower-rate tok-enizers shorten speech-token sequences (Défossez et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025c; Guo et al., 2026), but aggressive rate reduction may introduce compression–quality trade-offs, while tokenizer replacement may re-quire retraining or adapting the tokenizer, speech decoder, and SDM together. Moreover, this ap-proach still couples generation and synthesis to the same token space. Acoustic-piece and BPE methods have been explored in ASR (Ren et al., 2022; Chang et al., 2023, 2024), speech continua-tion (Shen et al., 2024), and TTS (Zhu et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), primarily through native training of small, task-specific models. In contrast, CoSTA enables post-hoc adaptation of existing large-scale SDMs for efficient compressed-token generation. We further uncover a consistent scaling regularity of speech-token compression and characterize the vocabulary-size trade-off. 

6 Conclusion 

We uncover an approximate log-linear scaling reg-ularity in speech-token BPE and introduce CoSTA, which decouples generation and synthesis token spaces while retaining the original tokenizer and decoder. CoSTA achieves approximately 1.7×

expanded speech-token generation speed and im-proves system efficiency while preserving response quality. It also supports more sustained interaction, reveals vocabulary-size trade-offs, and transfers across SDM architectures, complementing low-rate tokenization. 8Limitations 

This work has several limitations and possible ex-tensions. First, our dialogue-adaptation data is con-structed from synthesized speech-to-speech exam-ples. While such data provides controllable and consistent speech responses for studying speech-token compression, it is also a practical and widely adopted choice for training spoken dialogue mod-els, as large-scale, high-quality real speech-to-speech dialogue data is difficult to collect. Future work may further explore how synthetic and real conversational speech data complement each other. Second, we adopt BPE as a deterministic, lossless, and easily integrated compression method. Future work may investigate other lossless speech-token segmentation strategies and compare their behavior in spoken dialogue modeling. Finally, our evalua-tion focuses on general speech-to-speech dialogue and efficiency. Future work may extend the anal-ysis to finer-grained scenarios, such as emotional dialogue, personalized speaking styles, and diverse acoustic environments.

12 A Implementation Details and Hyperparameters 

This section reports the implementation details and hyperparameters used in CoSTA. Unless oth-erwise specified, all models are initialized from Step-Audio 2 mini (Wu et al., 2025). The origi-nal speech tokenizer and speech decoder are kept unchanged throughout all experiments. The origi-nal speech-token vocabulary contains 6,656 tokens. We train the BPE tokenizer using the HuggingFace 

tokenizers library 2 on speech-token sequences extracted from the general speech-to-speech dia-logue corpus, and extend the original vocabulary with 80K additional BPE speech tokens. Since the BPE trainer does not directly operate on integer-valued speech-token sequences, we map each orig-inal speech-token ID to a unique Unicode charac-ter before BPE training. During BPE expansion, generated BPE-token IDs are first converted into their corresponding Unicode strings, and BPE ex-pansion is performed in the Unicode space; the resulting BPE-expanded Unicode sequence is then mapped back to the corresponding original speech-token IDs. This conversion is deterministic and does not change the underlying speech-token se-quence. All adaptation and inference experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs. For all adaptation stages, we use the AdamW opti-mizer (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019), train with bfloat16 mixed precision (Micikevicius et al., 2018; Kalamkar et al., 2019), and use DeepSpeed (Rasley et al., 2020) with ZeRO-1 optimizer-state parti-tioning (Rajbhandari et al., 2020) for distributed training. 

A.1 Pronunciation Adaptation 

Pronunciation adaptation is conducted on com-pressed general TTS data. Following the progres-sive adaptation strategy, we split the pronunciation-adaptation data into two halves. In the first stage, only the embedding and LM head layers of the vocabulary-extended model are updated. We use a relatively large learning rate of 1.6 × 10 −4 in this stage, which leads to faster loss reduction and more stable optimization as shown in Figures 6 and 8. In the second stage, intermediate model lay-ers are further unfrozen and trained with a smaller learning rate of 2.0 × 10 −5 to complete pronuncia-tion adaptation. We evaluate models on URO-Bench (Yan et al., 2025), a comprehensive benchmark for end-to-end spoken dialogue models in speech-to-speech sce-narios. URO-Bench covers three major evalua-tion dimensions: Understanding, Reasoning, and Oral Conversation, and assesses not only response-content quality but also speech quality and speech-text alignment. In our experiments, we use 12 aligned Chinese and English datasets from URO-Bench, as summarized in Table 11. These bench-marks better reflect realistic spoken dialogue sce-narios, making them well aligned with our goal of improving the efficiency and usability of speech-to-speech SDMs. Following the URO-Bench evaluation proto-col, we evaluate output quality using its prompts and scoring procedures. Specifically, we re-port the following metrics: (1) G-Eval measures response-content quality by transcribing the gen-erated speech with an ASR model and prompting GPT-4o mini (OpenAI, 2024) to score the tran-scription. We use Whisper-large-v3 (Radford et al., 2023) for English transcription and Paraformer-zh (Gao et al., 2022) for Chinese transcription. (2) UT-MOS (Saeki et al., 2022) estimates speech quality with a predicted mean opinion score that reflects clarity, naturalness, and fluency. (3) WER/CER 

evaluates the alignment between the speech and text outputs of SDMs by computing the Word Er-ror Rate (WER) or Character Error Rate (CER) between the generated text and the transcription of the generated speech. URO-Bench uses different evaluation prompts according to the task format. For open-ended tasks, including AlpacaEval-zh, AlpacaEval, Claude-zh, CommonEval, Wildchat-zh, and WildchatEval, we use prompt_open . For semi-open tasks with ref-erence answers, including LCSTS-zh and Sum-mary, we use prompt_semi_open . For question-answering tasks with deterministic references, in-cluding APE-zh, Gsm8kEval, MLC-zh, and MLC, we use prompt_qa . These prompts are fed into GPT-4o mini together with the user instruction, the model response, and reference answer when avail-able, to obtain the final G-Eval score. C.10 Computational Cost of Vocabulary Extension Measurement setting. Vocabulary extension af-fects inference asymmetrically. The enlarged em-bedding table mainly increases parameter storage, while embedding lookup does not incur vocabulary-wide computation at each decoding step. In con-trast, the LM head projects each hidden state over the full vocabulary, and the subsequent token selec-tion also grows with vocabulary size. We therefore measure LM-head projection plus greedy argmax as the primary per-step computational overhead introduced by vocabulary extension. Transformer computation is excluded because its cost depends on the evolving prefix length, which itself changes with token compression and is therefore difficult to attribute to vocabulary extension alone. 26 Case Study 1: Short Factual QA Input: What breed of dog is the smallest?
