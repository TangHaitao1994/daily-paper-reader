Abstract 

We investigate how speech token representa-tions can support both understanding and gen-eration within a unified speech language model. We present PhonoVoxtLM, built on the exist-ing VoxtLM framework using phonological to-kens designed to retain linguistic content and prosodic information. The existing tokenizer is jointly trained for speech recognition and resynthesis. Its single-codebook units serve as both speech inputs and generation targets, with-out additional speech-token prediction mod-ules. Experiments show improvements over HuBERT-based baselines across understand-ing and generation under different training-data scales. Further comparisons with WavLM-based tokens reveal trade-offs between recogni-tion and synthesis, highlighting the importance of evaluating shared representations across both capabilities. We further extend the framework to spoken question answering through fine-tuning with replay. The adapted model im-proves answering performance over similarly adapted HuBERT baselines while retaining ad-vantages on the original speech tasks. Our findings support single-codebook representa-tions designed for both content and prosody as a practical choice for unified multitask speech modeling. 

1 Introduction 

Recent advances in text language models have es-tablished autoregressive modeling as an effective framework for diverse NLP tasks (Radford et al., 2019; Brown et al., 2020), motivating efforts to unify speech understanding and generation (Zhang et al., 2023; Maiti et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2025). Discrete speech tokens enable speech and text to be modeled within a shared architecture. When the same speech representation serves as both input and generation target, it must accommodate the in-formational demands of both capabilities (Mousavi et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2024). Existing systems address these demands through different representation and modeling choices. VALL-E uses acoustic codec tokens for synthe-sis (Wang et al., 2023), whereas GSLM mod-els content-oriented units for text-free speech generation (Lakhotia et al., 2021) (Figure 1(a)). Such representations emphasize different aspects of speech and may not serve understanding and generation equally well. Other systems introduce complementary mechanisms: Dual-SpeechLM uses AcousticGPT to predict acous-tic tokens from understanding-oriented represen-tations (Wang et al., 2026) (Figure 1(b)), while SpiRit-LM Expressive combines phonetic, pitch, and style tokens (Nguyen et al., 2025) (Figure 1(c)). These alternatives motivate our question: how ef-1arXiv:2610.18469v1 [eess.AS] 13 Oct 2026 fectively can a single-codebook representation sup-port both understanding and generation within a shared autoregressive framework? We study this question with PhonoVoxtLM (Fig-ure 1(d)), built on VoxtLM’s unified framework for ASR, TTS, and speech and text continuation (Maiti et al., 2024). We replace its HuBERT units with tokens produced by the existing Phonological To-kenizer (Onda et al., 2026), which combines ASR and speech resynthesis objectives through differ-entiable k-means to retain linguistic content and prosodic information. The resulting units serve as both speech inputs and generation targets, re-taining VoxtLM’s architecture without additional token prediction modules. Our comparisons ex-amine codebook size, speech encoder choice, and training-data scale to assess the benefits and trade-offs of this representation. We further extend the framework to spoken question answering (SQA), testing question-conditioned understanding beyond the original tasks. We adapt the model with replay and evaluate both SQA performance and original-task retention. To facilitate reproducibility, we im-plement our training and inference pipelines on ESPnet recipes (Watanabe et al., 2018). Our core contributions are as follows: • We systematically evaluate phonological to-kens as a shared input–output representation, finding improvements over 1K and 2K Hu-BERT baselines across understanding and gen-eration under two data settings. • We identify a trade-off relative to WavLM-large k-means units: phonological tokens im-prove synthesis, while the WavLM baselines achieve better recognition and speech discrim-ination. • We extend the shared interface to SQA and demonstrate gains over similarly adapted Hu-BERT baselines while retaining advantages on the original speech tasks. 

2 Related Work 

2.1 Speech Tokenization 

Speech tokenizers convert continuous speech into discrete units and are commonly categorized as acoustic or semantic (Borsos et al., 2023; Parker et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2024a). Acoustic tokeniz-ers emphasize signal reconstruction, preserving de-tailed acoustic information for generation (Zeghi-dour et al., 2021; Défossez et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2023a). However, these objectives do not necessar-ily yield representations well suited to speech un-derstanding (Shi et al., 2024b; Chang et al., 2024). In contrast, semantic tokenizers emphasize linguis-tic content, typically by discretizing self-supervised speech features (Hsu et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022) through clustering (Borsos et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; Shi et al., 2024a) or learned vector quantiza-tion (Huang et al., 2024). Another approach intro-duces quantization bottlenecks into ASR models (Du et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2024). Recent work explores representations that ac-commodate both linguistic and acoustic informa-tion, including multi-codebook designs (Zhang et al., 2024; Défossez et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025) and ReasoningCodec (Yang et al., 2026). The Phonological Tokenizer (Onda et al., 2026) combines ASR and speech resynthesis objectives through differentiable k-means and is designed to retain linguistic content and prosodic information in a single codebook. We adopt this existing phono-logical tokenizer and evaluate its units as a shared input–output representation within VoxtLM, focus-ing on their effects on both understanding and gen-eration. 

2.2 Speech Language Models 

Discrete-token speech language models formulate speech modeling as autoregressive sequence pre-diction (Borsos et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Kharitonov et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2023b). Sev-eral systems use representations with a particular informational emphasis in task-focused settings. VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) models acoustic codec tokens for text-to-speech synthesis, whereas GSLM (Lakhotia et al., 2021) models discrete units de-rived from self-supervised speech representations for text-free speech generation. These approaches demonstrate the effectiveness of discrete speech modeling, but their representations are not neces-sarily equally suited to the demands of both under-standing and generation. To accommodate these differing demands, other systems introduce specialized prediction components or combine complementary token types. DualSpeechLM (Wang et al., 2026) uses understanding-oriented tokens as language-model inputs and introduces AcousticGPT to predict acoustic tokens for generation. 3 Method 

We build on the unified autoregressive framework of VoxtLM (Maiti et al., 2024) and investigate the use of phonological tokens as a shared speech rep-resentation for model inputs and generation tar-gets. As illustrated in Figure 2, a frozen Phonolog-ical Tokenizer converts speech into discrete units, which are incorporated into a joint speech–text vocabulary through subword modeling. A single OPT-initialized decoder-only language model pre-dicts sequences over this vocabulary. We retain the original text language modeling, speech language modeling, automatic speech recognition (ASR), and text-to-speech synthesis (TTS) tasks, and ex-tend the framework to spoken question answering (SQA) using the same sequence interface. 

3.1 Shared Phonological Speech Representation 

Given a waveform X, a speech tokenizer Tϕ pro-duces a sequence of discrete units: 

D = Tϕ(X) = ( d1, . . . , d N ),di ∈ { 0, . . . , K − 1}. (1) We adopt the pretrained Phonological Tokenizer of Onda et al. (2026), which combines ASR and speech resynthesis objectives through differen-tiable k-means to encourage the retention of lin-guistic content and prosodic information. Our im-plementation uses its fine-tuned WavLM-large en-coder, extracting features from layer 21 and quan-tizing them with the released K = 2 ,000 cluster centroids. Both the encoder and centroids remain frozen during language-model training and SQA adaptation. To integrate speech units with text, we follow VoxtLM and map each discrete speech-unit in-dex di to a unique character in the CJK Uni-fied Ideographs block, with Unicode code point 

0x4E00 + di. This one-to-one mapping m is re-versible and allows speech units to be processed by SentencePiece alongside text. We then train a single SentencePiece unigram tokenizer U (Kudo and Richardson, 2018) on sequences containing mapped speech units and text from the ASR and TTS training data. This produces a joint subword vocabulary containing speech symbols and their 3subsequences, text subwords, and task-control to-kens. SentencePiece is trained before the lan-guage model and kept fixed thereafter. We de-note the resulting speech subword sequence by 

S = U (m(D)) and the tokenized text by T . The 2,000-entry speech codebook is distinct from the joint subword vocabulary: the former defines the shared discrete speech units, while the latter defines the prediction space of the language model. 

3.2 Unified Autoregressive Multitask Modeling 

We initialize the backbone of our decoder-only Transformer with pretrained OPT weights (Zhang et al., 2022), following VoxtLM, which reports im-proved performance with pretrained initialization compared to training from scratch (Maiti et al., 2024). All tasks share the same language-model parameters and joint output vocabulary. Accord-ingly, four control tokens specify the conditioning and output modalities. 3.3 Extension to Spoken Question Answering 

We formulate SQA as pθ(A | S, Q ) using the sequence format in Table 1, with supervision re-stricted to the answer and end-of-sequence to-kens. LibriSQA Parts I and II share this answer-generation objective: multiple-choice examples use the correct answer text as the target and omit can-didate options from the input. We fine-tune the four-task checkpoint on SQA mixed with replay examples from ASR, TTS, TextLM, and SpeechLM, retaining their original sequence formats. Only the shared language model is updated; the speech tokenizer and SentencePiece model remain fixed. No new task-control tokens or prediction modules are introduced. 

4 Experimental Setup 

4.1 Experimental Design 

We organize our experiments around an OPT-350M model trained on the balanced mixture DBal . We examine data scaling by increasing unpaired speech and text data to obtain D3M , while keeping the backbone and paired training data fixed. We also extend the balanced-data model to SQA through fine-tuning with replay of the original tasks, evalu-ating both SQA performance and retention of the original capabilities. 

4.2 Data 

We use LibriLight (Kahn et al., 2020) for speech-only modeling, the LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) language-modeling text corpus for text-only modeling, the 960-hour LibriSpeech training set for ASR, and the LibriTTS (Zen et al., 2019) and VCTK (Yamagishi et al., 2019) training data for TTS. 4.3 Models and Training 

We compare our phonological-token models with baselines using k-means units extracted from the sixth layer of HuBERT-base. Our primary baseline follows the 1,000-unit codebook setting evaluated in VoxtLM. Since the Phonological Tokenizer uses a 2,000-unit codebook, we additionally include 2,000-unit HuBERT baselines to assess whether the observed gains can be explained by the larger codebook alone. Both HuBERT baselines are evalu-ated under the balanced and expanded-data settings and after SQA adaptation. Corresponding systems use the same backbone size and task-data config-uration. Our comparisons evaluate the complete speech tokenization pipelines, which differ in their encoders and training objectives. Furthermore, we include WavLM-large k-means baselines with 1,000 and 2,000 units, extracting features from the 21st layer, as in the Phonologi-cal Tokenizer. These experiment use OPT-350M and DBal with the same language-model training settings. All models use a 10K SentencePiece unigram vocabulary and an OPT-350M-initialized backbone. For the original four-task training, we use Adam with a peak learning rate of 3×10 −4, 2,500 warmup updates, and weight decay of 10 −6. Training uses mixed precision, gradient accumulation over 20 minibatches, gradient clipping at 1.0, and label smoothing of 0.1. Checkpoints are selected by development-set target-token accuracy. For SQA adaptation, we reduce the peak learn-ing rate to 10 −4 and use 300 warmup updates. We select the checkpoint with the highest development-set answer-token accuracy and apply early stopping based on development loss with a patience of four validation epochs. The speech tokenizer and Sen-tencePiece model remain fixed during adaptation. 

4.4 Evaluation Language modeling and recognition. We evalu-ate TextLM and SpeechLM using perplexity (PPL), and lexical and syntactic discrimination using the sWUGGY and sBLIMP development sets (Dunbar et al., 2022). The discrimination pairs are ranked by summed token log-likelihood, without length normalization. SpeechLM is evaluated on speech inputs, while TextLM uses the corresponding text. The discrimination scores measure the percentage of pairs for which the model prefers the valid word or grammatically acceptable sentence. For Part II, we report multiple-choice accuracy by scoring each candidate answer as a continua-tion of the speech-and-question prefix. Table 2 shows that phonological tokens improve both speech understanding and generation over the HuBERT baselines within the same VoxtLM framework. In the balanced-data setting, gains span speech discrimination, recognition, and all four TTS metrics. For example, ASR WER de-creases from 7.1%/17.8% with HuBERT-1K to 4.2%/8.6%, and F0 correlation increases from 0.167 to 0.282. This pattern supports the value of improving the shared speech representation without adding speech-token prediction modules, although it does not isolate the contribution of prosodic in-formation.

5.2 Data Scaling 

The advantages of phonological tokens over both HuBERT baselines persist under D3M (Table 2). Gains remain consistent across recognition, speech discrimination, and all four TTS metrics, suggest-ing that the benefits of the shared representation are not confined to the balanced-data setting. However, scaling unpaired data yields uneven gains across tasks. For the phonological model, text and speech discrimination improve, whereas recog-nition changes little and synthesis metrics show mixed effects. In particular, TTS CER increases from 1.61% to 2.26%, despite a small improvement in UTMOS. Thus, the representation retains its rela-tive advantages, but additional unpaired data do not uniformly improve understanding and generation. 

5.3 Understanding–Generation Trade-offs 

Table 3 reveals a trade-off between understanding and generation when phonological tokens are com-pared with WavLM-large k-means units extracted from the same layer. The WavLM baselines achieve higher speech discrimination scores and lower ASR WER, while phonological tokens perform better on all four TTS metrics. The synthesis advantage is most evident in predicted quality and reference pitch correlation. Phonological tokens achieve a UTMOS of 3.98 and F0 correlation of 0.282, compared with 3.62– 3.70 and 0.144–0.159 for the WavLM baselines. The CER of PhnonVoxtLM is close to WavLM-1K and lower than WavLM-2K. These results highlight why a shared representation should be evaluated across both capabilities: the units with stronger recognition and discrimination scores do not nec-essarily provide stronger synthesis. Phonological tokens offer a useful balance, improving synthesis while retaining competitive understanding perfor-mance. 

5.4 Spoken Question Answering 

SQA adaptation extends the shared token interface to question-conditioned answer generation without additional prediction modules, using the sequence format in Table 1. Table 4 shows improvements in both answer generation and selection; for the phonological model, Part II accuracy rises from 35.73% to 46.76%. Among similarly adapted models, phonological tokens achieve higher BLEU-4 and BERTScore F1 than HuBERT-1K, with nearly identical ROUGE-L F1, and exceed HuBERT-2K on all three generation metrics. Using the calibrated score in Equation 4, Part II accuracy is higher by 1.66 and 1.99 percent-age points, respectively. The adapted model also exceeds the transcript-as-answer baseline on all Part I metrics, producing answers better matched to the references than simply returning the tran-script. These results extend the evidence for the shared representation beyond recognition and dis-crimination to spoken question answering. 

5.5 Retention of the Original Tasks 

SQA adaptation with replay affects the original tasks unevenly (the first two blocks of Table 2). For the phonological model, recognition and speech discrimination decline, and TextLM PPL increases. For example, ASR WER rises from 4.2%/8.6% to 5.4%/9.6%. TTS CER improves, while UTMOS, SpeechBERTScore, and F0 correlation decrease slightly. 5.6 Comparison with Prior Work 

Table 5 places PhonoVoxtLM in the context of prior systems, although training data and evalu-ation protocols differ. Our 350M model trained on D3M achieves higher reported speech discrimi-nation scores and lower ASR WER than the 1.3B VoxtLM-k200 without additional MLS data. 6 Conclusion 

We investigated phonological tokens as a shared input–output representation within the existing VoxtLM framework. Across balanced and expanded-data settings, these tokens improve speech recognition, discrimination, and synthe-sis over HuBERT baselines. Comparisons with WavLM-based units reveal a trade-off: phono-logical tokens provide stronger synthesis, while WavLM units perform better in recognition and speech discrimination. After SQA adaptation, the phonological model also outperforms similarly adapted HuBERT baselines while retaining advan-tages on the original speech tasks. Together, these findings support single-codebook phonological to-kens as a practical shared representation for under-standing and generation, without additional speech-token prediction modules. 

Limitations 

Our experiments compare complete speech repre-sentation pipelines. The WavLM baselines match the encoder architecture and feature-extraction layer, but do not isolate individual tokenizer train-ing objectives. F0 correlation provides evidence about reference pitch matching, but does not estab-lish the causal contribution of prosodic informa-tion to downstream performance or assess prosody comprehensively. Our evaluation is also limited to English, primarily read speech, with spoken ques-tion answering assessed only on LibriSQA. Further studies are needed to examine generalization to other languages and conversational settings. Be-sides, our experiments use a single language-model size; generalization to larger backbones remains to be evaluated. Finally, speech synthesis is eval-uated using automatic metrics; human listening 8tests would provide complementary evidence on perceived naturalness and prosodic quality.
