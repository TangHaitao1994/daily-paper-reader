# JAVIS: JOINT AUDIO-VISUAL INTERACTION AND STREAMING FOR REAL-TIME CONVERSATIONAL AGENTS 

# Junseok Ahn 1, Jaehyun Park 1, Jeongsoo Choi 1, Joon Son Chung 1, Ji-Hoon Kim 21Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, South Korea 

2Chung-Ang University, South Korea 

ABSTRACT 

Human communication is inherently multimodal, conveying intent not only through linguistic and vocal cues but also through visual signals. However, existing conversational systems have mainly focused on text or speech, leaving out the visual feedback that is essential for natural interaction. In this paper, we propose JAVIS ,an end-to-end streaming framework for real-time audio-visual con-versational agents. Our key idea is to let the voice and the face speak from a single representation. On top of a shared LLM, a Joint Refined Head produces a unified audio-visual representation, which is fed into dedicated streaming branches for speech and face generation. This end-to-end design avoids intermediate bot-tlenecks, enabling real-time generation while keeping speech and face tightly synchronized. In addition, we present JAVIS-Syn , a 334-hour synthetic corpus designed to address the lack of expressive audio-visual dialogue data, covering emotionally rich multi-turn conversations and assistant-style exchanges. Experiments show that JAVIS achieves approximately 200 × lower initial latency than a strong baseline, while outperforming baselines in lip synchroniza-tion and facial expression appropriateness. 

Index Terms — Joint speech-motion generation, real-time con-versational agents, talking face video 

1. INTRODUCTION 

In our daily lives, we see the face of the person we are talking to and hear their voice. As such, human communication is inherently multimodal [1, 2, 3, 4]. Speakers convey intent not only through words and speech but also through facial expressions, head move-ments, and gaze. Conversational AI systems, however, have evolved largely along the linguistic and auditory axes. From text-based chat-bots built on large language models (LLMs) to recent spoken dia-logue models that directly understand and generate speech [5], the medium of interaction has remained confined to text or speech. The absence of a visual channel limits the expressiveness and naturalness of these systems, calling for audio-visual conversational agents that can respond with both voice and face in real time. A common workaround is to cascade a conversational model with a separate generator, either a text LLM followed by text-to-audio-visual generation [6, 7], or a spoken dialogue system followed by speech-driven talking face generation [8, 9]. However, such pipelines are inherently limited by their sequential structure, in which the intermediate modality acts as an information bottleneck. Text discards prosodic nuance, and synthesized speech conveys only acoustic cues, preventing the downstream face generator from accessing the dialogue context encoded in the upstream model.

Furthermore, the sequential dependency between modules causes latency to accumulate, and errors propagate across stages and de-grade audio-visual synchronization [10]. To date, only a few recent works [11, 12] have explored end-to-end audio-visual dialogue generation. X-Streamer [11] adopts a Thinker–Actor architecture, where a frozen speech LLM guides an Actor that generates interleaved text and speech tokens and syn-thesizes video latents via chunk-wise autoregressive diffusion. Ex-Omni-2D [12] instead conditions a diffusion-based video generator on an LLM-generated visual plan and speech units produced by a separate speech generator. However, both approaches rely on iter-ative generation of spatiotemporal video latents, which imposes a heavy computational burden on visual synthesis. Real-time stream-ing generation of realistic visual responses thus remains an open problem, made even more challenging by the scarcity of expressive audio-visual dialogue data. In this paper, we propose JAVIS, an end-to-end streaming frame-work for real-time audio-visual conversational agents. Given a spo-ken query, JAVIS responds with synchronized speech and facial video in a streaming manner, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Rather than animating the face after speech is synthesized, our framework jointly generates both modalities from a single representation. Specifically, a Joint Refined Head produces a unified audio-visual representation, which is fed into two streaming branches for speech decoding and facial motion generation. By bypassing intermediate text or speech, this design preserves the conversational intent in both modalities and maintains tight audio-visual synchronization. Furthermore, to address the scarcity of audio-visual dialogue data, we construct 

JAVIS-Syn , a 334-hour synthetic corpus comprising emotionally rich multi-turn conversations and assistant-style exchanges. Exper-iments demonstrate that JAVIS generates high-quality audio-visual responses in real time, outperforming existing works in audio-visual synchronization. Notably, our model reduces initial latency by ap-proximately 200 × compared with a strong baseline while maintain-ing comparable visual quality. Human evaluation further confirms that JAVIS produces natural audio-visual responses with accurate lip synchronization and situation-appropriate facial expressions. arXiv:2610.22499v1 [cs.CV] 15 Oct 2026 2. METHOD 

Given a spoken query, the dialogue history, and a reference portrait, JAVIS generates a spoken response along with a synchronized fa-cial video of the reference identity, as depicted in Fig. 2. JAVIS is built upon the pre-trained Fun-Audio-Chat [5], which pairs a 5 Hz shared LLM with a 25 Hz speech refined head for efficient stream-ing generation. Rather than conditioning facial motion on the gen-erated speech waveform, we repurpose the speech refined head into the Joint Refined Head (JRH) via LoRA [13], so that the JRH hidden states serve as a unified audio-visual representation while preserving the pre-trained speech generation capability. The unified representa-tion is fed into two parallel branches: a speech branch that synthe-sizes the spoken response, and a motion branch where a Motion De-coder autoregressively generates chunks of continuous facial motion, which are subsequently rendered by the Motion Renderer [9] with the reference portrait. As verified in Sec. 3, this end-to-end architec-ture enables real-time streaming generation of situation-appropriate responses with tight audio-visual synchronization. 

2.1. Joint Refined Head 

The JRH repurposes the speech refined head of Fun-Audio-Chat [5] to produce a unified audio-visual representation for both speech to-ken and facial motion generation. Since the shared LLM and the JRH operate at 5 Hz and 25 Hz, respectively, the t-th shared LLM output is ungrouped into five conditioning vectors. The JRH then processes the five conditioning vectors autoregressively, each com-bined with the embedding of the preceding speech token. At each autoregressive step, the next speech token is sampled from a linear projection of the final-layer hidden state. The resulting five hidden states are stacked into Ht ∈ R5×d, where d denotes the JRH hid-den dimension, and serve as the unified representation for the t-th chunk. The Motion Decoder takes Ht directly as its condition, by-passing waveform decoding and re-encoding for motion generation. Throughout response generation, the JRH attends to the shared LLM outputs and dialogue history, allowing Ht to encode the di-alogue context beyond mere speech content. To exploit this con-text while preserving the pre-trained speech generation capability, we freeze the shared LLM and jointly train the LoRA-adapted JRH and the Motion Decoder. The JRH is thus supervised by both the cross-entropy loss Lspeech for speech token prediction and the mo-tion generation loss Lmotion (Sec. 2.2), encouraging Ht to capture facial dynamics that are synchronized with speech. 

2.2. Streaming Audio-Visual Generation Speech branch. The speech branch adopts the pre-trained speech decoder and vocoder of CosyVoice 3 [14], which are kept frozen throughout training. The flow-matching speech decoder converts the sampled speech tokens into mel-spectrograms, which the vocoder then synthesizes into waveforms. The decoder’s chunk-aware causal design enables incremental decoding, so the spoken response can be streamed while subsequent speech tokens are still being generated. 

Motion branch. The motion branch consists of the Motion De-coder, which autoregressively generates facial motion chunk by chunk from the unified representation, and the Motion Renderer [9], which renders the generated motion with the reference portrait into video frames. We represent the facial motion of each frame as 20-dimensional coefficients over the orthonormal motion basis of FLOAT [9]. To address this, we construct JAVIS-Syn, a 334-hour synthetic audio-visual dialogue dataset that provides expressive facial motion and assistant-style interactions. We utilize three dialogue sources to construct JAVIS-Syn. Dai-lyTalk [19] and ShareChatX-Emotion [20] provide multi-turn con-versations with utterance-level emotion labels, while VoiceAssistant-400K [21] 1 provides question–answer pairs reflecting how an AI assistant responds to user requests. For DailyTalk and ShareChatX-Emotion, we render every utterance in each dialogue using its emotion label, whereas for VoiceAssistant-400K, we retain the user query as audio and render only the assistant response with a neu-tral emotion prompt. The corresponding videos are generated by InfiniteTalk [8], an audio-driven talking face model whose facial ex-pressions can be controlled through an emotion prompt. Reference portraits are drawn from a pool of 1,220 frontal, neutral-expression portraits generated with Gemini 3.1 Flash Image [23], covering di-verse genders and ages. Each speaker is assigned a portrait matching their gender and age group, obtained from the speaker metadata, and the same portrait is used for all utterances of that speaker within a dialogue. The generated videos are filtered by lip-sync quality us-ing SyncNet [24], yielding 225,780 utterance videos totaling 334.13 hours, with the resulting dataset composition summarized in Table 2. 3. EXPERIMENTS 3.1. Experimental Setup Training. JAVIS is trained in two stages. In the first stage, we train the model in a monologue setting using 940,497 single-utterance clips from the Talker-T2AV [6] dataset. The model is prompted to repeat the input speech, and the same speech together with its facial motion serves as the training target. In the second stage, we adapt the model to dialogue response generation using 716,565 samples from MultiDialog [17], JAVIS-Syn, and a subset of Seamless Inter-action [18]. Each sample contains up to three preceding turns as context, and the loss is applied only to the final assistant response. 

Implementation details. In both stages, only the LoRA adapter of the JRH and the Motion Decoder are trained, while the remaining modules are frozen. The Motion Decoder is an eight-layer Trans-former with a hidden dimension of 1,024, and LoRA is applied with rank 16 and α = 32 . Both stages use AdamW with a learning rate of 

10 −4, a cosine schedule, and a batch size of 8. At inference, motion is sampled with 10 Euler steps and a noise temperature of 0.7. 

Evaluation. JAVIS is compared with an end-to-end system, Ex-Omni-2D 2, and three cascaded systems that pair Fun-Audio-Chat (FAC) with FLOAT [9], InfiniteTalk [8] 3, or Talker-T2AV [6]. Since FAC produces both a text and a speech response, FLOAT and In-finiteTalk animate its speech output (s2s), whereas Talker-T2AV generates both speech and video from its text output (s2t). All mod-els are evaluated on 140 samples from VStyle [25], which is unseen during training. 

Metrics. For video, we measure image quality (IQ), subject con-sistency (SC), and motion smoothness (MS) using VBench [26]. Lip synchronization is measured with LSE-C and LSE-D [24]. For speech, we measure speech naturalness (UTMOS [27]), speaker sim-ilarity (SpkSIM [28]), and intelligibility (WER [29]) of the gener-ated speech. Generation efficiency is evaluated by the initial latency, defined as the delay until the first audio and video outputs are both available, and the real-time factor (RTF), computed as the ratio of total generation time to the duration of the generated response.

3.3. Subjective Evaluation 

We conduct a human evaluation to assess the perceptual quality of the generated responses, including whether the facial expressions are appropriate to the user’s situation. We sample 30 queries from VStyle with a balanced distribution across emotion categories. A to-tal of 26 raters listen to the user’s speech and rate the response videos on a five-point scale in terms of audio-visual synchronization, mo-tion naturalness, and facial expression appropriateness. The mean scores are reported in Table 4 with 95% confidence intervals. As shown, JAVIS achieves the highest audio-visual syn-chronization score of 4.37, consistent with the objective evaluation results in Sec. 3.2. Its motion naturalness score of 3.92 is comparable to the best score of 4.02 obtained by FAC + InfiniteTalk, with over-lapping confidence intervals. Most importantly, JAVIS achieves the best facial expression appropriateness score of 3.76, clearly outper-forming all baselines. This suggests the effectiveness of our end-to-end architecture, in which the motion branch directly attends to the hidden states that encode the user’s query context, whereas cascaded systems generate faces solely from the response speech or text.

3.4. Ablation Study 

To validate the effect of each component, we report an ablation study in Table 5. First, removing the lookahead representation Ht+1 de-grades lip synchronization (LSE-C 9.59 →8.80, LSE-D 6.74 →7.55), indicating that access to upcoming speech context is important for generating accurate lip movements. Removing JAVIS-Syn from di-alogue adaptation also degrades lip synchronization (LSE-C 6.71, LSE-D 8.72) as well as speech quality (WER 1.21%, UTMOS 4.03). We attribute the lip-synchronization gains brought by JAVIS-Syn to its clean frontal faces and clearly visible lip movements, which pro-vide more reliable supervision than existing dialogue videos that of-ten contain low-quality frames with occlusion or blur. Finally, the model trained only in the monologue stage shows degraded perfor-mance across all metrics, with particularly severe drops in speech intelligibility (WER 13.91%) and lip synchronization (LSE-C 6.17, LSE-D 8.79), highlighting the importance of dialogue adaptation for audio-visual response generation. 

4. CONCLUSION 

We presented JAVIS, an end-to-end streaming framework for real-time audio-visual conversational agents. Rather than animating a face after speech is synthesized, our framework generates speech and facial motion jointly from a single representation, allowing both modalities to be streamed together while sharing the dialogue con-text. To address the scarcity of expressive audio-visual dialogue data, we further constructed JAVIS-Syn, a 334-hour synthetic corpus of emotionally rich multi-turn conversations and assistant-style in-teractions. Extensive experiments demonstrate that JAVIS achieves strong audio-visual synchronization and facial expression appropri-ateness, while reducing initial latency by approximately 200 × over a strong cascaded baseline and preserving the speech quality. Fu-ture work includes extending JAVIS to full-duplex interaction with listening behavior. We believe JAVIS offers a practical step toward conversational agents that engage with users through both voice and face in real time. 5. REFERENCES
