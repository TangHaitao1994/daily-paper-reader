# VoxTurn: Answering Several Turn-Taking Questions in One Pass with One Model 

An open 0.8B speech LLM for end-of-turn detection and related voice-agent decisions 

Yang Xiao 

> The University of Melbourne

ò Model & code  Interactive demo 

Abstract 

A cascaded voice agent must decide, throughout each silence of its user, whether the user has finished their turn: answering too early cuts the user off, answering too late makes the agent slow. It often needs related decisions too: will the user soon go on, is speech during its own turn an interruption or a backchannel, is a sound speech at all. With open detectors, each extra decision costs another model or another call, and on the 14-language eot-bench they cut users off in more than a quarter of mid-turn pauses. Because they are trained on segmented utterances, this mismatch is the largest single source of error. We present VoxTurn, a 0.8B speech LLM that casts these decisions as multiple-choice questions, scores the hidden state of each option, and answers any set of them in one forward pass with a block-isolating attention mask, exactly as if asked one at a time. Trained on real conversational pauses with labels derived from their timing, and re-queried while a silence lasts, VoxTurn reaches 11.5% false cut-offs in English at a 300 ms latency budget, comparable to two commercial detectors, and a 14-language mean of 19.9%, the lowest among open models, at 8.3 ms per decision. In Pipecat and LiveKit it cuts off 17% and 9% of turns, and it ranks fifth of 24 in TurnBench end-of-turn detection without any TurnBench training data. The model, code and framework plugins are released.

## 1 Introduction 

Turn-taking is the timing backbone of spoken dialogue [3]. A cascaded voice agent listens while the user speaks and, whenever the user falls silent, must decide whether to answer: answering too early cuts the user off mid-thought, answering too late makes the conversation sluggish. A voice activity detector (VAD) [4] with a fixed silence timeout can only trade one error for the other, so recent systems add an end-of-turn (EOT) model that judges what was said and how. These range from text-only language models over the transcript [5, 6] and small audio classifiers [7] to larger audio models in commercial products [6, 8, 9] and in the open [10]. EOT-Bench [1] now compares them on real user turns in 14 languages, and TurnBench [2] on two-party conversations. Although many works address turn-taking, open detectors leave three problems.

Widely used training sets consist of segmented utterances, many of them synthetic [7, 11], each labelled once at its end, while a deployed detector is asked at varying times inside real pauses, where the answer depends on how long the silence has lasted. 

2. A deployed agent needs more than one decision : whether a pause will end soon, whether speech during its own turn is an interruption or a backchannel, and whether a sound is speech at all. Each open detector answers one of these per call, so every extra answer costs another model or another call. 

3. Frameworks query the detector once , when the VAD reports silence, and fall back to a timeout if the answer is not yet confident, although a good model grows more certain as the silence goes on. VoxTurn addresses each problem. For the first, we train on real conversational pauses from phone calls and face-to-face dialogue, draw each decision time from anywhere in the pause, and derive labels from the pause timing. For the second, we write each decision as a multiple-choice question for a speech LLM built on Qwen3-ASR-0.6B [12], score the hidden state of each option instead of generating text, and answer any set of questions in one forward pass with an attention mask that isolates question blocks after a shared audio prefix, with exactly the result of asking them one by one. The same pass lets us supervise every training sample with all labels its timing determines. For the third, we re-query VoxTurn every 0.25 s while the silence lasts.           

> Key results
> ▶Real pauses matter most. Real conversational pauses, with decision times drawn throughout each pause and labels derived from its timing, bring most of the gain on eot-bench: English false cut-offs fall from 26.2 to 16.5% and the 14-language mean from 33.0 to 20.0%, more than a speech-aligned backbone, multi-question training or four times more training (§6.3).
> ▶One pass, five decisions. A one-pass multi-question readout, exactly equivalent to asking the questions one by one, lets one 0.8B model answer five turn-taking decisions in 8.3 ms per decision, and four questions in 9.0 ms instead of 33.2 ms (§3.3, §6.6).
> ▶Best open model on eot-bench. 11.5% English false cut-offs at 300 ms, comparable to two commercial detectors, and a 19.9% 14-language mean, the lowest among open models evaluated in all 14 languages (§6.1, §6.2).
> ▶Works in real frameworks. Re-queried during each silence, VoxTurn cuts off 17% and 9% of turns in Pipecat and LiveKit instead of 45% and 18%, and answers sooner (§6.6).
> ▶Transfers to two-party conversation. Without TurnBench training, it ranks fifth of 24 in end-of-turn and sixth of 23 in interruption detection on the TurnBench test leaderboard (§6.5).

## 2 Related work  

> Turn-taking models.

Voice Activity Projection [13] predicts the future voice activity of both speakers from dialogue audio, and full-duplex speech models such as Moshi [14] make turn-taking implicit. Cascaded agents instead use a dedicated detector on the user channel: TurnGPT [5] and the LiveKit text detector [6] score the transcript, Smart Turn [7] classifies the last seconds of audio, and turn-1 [10] combines speech-model features with the agent’s previous utterance. Easy Turn [11] and TEN [15] extend the binary decision to dialogue states such as backchannel and wait, and the SemanticVAD data [16] label dialogue text as complete, incomplete, interruption or backchannel. VoxTurn covers these decisions with one model and one pass.

LLMs as classifiers. Scoring answer options instead of generating free text is a standard way to use language models for multiple-choice questions [17]. VoxTurn applies it to speech, reading a learnt linear head on the hidden state of each option, and adds a mask that lets several questions share one encoding of the audio. 

Benchmarks. eot-bench [1] scores detectors on real single-speaker user turns in 14 languages, asking for a decision repeatedly as each silence unfolds. TurnBench [2] scores end-of-turn and interruption events in two-party conversations with one channel per speaker. We report both, together with the test sets of Easy Turn [11] and TEN [15] and a nonverbal-vocalisation set [18] (Section 5). 

## 3 VoxTurn 

3.1 Turn-taking decisions as multiple-choice questions 

At a decision time t, VoxTurn sees the 8 s of user audio ending at t (left-padded with silence), optionally a transcript or the partner’s last words, and a marker of who is speaking (user or agent). It answers five questions, each with a fixed option set (Table 1). Every question has 12 phrasings in English and in Chinese; training samples one, inference uses a fixed one. 

3.2 Model and readout 

The backbone is Qwen3-ASR-0.6B [12]: its AuT audio encoder (frozen) turns 128-bin log-mel fea-tures into 13 tokens per second (104 for 8 s), a projector maps them into Qwen3-0.6B [19], and we add LoRA [20] (rank 16, α = 32) to every linear layer of the language model. The input sequence is [chat header ][ audio ][ text ][ speaker marker ] followed by the questions, each written as “Question: . . . Options: . . . ”. Nothing is generated: a linear head w scores the final hidden state hq,o of each option o of question q, and

3.3 One-pass multi-question inference 

Let the prefix occupy positions 1 .. P and question block k occupy a contiguous span Bk after it (Figure 2b). We use the attention mask 

Mi j = 1[ j ≤ i ] · 1[ j ≤ P ∨ blk (i) = blk ( j) ] , (2) and give the m-th token of every block the position id P + m − 1, the id it would have if its block were the only question. Prefix tokens never attend to a block, so their states do not depend on the questions; by induction over layers, every token of Bk then attends to exactly the keys, at exactly the positions, that it would see in the sequence [prefix; Bk]. With a full-attention backbone, one pass therefore returns the same probabilities as K separate passes (we measure a maximum difference of 1 .5 × 10 −5 in fp32), with no dependence on question order. The prefix ( ≈120 tokens) is encoded once and each extra question adds about 15 tokens. The argument does not hold for recurrent or linear-attention layers, whose state cannot be masked per block, which is one reason we use a full-attention backbone. 

3.4 Training objective 

Each training sample has a main question from its source (Table 2). With the one-pass readout we add, at almost no cost, every auxiliary question whose label is exact: all real speech is labelled not invalid , and every pause sample gets a resume label from its timing. For a decision time o into a pause of length d, a hold pause with remaining silence d − o ≤ 0.5 s is within 0.5 s , a longer one is later , and an end-of-turn pause is not in this turn . We also ask the main question a second time with another phrasing in the same pass. The loss is

## 4 Training data and recipe  

> Data.

Table 2 lists the mixture. Smart Turn and Easy Turn provide segmented utterances, much of them synthetic. The pause sources are real conversations: for SpokenWOZ (8 kHz task-oriented phone calls, user channel) and Seamless Interaction (face-to-face, each speaker’s own track) we run Silero VAD [4] on both channels and label every silence of at least 100 ms between two segments of a speaker as end of turn if the other speaker takes the floor ( ≥1 s of speech) and as hold if nobody speaks before the speaker resumes within 5 s; overlaps and backchannels are skipped. RAMC pauses come from its time-aligned transcripts. For every pause the decision time is drawn uniformly from the first min (d, 1.5 s ) of the silence, so the model learns how the answer changes as a pause unfolds. Seamless audio is band-limited to 8 kHz half of the time so that bandwidth does not reveal the source. Easy Turn samples are audio only, audio with transcript or transcript only (6:3:1); invalid negatives are real speech from the other sources; labels within a source are rebalanced by nα

> Training.

We train on 300k samples with batch 128 (4 micro-batches of 32), AdamW (lr 2 × 10 −4,

β = ( 0.9, 0.98 ), weight decay 0.01), 100 warm-up steps and cosine decay to 10%, gradient clipping at 1.0 and bf16 autocast (Appendix B). One run takes 78 minutes on one NVIDIA L40S. ## 5 Evaluation suite 

Table 3 summarises what each benchmark measures. The headline benchmark is eot-bench, whose protocol is worth spelling out because it differs from utterance-level classification.  

> The eot-bench protocol.

eot-bench [1] contains user turns in 14 languages with their silences labelled 

hold (the user goes on) or end of turn . A model is scored every 100 ms into each silence (minimum silence 100 ms), and the eot-harness sweeps decision policies (threshold, action delay, timeout). A false cut-off is a hold silence in which the policy takes the turn; the latency is the mean time from the start of an end-of-turn silence to the response. For each detector we report the lowest false cut-off rate at a mean response latency of 300 ms and 600 ms, the lowest mean latency at 5% and 10% cut-offs, and the AUC between hold and end-of-turn silences 200 ms into the pause. Every number is therefore a point on a trade-off curve (Figure 3a), and lower is better for both cut-offs and latency.

6.3 What drives the gains 

Table 5 and Figure 5 trace the development.  

> Data first.

With segmented utterances (r0), AUC stayed below 0.90 whatever we tuned. Drawing RAMC decision times from anywhere in a pause (r1) helped Chinese most ( −9.4 points), and adding real English pauses from task-oriented phone calls (r2) gave the largest single English gain: 26.2 → 16.3% false cut-offs and AUC 0.896 → 0.944. Face-to-face pauses (r3) added little in English but improved Chinese and latency. The pause data, not the amount of training, is what matters: four times more updates gain only 0.7 points, and neither a larger readout head nor an explicit pitch branch helps.  

> Then the backbone.

The second large step is the backbone. Replacing Whisper-small and a lightly trained adapter with Qwen3-ASR-0.6B, whose encoder and language model are already aligned on large-scale speech, lowers English cut-offs from 16.6 to 12.8% and the Chinese calibration error of p(eot )

on eot-bench from 0.30 to 0.11.  

> Then multi-question training.

Multi-question training improves English at every latency budget between 250 and 500 ms by 0.4–1.8 points (11.5%, AUC 0.963) while adding the resume, invalid and consistency supervision; the English p(eot ) is well calibrated (ECE 0.007). In Chinese it costs 1.0 point at 300 ms and the 14-language mean is nearly unchanged (19.7 vs. 19.9%). 

6.4 Other decisions from the same pass 

The same model and pass also answer the other questions (Figure 6). 6.6 Efficiency and use in voice-agent frameworks 

Latency. One decision takes 8.3 ms in serving mode (Table 7, 1.9 GB of GPU memory), and four questions in one pass take 9.0 ms instead of 33.2 ms, a 3.7 × saving whose answers differ from separate passes only by bf16 rounding. Serving mode keeps the eot-bench result (11.6% vs. 11.5% at 300 ms).

## 7 Conclusion 

VoxTurn answers several turn-taking questions with a speech LLM in one forward pass. Real conver-sational pauses, a speech-aligned backbone and supervision of every label a pause’s timing determines bring an open 0.8B model to 11.5% English false cut-offs at 300 ms and the best 14-language mean among open models, at 8.3 ms per decision, and to fifth place in TurnBench end-of-turn detection.
