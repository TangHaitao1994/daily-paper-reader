# Abstract 

Real-world voice separation must recover individual voices from overlapping speech, environmental noise, and music, yet isolated source recordings are rarely available for supervision. We introduce Soloist, a generative model for blind voice separation that autoregressively generates individual voice streams as audio-codec sequences conditioned on the mixture, without requiring a target-speaker query or a predefined voice count. We propose a two-stage training paradigm to learn from synthetic mixtures and adapt to real recordings. The first stage combines supervised separation on synthetic mixtures with isolated source targets and an auxiliary task that predicts speaker timelines from real recordings, learning who speaks when from natural acoustics. The second stage applies reinforce-ment learning to real recordings, with rewards supplied by a model judge that compares candidate separations based on voice purity and content preservation without isolated source targets. Across diverse scenarios, Soloist recovers clean speech from multi-speaker overlap and noisy recordings, and extracts singing vocals from music accompaniment while maintaining relatively high pitch accu-racy and intelligibility. On real-world recordings with unknown voice counts, Soloist also separates voices well. Finally, we integrate Soloist into a full-duplex conversation processing pipeline that as-sembles separated outputs into continuous speaker tracks, demonstrating its practical utility in real conversations. 

# 1 Introduction 

People speak over one another in conversations, films, and online recordings. Recovering their individual voices requires a separator to identify how many voices are present, retain each voice’s content and identity, and exclude unrelated sound. Training references are less complete: a natural recording often comes with a transcript or speaker timeline, but rarely with the isolated waveforms that produced the mixture. Supervised separators learn this mapping from mixtures constructed with known sources [1, 2]. Their targets are precise, while the acoustics and source combinations available for construction constrain the training distribution. Learning from unpaired mixtures has also been studied: mixture invariant training (MixIT) uses mixture-level reconstruction without isolated sources [3], including adaptation to real meetings [4]. Our focus is a complementary setting in which a generative separator can use several incomplete references 1arXiv:2610.19716v1 [cs.SD] 14 Oct 2026 and can be updated on natural audio through model-generated feedback. Section 2 positions this approach relative to prior work. We introduce Soloist, a generative model for blind voice separation in real recordings. Given a single-channel mixture, the model generates one audio-codec sequence per voice without requiring a target-speaker query or a supplied voice count. Each sequence is decoded into an individual voice waveform. Our training recipe follows the references available for each recording (figure 1). Constructed mixtures pro-vide source targets; a fixed music separator provides pseudo-vocal targets for songs; and speaker timelines provide text targets for natural recordings without isolated waveforms. The judge must distinguish useful separation from fluent but incorrect resynthesis. Signal checks detect degenerate outputs and likely copies. The input mixture is included as an anonymous baseline, allowing the judge to compare each separation with returning the input unchanged. To check whether candidate order affects its preferences, the judge ranks the same candidates twice, first in a shuffled order and then in the reverse order. Groups with inconsistent rankings, or reports that cannot distinguish candidate quality, are excluded from RL updates. The remaining groups provide relative rewards that favor purer individual voices and more complete coverage of the audible content. This report makes three contributions. First, we formulate blind voice separation as serial codec-sequence generation with an explicit voice count, and show that predicting speaker timelines on real recordings without isolated sources improves count inference. Second, we propose a model-judge reward for RL on real recordings without isolated sources: tracks are described independently, candidates are ranked in text against the unprocessed mixture as an anonymous anchor, and groups whose preferences change substan-tially when the candidate order is reversed are discarded. Third, we evaluate the model on synthetic, noisy, 2singing, and real multi-speaker recordings, and show how it is used in a full-duplex conversation processing pipeline. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Supervised Separation and Variable Source Counts 

Paired-source separation learns to recover isolated waveforms from constructed mixtures. Conv-TasNet op-erates in a learned time-domain representation [1]; SepFormer and MossFormer develop attention-based sequence models [2, 5]; and TF-GridNet combines full-band and sub-band modeling in the time-frequency domain [6]. These approaches establish strong reconstruction baselines, while the choice of training mixtures determines the acoustic conditions and source combinations available to supervision. LibriMix provides a reproducible construction for studying speech separation and generalization [7]. Output assignment and source count are distinct problems. Permutation-invariant training handles the arbitrary ordering of reference sources [8, 9], whereas recursive separation extracts speakers successively to accommodate unknown counts [10]. SR-CorrNet instead adapts the number of output streams with an attractor-based split module [11]. Soloist represents count, stream boundaries, and voice audio in one serial response. Its supplied-count and blind evaluations therefore probe different capabilities: recovering voices under a known count and deciding how many streams to produce (section 6). 

2.2 Neural Audio Codecs and Generative Separation 

SoundStream and EnCodec learn compressed audio representations that can be decoded back into wave-forms [12, 13]. AudioLM shows how discrete audio representations support language-model-style gener-ation [14]. This representation makes separation amenable to conditional sequence prediction, alongside tasks such as synthesis and recognition. TokenSplit is a direct precedent: it jointly handles speech separa-tion, transcription, and speech generation through discrete representations, with both direct separation and refinement of a conventional separator’s outputs, and with or without transcript conditioning [15]. Soloist uses codec sequences as its separation output, conditioned on continuous audio features and discrete mixture codes (section 3.2). Its backbone predicts the response structure and conditions a local decoder for each frame’s codec codes. The relevant distinction is the combination of variable voice-stream generation, speaker-timeline supervision, and adaptation on real mixtures through candidate feedback. Generating plausible audio and preserving the particular voices and words in a mixture remain separate requirements, motivating the content and identity checks used in our evaluation and application. 

2.3 Prompted Extraction and Singing-Voice Separation 

Target extraction specifies which sound to recover through an external cue. AudioSep uses natural-language descriptions [16]; SoloAudio performs generative extraction with a diffusion Transformer and supports audio and language conditioning [17]; and SAM Audio supports text, visual, and temporal prompts [18]. These interfaces allow a user to select a sound of interest. Blind voice separation instead requires enumerating the audible voices without a target query. Our comparisons make the supplied-count setting explicit because count information and target selection change what a system must infer. Music separation introduces sustained pitch, accompaniment, and potentially layered vocals. Mel-Band Ro-Former models overlapping mel-frequency bands for music source separation [19]. MIR-1K and MUSDB18-HQ provide established singing and music evaluation resources [20, 21]. We use a fixed music separator to produce song pseudo-vocals and train these as one vocal stream (section 4). This supervision can represent multiple singers in a combined vocal target, whereas speech examples teach individual voice streams. The two target definitions determine what each evaluation can establish. 32.4 Learning from Unpaired Recordings and Teacher Targets 

MixIT removes the need for isolated training sources by separating a mixture of mixtures and assigning estimated components back to the observed mixtures [3]. Its application to real meetings demonstrates adaptation to far-field, overlapping, reverberant speech without isolated in-domain references [4]. RemixIT follows a teacher–student approach: estimated speech and noise are remixed to create training pairs, and the teacher is updated from successive students [22]. These methods provide alternatives to relying entirely on constructed mixtures with known sources. Our supervision is organized by the reference available for each recording. Constructed mixtures provide source audio, songs provide fixed-teacher pseudo-vocals, and natural recordings provide speaker timelines. Subsequent RL compares candidate separations of the same real mixture using a model judge (section 5). The distinction concerns the training signal: mixture reconstruction, teacher-generated audio, speaker-activity labels, and relative quality feedback impose different constraints on the outputs. 

2.5 Speaker Activity, Diarization, and Identity 

Diarization estimates who speaks when, including overlapping activity. End-to-end neural diarization formulates this as multi-label prediction with a permutation-free objective and supports adaptation from speaker-segment labels [23]. Such labels describe temporal activity without specifying each speaker’s waveform. MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize supplies the speaker-attributed intervals used in our timeline super-vision [24]. We encode those intervals as a text response within the same model that generates separated audio, allowing natural recordings to contribute supervised examples without isolated targets. Activity labels and speaker identity serve different roles in long recordings. Speaker-verification systems such as CAM++ and ERes2Net learn representations for comparing voices [25, 26]. Our conversation pipeline uses speaker embeddings and consistency across pieces to associate local outputs with recording-level speak-ers, then combines activity and identity checks to detect missing or intruding speech (section 7). Agreement on identity alone does not establish that the original words have been preserved, which motivates the sepa-rate content checks. 

2.6 AI Feedback, Policy Optimization, and Judge Reliability 

RLHF uses human preferences to train language models beyond supervised demonstrations [27], while RLAIF studies preferences supplied by AI systems [28]. PPO supplies a clipped policy-gradient objective [29]; GRPO in DeepSeekMath and the optimization components of DAPO develop group-based feedback and practical training procedures for language-model RL [30, 31]. Our update uses relative rewards within a group of candidate separations, with an unnormalized group-centered advantage and signal-based shaping (section 5.5). Audio-specific feedback learning provides closer precedents. MOS-based RLHF fine-tunes speech enhancers using a learned perceptual-quality reward [32]. MARS-Sep optimizes a mask policy with a multimodal pref-erence reward for query-conditioned sound separation [33]. LLM-guided audio-visual enhancement con-verts audio-language descriptions into scalar ratings for PPO updates [34]. These studies connect feedback to audio processing through quality prediction, cross-modal alignment, or textual descriptions. Our setting combines blind generation of multiple voice streams with comparison of complete candidate separations of the same recording. The feedback must account for voice purity and coverage across all outputs, as well as each track’s quality. The quality of the feedback is a separate concern from the optimizer. LLM-as-a-judge studies identify presentation-order bias and other limitations in model judgments [35]. Reward-model overoptimization can increase a proxy score while degrading the quality it is intended to measure [36]. Our judge first describes clips independently, then compares their reports alongside an anonymous mixture anchor and filters incon-sistent rankings under shuffled and reversed candidate orders. The consistency check targets sensitivity to candidate order, and the signal checks target degenerate outputs. 42.7 Evaluating Content, Acoustics, Pitch, and Identity 

Waveform-based measures such as BSS Eval and SI-SDR quantify aspects of reconstruction fidelity relative to source signals [37, 38]. Recognition-based evaluation instead asks whether the separated speech retains recoverable words. Permutation-aware multi-speaker ASR and work on WER definitions provide the ba-sis for matching streams before counting transcription errors [39–41]. Our PI-WER protocol pads unequal stream sets with empty transcripts and evaluates the minimum-cost assignment; count accuracy separately assesses the number of voiced outputs. Speech representations such as WavLM are trained to support tasks involving both content and speaker information [42]. Our SingerSim metric uses WavLM features, while raw pitch accuracy measures pitch preservation (section 6.2). These quantities complement transcription recovery: correct words do not imply correct pitch or voice identity, and high embedding similarity need not imply exact words. Reference prove-nance also matters. Comparisons against teacher-extracted vocals measure agreement with that teacher, while isolated-source references support a different fidelity interpretation. We therefore distinguish these dimensions and reference types rather than treating one score as a complete measure of separation. 

2.8 Continuous Separation and Full-Duplex Conversation Data 

Continuous speech separation converts long recordings containing overlap into continuous output channels suitable for recognition. Speaker-independent CSS allows successive utterances to occupy different channels while keeping each utterance intact [43]. Our application requires an additional association step: indepen-dently generated pieces must be linked to persistent speaker identities and placed on the original recording timeline. Window planning, stream assignment, alignment, and targeted repair jointly determine whether the assembled tracks preserve coverage (section 7). Full-duplex dialogue models such as Moshi model user and system speech in parallel streams to support overlap and conversational timing [44]. This creates a need for temporally aligned conversational audio. Our pipeline prepares continuous speaker tracks and aligned transcripts offline from existing recordings. It checks speaker purity, missing speech, and content consistency before packaging the outputs. 

# 3 Generative Blind Voice Separation 

We formulate blind voice separation as conditional audio generation: given a single-channel recording, the model infers how many voices are present and generates a separate waveform for each. The recording may also contain music or environmental sound, and inference requires neither a supplied voice count nor a target-speaker query. Soloist implements this task by autoregressively generating a variable number of voice streams as sequences of audio-codec frames, which are decoded into waveforms. Its output contains only the separated voices, with no background or residual stream.

3.2 Architecture 

Soloist uses a Qwen3-4B decoder [45] conditioned on both continuous and discrete representations of the mixture (figure 2). The AuT encoder from Qwen3-Omni [46] supplies continuous features, and MOSS-Audio-Tokenizer-v2 [47] supplies twelve residual codec codes per frame at 12.5 frames/s. Gated multilayer percep-trons project the two representations into the backbone space and sum them at input frame positions. At an output frame, a one-layer local transformer predicts the twelve codes sequentially, conditioned on the back-bone state. This frame-local decoder contains causal multi-head self-attention and a two-layer feed-forward network, each preceded by layer normalization and wrapped in a residual connection. Codebook-specific lin-ear heads predict each code using the backbone state and the preceding codes within that frame. A separate adaptor feeds the generated codes back into the backbone. The codec is fixed; the audio encoder, backbone, adaptors, and local transformer are trained. The full component configuration is listed in table 8. 

3.3 Output Format and Inference 

The backbone’s text head predicts tokens that control the output structure, including the voice count, stream indices, and audio boundaries. For separation, it first emits the count and the first stream index, followed by an audio-start token to begin that stream. Whenever the text head emits a frame-request token, the corre-sponding backbone hidden state conditions the local transformer, which generates the twelve codec codes for that audio frame. The output adaptor maps these codes back into the backbone input space for subsequent autoregressive steps. Frame generation repeats until the text head emits an audio-end token. The model then generates the next stream index and its audio, or emits an end-of-response token to end the response. The codec decoder converts each stream’s frame sequence into a waveform. 6During supervised training, voice targets are ordered by first onset to define a canonical serial output se-quence; evaluation still matches generated streams to references by permutation. For the speaker-timeline task, a separate instruction asks the text head to emit [start ][ speaker ][ end ] triples sorted by start time, with-out triggering audio-frame generation. 74.1 Data Construction 

Constructed mixtures. We construct mixtures from voice sources, a cleaned Pixabay sound-effect library, and music recordings. Mixtures are built in three steps. Semantic tagging labels each background recording with a scene category derived from pre-computed scene topics. Candidate retrieval queries each scene label for se-mantically similar audio and keeps a pool of 20 candidate clips that mixes long and short recordings at a fixed ratio. Mixture synthesis lays a long recording as the acoustic bed when a bed is used, overlays short sound ef-fects at random positions, and adds the voice sources as independent streams. Because every source is known by construction, the isolated voice waveforms serve as separation targets without manual annotation. Scene mixtures place one to four Chinese, English, or Japanese voices on a retrieved background: a music or ambi-ent bed, a bed with transient effects, transient effects alone, or silence. Overlay and background-preserving windows instead add donor voices to 5–30 s windows that already carry an acoustic background. Overlay windows contain three to five voices, while background-preserving windows contain two to six. 

Speaker timelines. Speaker timelines describe who speaks when without providing either words or sepa-rated waveforms. These rows train the model to infer speaker activity from natural acoustics through the timeline instruction. They do not provide waveform supervision for separation. We annotate the natural recordings with MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize [24], retaining speaker labels and start/end times to construct the timeline targets. We select multi-speaker recordings from in-domain data spanning film, television, animation, short video, podcasts, radio, audiobooks, and sports commentary to provide speaker-activity supervision. The nominal windows span 10–120 s and contain two to six speakers. Targets are time-interval triples ordered by start time, with speaker IDs assigned by first appearance. Upstream transcripts are excluded from the prediction target; the output format is specified in appendix A.3. 

Singing vocals. A fixed Mel-Band RoFormer separator [19] extracts a vocal pseudo-target from each song. Training uses the song as input and the teacher’s vocal output as a single target stream. Accompaniment is not an output target. This is teacher supervision, so agreement with the pseudo-target measures consistency with the teacher rather than recovery of an independently recorded studio vocal. Song segments are cut from in-house song recordings into clips of at most two minutes before teacher ex-traction. The teacher’s vocal stream can contain backing vocals or multiple singers, so it forms a combined vocal target rather than individual singer streams.

# 5 Reinforcement Learning on Real Recordings 

Supervised training establishes the separation interface using source audio and speaker timelines. Reinforce-ment learning then adapts the model to real recordings without isolated source targets. For each recording, we sample alternative separations and use a model judge to compare voice purity, content coverage, and audio quality. 

5.1 RL Data 

The RL prompt set consists of real recordings without isolated source targets. Every recording has a voice-count annotation. AliMeeting [48] uses its manual TextGrid annotations; other recordings use dataset anno-tations where available and MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize otherwise. All prompts use the model-judge reward described in section 5.2, with the annotated count used in the signal checks and ranking request. The recordings include far-field meetings, film, television, animation, podcasts, short video, and audiobooks. 

5.2 Model-Judge Reward on Real Recordings 

In early trials, directly asking Qwen3-Omni to assign rubric scores to separated audio produced large score fluctuations across repeated evaluations of the same clip. We therefore construct relative feedback in two steps (figure 4): an audio-language model first describes the mixture and each candidate track independently, and a text model then ranks candidate sets from these reports, using an anonymous copy of the input mix-ture as an anchor. Repeating the ranking under reversed candidate order provides a consistency check for selecting feedback used in policy updates. 

Signal checks. We screen stream duration, signal level, and active fraction, then detect likely input copies and duplicate streams with a log-mel frame-match score. 5.3 Judge Inference and Failure Handling 

Each perception call processes one clip. For long recordings, the mixture, anchor, and candidate streams share a uniformly positioned 30 s window. Mixture descriptions provide global context; independently ob-tained track descriptions provide the evidence for each candidate. The mixture anchor uses the same track prompt as generated streams. The text ranker receives the mixture report, the candidate track reports, and the count annotation 𝐾 ; it receives no audio. The perception requests do not receive the count annotation. The serving backend disables thinking through the chat-template option. If the primary text service fails, the implementation falls back to the perception model for text ranking. This branch uses a different ranker. A description fails when the perception model returns no valid JSON in three attempts. A surviving candidate with a failed track description is left out of the ranking and receives a floor reward of 0.05; if fewer than two fully described candidates remain, the whole group is rejected.

# 6 Evaluation 

6.1 Evaluation Setup 

We evaluate separation in three domains: overlapping speech, noisy environments, and singing. Baselines comprise the speech separators SepFormer [2], MossFormer [5], ConvTasNet [1], and SR-CorrNet [11], and the query-based extractors SAM Audio [18], AudioSep [16], and SoloAudio [17], whose target and residual outputs are scored as two streams. With supplied counts, we prefill Soloist’s first output token with 2 for LibriMix, or 1 for Voices in the Wild (ViW) and singing vocals, matching baselines with fixed output counts or a single target. 

6.1.1 Evaluation Data 

LibriMix. Clean and noised two-speaker mixtures are generated with the official LibriMix scripts [7]. 

Voices in the Wild (ViW) subset. We select 3,000 single-speaker clips from Voices-in-the-Wild-2M [51]. From its 54 compound acoustic scenarios, we retain twelve: five single conditions ( noise , echo , far_field ,

obstructed , and recording ) and seven pairs ( echo_noise , echo_recording , far_field_noise ,

far_field_recording , obstructed_noise , obstructed_recording , and recording_noise ). Scenarios involving distortion or dropout are excluded because they corrupt or remove speech content beyond the denoising and over-splitting behavior evaluated here. The retained conditions cover noise, reverberation, distance, occlusion, and recording-device artifacts. We require decodable audio, a duration of 1–30 s, and a nonempty transcript, then randomly sample 250 clips per scenario (125 Mandarin and 125 English) with a fixed seed. Audio is resampled to 16 kHz mono. The resulting set spans 1.0–19.65 s per clip (median 5.11 s), totaling 5.54 h. 

WildTV. WildTV consists of 46 real-world speech clips from several domains, including film and TV drama and podcasts, each with one to three speakers: 11 with one speaker, 25 with two, and 10 with three. Refer-ence labels (speaker counts and per-speaker transcripts) are annotated with MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize [24] and then manually reviewed and corrected. Soloist and SR-CorrNet (the released SR-CorrNet-SS checkpoint) each use a single model with blind count inference. SepFormer, MossFormer, and ConvTasNet switch check-points using the true count: enhancement for one speaker, Libri2Mix for two, and WSJ0Mix for three. Their perfect output-count agreement follows from this construction. 

Singing benchmarks. SongYT contains 3,000 song recordings in Chinese, English, and Japanese, so it tests whether separation preserves vocals across languages and regional singing styles. SongYT uses vocal pseudo-references produced by Mel-Band RoFormer [19]. Agreement with these references measures con-sistency with the teacher estimate. MIR-1K [20] contains 1,000 clips of 4–13 s cut from 110 Chinese karaoke pop songs sung by 19 mostly amateur singers. Accompaniment and singing voice are recorded on the left and right channels, so every clip has an isolated vocal reference. MUSDB18-HQ [21] is the uncompressed version of MUSDB18: 150 full-length stereo tracks of various genres (about 10 h), each with isolated vocals, 12 drums, bass, and other stems. MUSDB18-HQ has no lyric references, so we report only RPA and SingerSim on it.

Extra output can therefore push WER above 1, while an extra empty transcript adds no errors. 

Transcription and PI-WER. Separated outputs are resampled to 16 kHz and transcribed with Whisper large-v3 [52]. Text is normalized with Unicode NFKC and case folding. CJK characters, kana, and Hangul are tokenized character by character, while Latin text is tokenized into words. Punctuation separates tokens, ex-cept that internal apostrophes are retained. Thus, the reported WER uses script-dependent word/character units. Count accuracy compares the number of voiced output streams with the reference speaker count; the declared count is stored separately as stated .Corpus-level WER is a micro-average: total edit errors divided by total reference tokens, rather than the mean of clip-level WERs. 

Speech quality. We use DNSMOS on ViW to measure how much background noise remains after separa-tion. ViW clips carry noise, reverberation, and other acoustic degradations, and WER alone cannot show whether a system removes the noise or only keeps the speech intelligible. DNSMOS [53] is a non-intrusive neural predictor of ITU-T P.835 subjective ratings. Its background-noise score (BAK) rates how intrusive the remaining background is, and its overall score (OVRL) rates overall quality; both use a 1–5 scale with higher values better. We report means and medians over the ViW clips. 

Music similarity and pitch. SingerSim uses layer-14 features from WavLM-large [42], with L2 normalization on RMS-active frames followed by mean pooling. RPA (raw pitch accuracy) is the fraction of voiced reference frames whose estimated F0 lies within 50 cents of the reference F0. Transcript-based metrics are insensitive to melody: a separated vocal can keep every lyric while its pitch drifts. This matters for a generative separator, which re-synthesizes the vocal rather than masking the mixture. RPA therefore tests whether separation preserves the sung melody, complementing WER for lyric content and SingerSim for vocal similarity.

6.7 Judge Validation on Known-Source Mixtures 

The judge never observes isolated sources, reference transcripts, or evaluation metrics; it only orders candi-dates of the same recording. We therefore test whether this ordering agrees with a reference ordering on mixtures whose sources are known. We take 40 LibriMix mixtures (20 clean and 20 noised) and, for each, eight separation outputs randomly drawn from the inference results of intermediate supervised checkpoints. LibriMix sources are clean read speech, so PI-WER reflects most quality differences between separation out-puts and serves as the reference ordering. The judge describes each track with Qwen3-Omni, ranks each group twice in text with the candidate order reversed, and applies the order-consistency check. In this test the text ranker runs at temperature 0, and dead and below-anchor candidates receive a common floor score; pairs are compared by the final judge scores. With Qwen3.6-27B as the text ranker, 36 of the 40 groups pass the order-consistency check. These groups contain 307 output pairs whose PI-WER differs by more than 0.2. The judge prefers one output in 207 of 15 them and agrees with the PI-WER order in 184, or 88.9% (95% confidence interval 80.8–95.6% from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of groups). Per group, Kendall’s 𝜏 𝑏 between the judge scores and the PI-WER order has a median of 0.40 (interquartile range 0.16–0.57) over the 31 passing groups whose judge scores are not all tied, and it is positive in 29 of them.

7.1 Window Mining and Planning 

Window mining. The pipeline first selects conversation windows that are long enough to capture turn-taking and reliable enough to annotate. Each window contains exactly 𝐾 speakers, gives each speaker enough speech to keep short backchannels, including at least 1 s of exclusive speech, and is dense in speech. Bound-aries fall in silence gaps and avoid the end of a possibly truncated transcript. Windows with ambiguous multi-speaker labels, near-duplicates of another window from the same recording, or a suspicious single-block speaker annotation are discarded. For 𝐾 = 2, each retained window is assigned to the overlap or turn-taking stratum according to whether the timeline contains a sufficiently long speaker intersection; overlap that the timeline misses is therefore not reflected in the strata. Windows with 𝐾 = 3 form a single stratum. 

Piece planning. A conversation window is too long to separate in one pass, so it is split into inference pieces. A dynamic program chooses pieces that cover all annotated speech while minimizing the sum of piece du-ration times requested stream count, a proxy for the number of generated audio tokens. Each piece requests one stream per speaker active in it, rather than one per speaker in the whole window, and adds context on both sides for later cross-fading. Long monologues are split before planning, and a soft penalty, proportional to the missing duration and the stream count, discourages pieces shorter than the preferred length for their stream count. A piece requests at most min (𝐾, 3) streams. 

7.2 Separation and Assembly 

Separation and speaker assignment. Soloist separates every planned piece, including single-speaker pieces. CAM++ embeddings [25] then assign the local streams to the 𝐾 conversation speakers, and a cross-piece consistency term keeps assignments stable across neighboring pieces. A stream is rejected as mixed when its second-highest speaker similarity is also high; the margin adapts to the similarity between the speaker references, so that speakers with similar voices are not rejected wholesale. 

Placement. Each assigned stream is placed on the recording time axis relative to its speaker’s timeline onset. When its confidence is high enough, energy-envelope alignment refines the offset within a bounded range. Neighboring pieces are cross-faded over their shared context. Timeline masks, slightly expanded but never entering other speakers’ intervals, remove residual speech outside the speaker’s own activity, and all zeroed regions are filled with a low-level noise floor sampled from the track’s quietest frames. Speaker verification over each speaker’s full timeline intervals then checks the assembled assignment. For 𝐾 = 2, the two tracks are swapped only when both assignments are reversed; for 𝐾 ≥ 3, the Hungarian algorithm reassigns tracks whenever a better permutation exists. Streams that remain misassigned after this check are discarded. 

Repair. Pieces that return too few usable streams, and streams rejected during assignment, are re-queried. The repair window keeps the active-speaker set and strictly contains the previous interval, so a replacement cannot open new coverage gaps. For a piece that already had enough streams, a replacement is accepted only if it improves separation, judged by the energy contrast between speaker streams and by envelope agreement in their exclusive regions; when those regions are too short to compare, the existing output is kept. For a piece with missing streams, a replacement is accepted once it provides enough voiced streams. Repair stops at a round limit or when the number of empty slots stops decreasing appreciably, and unrecoverable streams remain explicitly marked as missing. 17 7.3 Quality Control 

Each assembled track is checked for three failure modes, each requiring different evidence. 

Intrusive speech. MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize re-diarizes each track. When it detects more than one speaker, speaker verification localizes the intrusive intervals with CAM++ and ERes2Net [26]; a region is kept only when both verifiers accept the target speaker, and scrubbed intervals are filled with the noise floor. An utterance that is mostly scrubbed is removed from the timeline. The track fails the purity check if minority-speaker speech that the verifiers cannot resolve (score gap within 0.10) accumulates beyond the threshold, or if verification cannot cover it. Combining diarization with verification separates true intrusions from diarization over-segmenting a single speaker. 

Missing speech. Hole detection compares the track with the mixture over the source speaker timeline. The hole fraction is measured over frames in the speaker’s exclusive regions where the mixture has speech-level energy. 

Mismatched content. A track can carry the right voice with the wrong words. Qwen3-ASR-1.7B and Qwen3-ForcedAligner-0.6B [54] produce the track transcript and word timestamps, and the word count is compared with MOSS-Transcribe-Diarize. Within exclusive-speaker regions, an utterance whose normalized energy envelope correlates poorly with the mixture is marked as mismatched, with an amplitude-domain fallback. Instead of discarding the whole conversation, the pipeline cuts out mismatched utterances by splitting all tracks and the mixture at those utterances into separate samples; pieces shorter than 20 s are dropped, so final samples can be shorter than 60 s. Regions with too little exclusive speech are marked unassessable and reported separately. 

7.4 Acceptance and Output 

A conversation is accepted when every speaker has a track and transcript, every track passes the purity check, each speaker’s completeness reaches the threshold, the speaker with the least speech contributes enough, and no track fails the hole gate. The hole gate requires both a large hole fraction and a long missing duration, so very short exclusive regions are not rejected on their ratio alone. Completeness is an automatic proxy that can exceed one, so it is used only for coarse filtering. A batch must also stay within the failure-rate limits in table 15, and every delivered track must be non-silent. Listening to stress cases complements these automatic checks. Each accepted conversation provides one 24 kHz mono track per speaker (and, for two speakers, a stereo version with one speaker per channel), together with speaker intervals, utterances, and word timestamps on the same time axis. Speaker assignments are marked as model-derived. A filtered export applies all gates, while an unfiltered export keeps the hole, purity, and completeness metadata for downstream selection. 

# 8 Conclusion 

We introduced Soloist, a generative model that separates individual voices from a mixture without a target-speaker query or a supplied voice count. Its training combines source-audio and speaker-timeline supervi-sion with reinforcement learning on real recordings, where a model judge compares independently obtained track descriptions under repeated candidate orderings. With supplied voice counts, Soloist achieves the lowest transcription error among the compared separators on noisy overlapping speech, speech in noisy environments, and singing vocals. On real-world multi-speaker recordings with unknown voice counts, it also separates voices well, while the singing results show that lyric content, pitch accuracy, and vocal similarity capture different aspects of separation quality. We also integrate the model into an offline pipeline that assembles continuous speaker tracks and aligned transcripts from 18 real conversations. Together, the model and recipe provide a way to learn voice separation from constructed mixtures and natural recordings with incomplete references.
