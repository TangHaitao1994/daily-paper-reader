# DRIFTTTS: ONE-STAGE SINGLE STEP TEXT-TO-SPEECH VIA GENERATIVE DRIFTING 

# Jaehyun Ahn, Dohyun Kim, Heeseung Kim †

# Department of AI, University of Seoul, Seoul, South Korea 

ABSTRACT 

Text-to-speech models built on score matching and flow matching produce natural speech, but only by calling the decoder many times, which makes synthesis slow. Existing ways to cut the number of calls either distill from a multi-step teacher or add a stage on top of a pretrained model, leaving single-step TTS without a pretrained TTS model unexplored. We question whether pretraining a TTS model is necessary, and propose DriftTTS, which adopts generative drifting, an objective that moves iterative sampling from inference into training. Drifting assumes samples of fixed shape, whereas speech has variable length; we address this issue with seg-ment drifting. On LJSpeech, DriftTTS matches the multi-step quality of Matcha-TTS and RapFlow-TTS in a single decoder call, while being faster. To our knowledge, this is the first application of generative drifting to text-to-speech. 

Index Terms — text-to-speech, generative drifting, single step generation, one-stage training 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Generative models have advanced since the introduction of diffusion models [1]. Earlier approaches often suffered from over-smoothing, a limitation diffusion models are widely re-garded as having largely overcome. Instead of transporting a prior sample to the data distribution in a single step, they train a model to remove a small amount of noise at a time, and generate data by applying it repeatedly, which stably models continuous distributions. Building on this paradigm of dis-cretized iterative sampling, subsequent work has produced score-based generative models [2], which estimate the gra-dient of the log-density of perturbed data distributions. However, iterative sampling requires a large number of function evaluations (NFE), which slows down inference and has motivated efforts to simplify the sampling process. Flow Matching [3] provides a simulation-free framework for train-ing continuous normalizing flows by directly regressing the vector field that transports prior samples to data. Neverthe-less, generation still relies on iterative integration, leaving the number of function evaluations as an important bottleneck. MeanFlow [4] addresses this limitation by learning the aver-age velocity between two points in time instead of the instan-

taneous velocity, enabling effective generation with very few function evaluations. Consistency Flow Matching [5] instead imposes velocity consistency across different starting times toward the same endpoint, encouraging straighter sampling trajectories. These approaches have demonstrated strong per-formance at low NFE in image generation. Text-to-speech (TTS), which generates natural speech from text, has largely followed this progression. Grad-TTS [6] introduces score-based generative modeling, while Matcha-TTS [7] adopts flow matching. Both achieve high-quality speech synthesis but still require a non-trivial number of sampling steps, typically ten or more NFE. IntMeanFlow [8], a zero-shot TTS model, adapts MeanFlow to speech by learning the average velocity along trajectories generated by a pretrained flow matching model, achieving performance comparable to existing models with a single NFE. RapFlow-TTS [9] instead adapts consistency flow matching by further training a pretrained Matcha-TTS so that points along its es-timated trajectory are mapped toward the same data sample. With only two NFE, it matches or surpasses Matcha-TTS using ten. However, both approaches depend on a pretrained TTS model to provide the underlying generation trajectory, increasing training complexity and computational cost. In this work, we introduce DriftTTS, a TTS model that achieves competitive performance with a single NFE without relying on a pretrained TTS model. DriftTTS builds on gener-ative drifting [10], which moves iterative sampling from infer-ence to training. Generative drifting formulates sampling as a pushforward map from the prior to the data distribution and directly learns this map during training. At each training step, the model is updated to minimize the magnitude of the drift from its predicted distribution toward the data distribution. To the best of our knowledge, DriftTTS is the first application of generative drifting to TTS. Our experiments show that DriftTTS with a single NFE performs on par with existing few-step TTS models while requiring fewer function evaluations and no pretrained TTS model. Ablation studies further show that both the feature ex-tractor and segment-level drifting are necessary: without ei-ther component, training leads to loudness divergence or false convergence. 3. DRIFTTTS 3.1. Architecture of DriftTTS 

DriftTTS builds on Matcha-TTS and consists of a text en-coder, a duration predictor, and a decoder. The text encoder converts the input text into token-level representations. Dur-ing training, monotonic alignment search (MAS) [12] aligns these representations with the target mel-spectrogram. Specif-ically, MAS searches for a monotonic alignment that min-imizes the L2 distance between the target mel-spectrogram and the text encoder outputs. The resulting alignment pro-vides the duration of each token, which is used to train the duration predictor. It is also used to construct the prior sam-ple μ by repeating each token representation according to its duration, such that μ has the same length as the target mel-spectrogram. Since MAS explicitly searches for an alignment that minimizes the L2 distance, the resulting μ is already close to the target mel-spectrogram. The text encoder and duration predictor are trained using the prior loss Lprior and duration loss Lduration , respectively. Unlike Matcha-TTS, whose decoder repeatedly predicts a velocity field that is integrated over time, the DriftTTS de-coder directly predicts the target mel-spectrogram. Generative drifting does not require a timestep input, allowing us to re-move the timestep embedding from the Matcha-TTS decoder and reduce the number of parameters. 3.2. Segment Drifting 

Generative drifting is formulated for fixed-shape images with class conditions. TTS, in contrast, involves variable-length speech and text conditions that do not belong to a fixed set of classes. To handle variable length, we sample fixed-length segments from both the prior and target speech during train-ing, following [6]. 4. EXPERIMENTS 4.1. System 

We trained both DriftTTS and MAE on LJSpeech [13], fol-lowing the split used in [7]. The predicted mel-spectrogram is converted to speech using HiFi-GAN [14], and we therefore follow the mel-spectrogram configuration of HiFi-GAN V1. DriftTTS was trained for 2000 epochs on four NVIDIA A10 GPUs using AdamW with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4

and a weight decay of 0.01. Gradients are clipped at 5.0, and an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights with a decay of 0.999 is used for inference. The batch size mat-ters more here than usual: the targets in a batch serve as the positive samples and the generated segments as the negative samples, and previous work reports that generation improves as both increase [10]. We therefore use the largest batch that fits in memory, 64 pairs of text and speech per GPU. The text encoder is trained on the entire utterance, while the decoder is trained on a segment of 80 frames ( ∼0.93 ,s), and monotonic alignment search runs on GPU with SuperMAS [15]. The decoder takes a segment of the prior μ obtained by MAS together with a noise sample as a stochastic condition, and predicts the target segment. The predicted segments serve both as the generated samples x and as the negative samples

4.2. Evaluations 

We measure pronunciation accuracy by word error rate (WER), transcribed with openai/whisper-large-v3-turbo [16], perceptual naturalness by a 5-point mean opin-ion score (MOS) study on the evaluation set, and synthesis speed by the real-time factor (RTF). For each utterance, RTF is computed as the model inference time excluding the vocoder, divided by the generated audio duration. We average RTF over the 500 evaluation utterances at a batch size of one. Every system is run in the same environment on a single GPU, and we report the median over five interleaved runs. For a controlled comparison, we focus on models that share the Matcha-TTS architecture or are directly built upon it. At a single step, we compare DriftTTS with Matcha-TTS, Matcha-TTS with MeanFlow applied, and RapFlow-TTS. We additionally evaluate the standard multi-step settings of Matcha-TTS and RapFlow-TTS, using ten and two steps, respectively, to examine whether single-step DriftTTS can match their reference generation quality. We first ablate the MAE by applying segment drifting di-rectly in the mel-spectrogram space, testing whether the fea-ture extractor is necessary. We then replace segment drift-ing with frame drifting, where each frame is treated indepen-dently. 4.3. Results 

Table 1 summarizes the main comparison. DriftTTS achieves comparable WER and MOS to the other synthesized systems. Matcha-TTS loses much of its naturalness at a single NFE. Matcha-TTS with MeanFlow and RapFlow-TTS recover most of this loss, but both require further training of a pretrained Matcha-TTS. DriftTTS reaches this level of quality with a single NFE, trained in a single stage without any pretrained TTS model. It also performs comparably to Matcha-TTS at ten steps and RapFlow-TTS at two steps while running about 

4.2× and 1.3× faster, respectively. The single-step baselines remain slightly faster, which we attribute to our decoder im-plementation rather than the training objective. Table 2 reports the ablations. The single temperatures perform comparably to the multi-temperature loss in UT-MOSv2 and DNSMOS, with no single value consistently outperforming the others. We adopt the multi-temperature loss with [0 .02 , 0.05 , 0.2] from previous work [10], which also achieves the lowest WER. The feature extractor and drifting unit, in contrast, matter substantially. When segment drifting is applied directly in the mel-spectrogram space with-out the MAE, every metric collapses as the predicted energy diverges: high-energy mel bins are pushed toward the upper bound of 0, while low-energy bins continue decreasing. We hypothesize that distances between high-dimensional raw mel segments are dominated by coarse energy differences, caus-ing the drifting field to emphasize overall energy rather than local spectral structure. 5. CONCLUSION 

We introduced DriftTTS, a TTS model built upon generative drifting that moves iterative sampling from inference to train-ing, predicting the target mel-spectrogram directly rather than its dynamics, in a single step and without any pretrained TTS model. To apply generative drifting to speech, we proposed segment drifting, which samples fixed-length segments dur-ing training and uses the encoded text representation as the prior. It thereby removes the inference cost that grows with the number of steps, while performing on par with the multi-step results of existing models. Extending DriftTTS to multi-speaker and zero-shot synthesis and reducing its dependence on the trained feature extractor remain future work. 6. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS
