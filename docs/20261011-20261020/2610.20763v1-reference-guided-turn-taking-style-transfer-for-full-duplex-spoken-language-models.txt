ABSTRACT 

Full-duplex spoken language models decide at every frame whether to take the floor, defining a conversational person-ality — yet each set of weights encodes exactly one, which human speakers bracket rather than share. Existing controls mostly act from outside; none identifies the quantity that sets how much the model speaks. We identify it: a single late-layer residual-stream direction, the initiative vector , ex-tracted from the model’s own conversations without training or annotation. The same direction reads the decision, antici-pating onsets up to 800 ms ahead, and shifts it: scaled during generation, it moves speech ratio from 6–12% to 42–61% (no steering: 21–30%), consistently across conversations, weights, and corpora. A short reference recording suffices to transfer a speaker’s turn-taking style to the model, and the transfer is reference-specific: swapping in another speaker’s reference worsens the match in 93–98% of cases, whereas the best uniform dose is indistinguishable from no steering. Turn-taking style is not frozen into the weights but a direction that can be read, shifted, and calibrated per person. Au-dio examples are available at https://elpsykongloo. github.io/initiative-vector/ .

Index Terms — Conversational Style Cloning, Full-Duplex Spoken Language Models, Conversational Dynamics 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Spoken language models are converging toward full-duplex interaction [1–3], listening while speaking and deciding at ev-ery frame whether to take the floor rather than waiting for an explicit endpoint [4, 5]. Collectively, this frame-level initia-tive defines a conversational personality — eager or patient, interruptive or deferential [6] — and different applications de-mand different ones: a tutoring agent should lead; a com-panion should listen. Activa-tion steering acts on the model itself [20–22], yet perception vectors set a binary listen/speak state at a fixed dose on one checkpoint: a fix for interruption errors, not a variable to be set per person. Whether a full-duplex spoken language model takes the floor is usually treated as an emergent property of its next-token distribution, decodable from internal states [23] but never set by degree. A single direction in the residual stream, the initiative vector , linearly encodes the model’s propensity to speak [24,25]; scaling the residual along that direction dur-ing generation controls that propensity. Because the direction is computed from the model’s own conversations, contrasting frames that precede its speech onsets with frames where it stays silent [26], each model yields its own vector under an identical procedure (Fig. 1). The direction reads the decision to take the floor: its projection anticipates the model’s speech onsets well before they occur, above a baseline built from both speakers’ timing, and remains predictive even when no recent text is present (Fig. 2b). It also shifts the decision: as the steering dose increases, the model’s speech ratio rises monotonically, con-sistently across conversations, weights, and corpora (Fig. 3). Refitting such contrasts at every layer locates the computa-tion: whether the model is currently speaking is decodable from the first layer, evidence for yielding the floor peaks in earlier layers and then fades, and evidence for taking the floor strengthens toward the final layer (Fig. 2a). The model thus decides to take the floor late in the network, well after its speaking state is available and at a different depth from its decision to yield. Calibrated against a short reference record-ing, the direction transfers a speaker’s turn-taking style, and the transfer is reference-specific: neither a uniform dose nor another speaker’s reference matches as well (Fig. 4). Our contributions are as follows: 1. Initiative vectors. A single residual-stream direction, extracted without training or annotation, both antici-pates the model’s onsets and sets how much it speaks — across weights and corpora. 2. Taking the floor is a late-stage computation , peaking in the final layers, with the speaking state decodable in the first and yield evidence peaking between them. 3. Reference-guided style transfer. A single short recording suffices to transfer a speaker’s turn-taking style, with no retraining; swapping in another speaker’s reference worsens the match in 93–98% of cases.

L30 is the deepest position whose perturbation is still processed by a full transformer block and written into that block’s key–value cache; the shift therefore persists in the context that later frames attend to. 

Depth profile. To locate the decision, a class-mean con-trast of the same form is refitted at each of the 32 positions for four targets, each scored by held-out AUC: taking the floor within 400 or 800 ms of a frame at which both speakers are silent, being silent 800 ms after a frame of sustained speech (yielding), and the current speaking state. 

Reference-guided transfer. A speaker is summarized by three descriptors of a five-minute reference recording: speech ratio, overlap onsets per minute of partner speech, and re-sponse probability, the share of partner turn ends ( ≥400 ms of partner silence) followed within 3 s by the speaker’s onset be-fore the partner resumes, with turn ends during the speaker’s own speech kept in the denominator. 3. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP Models and conversations. We study three full-duplex weights that share the Moshi architecture [1]: Moshika (base), Moshika-RL, further aligned for interactivity [15], and PersonaPlex [7], all operating on 80 ms frames. Each model hears one side of a dyadic Seamless Interaction record-ing [27] as its user stream (64 s, after 3 s of silence) and gen-erates the other side. Directions and baselines are fitted on 100 conversations and evaluated on a fixed set of 500 held-out conversations per weight. The same directions and scale are applied, without refitting, to 500 CANDOR segments [28]. Doses are {− 2a, −a, 0, a, 2a} with a = 0.125 (Moshika, PersonaPlex) or 0.25 (Moshika-RL). 

Readout. AUC is computed per conversation and aver-aged. The baseline, trained on the same 100 conversations, is a logistic regression on timing alone: recent activity of both speakers, the time since each last spoke, activity rates, and the time since the model’s last text token. 

Transfer. References are five-minute windows of Seam-less Interaction speakers from naturalistic and improvised conversations, with descriptors extracted by Silero VAD 1.Standard deviations come from 120 training references, and the best uniform dose is selected for them on 100 develop-ment targets; each individual dose uses the same 100 targets alone, and transfer is evaluated on 550 targets for 568 held-out references, averaged over three seeds. Baselines are no steering and the best uniform dose; the dose that is best in hindsight for each reference serves as the upper bound. In-tervals are 95% two-way bootstrap intervals over targets and references (5,000 resamples, stratified by conversation type, doses fixed).

Taking and yielding the floor peak at different depths. 

Across the 32 positions (Fig. 2a), onset evidence is strongest at the deepest ones on all three weights (0.968, 0.898 and 0.966 at L31 for onsets within 400 ms), whereas evidence for yielding the floor peaks mid-network (L26, L22, L19) and then fades. Taking and yielding the floor are thus separate axes at different depths. The speaking state is decodable at about 0.99 from L0: it is present in the input rather than com-puted. The model thus decides to take the floor late in the network. 

Adding the direction sets how much the model speaks. 

Speech ratio rises monotonically with the dose on all three weights, from 6–12% at −2a to 42–61% at +2 a, against 21– 30% without steering (Fig. 3a), and overlap onsets and re-sponse probability move in the same direction. Under the same input and seed, moving from −a to +a raises it by 20.1, 27.4 and 14.1 points, with increases on 480, 497 and 408 of 500 conversations (Fig. 3b). The effect belongs to the direc-tion rather than to the size of the perturbation: three norm-matched random directions at the same displacement change speech ratio by at most 5.6 points. The same direction and scale carry over to CANDOR without refitting ( +20 .3, +33 .9

and +6 .8 points). 

One dose per speaker transfers that speaker’s style. 

On average, the distance to a reference is smallest at its own dose on all three weights (Fig. 4a). Swapping in another held-out reference’s dose, whenever it differs, worsens the match in 92.7, 98.0 and 95.6% of cases, and on average for 568, 568 and 566 of the 568 references. After calibration, the model’s speech ratio follows its reference across deciles (Spearman 0.69, 0.60, 0.78; Fig. 4b). 

The gain comes from personalization itself. References fall on both sides of every model’s default (Fig. 4b), so no sin-gle setting fits them: the best uniform dose is indistinguish-able from no steering on every weight, with all 95% inter-vals including zero. The per-speaker dose improves on it by 0.129, 0.208 and 0.164 reference SD, 93.1, 99.5 and 98.1% of the gain of the dose that is best in hindsight, and it is strictly closer for 317/317, 257/257 and 270/289 of the references whose dose differs from the uniform one (Fig. 4d). Ten de-velopment conversations per weight already reach the plateau of this gain (Fig. 4c). The dose a speaker needs is also consis-tent across weights: the grid levels selected for the same held-out reference correlate at Spearman ρ = 0 .81 (Moshika–RL), 0.77 (Moshika–PersonaPlex) and 0.70 (RL–PersonaPlex), so calibration captures a property of the speaker rather than of one checkpoint. 

5. CONCLUSION 

A full-duplex model’s turn-taking style need not be fixed at training time. A single late-layer direction, extracted from the model’s own conversations without training or annota-tion, anticipates when the model will take the floor, sets how much it speaks across weights and corpora, and, from a single short recording, transfers an individual speaker’s style with-out retraining. The benefit comes from personalization itself: turn-taking becomes a setting that can be read, shifted, and calibrated per person. 6. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS 

This study was conducted retrospectively using human sub-ject data made available for research by the Seamless Interac-tion [27] and CANDOR [28] corpora. Ethical approval was not required as confirmed by the licenses attached with the data.
