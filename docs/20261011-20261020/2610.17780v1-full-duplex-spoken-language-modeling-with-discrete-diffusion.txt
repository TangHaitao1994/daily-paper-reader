# ABSTRACT 

We introduce Diffuplex , a full-duplex spoken language model based on discrete diffusion that achieves an unprecedented balance between linguistic intelligence and response efficiency. With thinking mode, Diffuplex delivers 13 .0 tokens per forward pass, reaching an average of 1872 tokens per second and substantially surpassing the frontier autoregressive (AR) models in decoding speed. Without thinking, Diffuplex matches the first-audio latency of existing full-duplex models while achieving markedly stronger linguistic intelligence. This capability is en-abled by separating when to say from what to say at the training level, rather than compressing both into a single autoregressive timeline. During inference, Diffu-plex continuously tracks the user speech through an AR interaction policy, while independently generating and refining response content through parallel diffusion. Consequently, semantic computation is no longer bounded by the perception audio frame rate, allowing intelligence and responsiveness to improve simultaneously. Taken together, these results establish a new paradigm for spoken agents that can sustain real-time dialogue while devoting parallel computation to intelligent re-sponse generation. # 1 INTRODUCTION 

An ideal spoken language model should exhibit both fluid conversational behavior ( when to say )and intelligent response generation ( what to say ). From a cognitive perspective, when to say re-quires tracking real-time speaking stream of interlocutor, whereas what to say involves progres-sively formulating internal thoughts. Neural recordings during conversation identify distinct activity associated with speech perception, planning, and production, even when these processes overlap in time (Castellucci et al., 2022). This suggests a useful design principle: conversational timing and response formulation should be coordinated without being forced into a single sequential process. Turning this separation into a spoken language model, however, remains a formidable challenge. While recent full-duplex models that unify when to say and what to say deliver fluid interactions, they exhibit severe linguistic and cognitive degradation compared with frontier autoregressive (AR) models—which handle only what to say and rely on an external voice activity detection to govern response timing. We argue that this degradation arises from conflating these two learnable dimen-sions into a single one-dimensional AR timeline: (1) Conflicting Objectives: Optimizing both tasks via a single Cross-Entropy loss results in gradient conflict, where the dense supervision of low-level temporal alignment heavily dilutes the delicate representations required for high-level semantic gen-eration Li et al. (2026). (2) Temporal Frame-Rate Binding: By tying AR generation directly to a rigid physical frame rate, e.g., 80 ms in Moshi (D´ efossez et al., 2024), the model is compelled to process semantics in lockstep with real-time spoken input, stripping its capacity to allocate elastic test-time compute (i.e., Chain-of-Thought) for deep inference. To break this coupling, we exploit a distinctive property of discrete diffusion language models (DLM): response tokens can be generated and refined in parallel (Nie et al., 2025). This introduces a second computational axis that is independent of physical time. As illustrated in Figure 2, an AR policy advances along the horizontal timeline t, continuously processing incoming speech and determining when a response should begin. Once triggered at time θ, a diffusion process expands orthogonally from the timeline and iteratively refines an entire response canvas (i.e., a generation block). To support streaming interaction, we further design an anchored first-n decoding strategy: the first n response tokens are finalized early and immediately synthesized, while audio playback provides a time buffer for the remaining token refinement. We instantiate this two-axis formulation as Diffuplex , a spoken language model that separates se-mantic generation from conversational control within a unified backbone. We efficiently connect a pretrained speech encoder (NVIDIA, 2025) to a text DLM (DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026), allowing Diffuplex to take spoken input as diffusion condition, while inheriting the linguistic capa-bilities and knowledge encoded in the text model. Since the same backbone can also operate in AR mode, we introduce only a lightweight state embedding and prediction head to learn semantically informed speaking decisions from conversational data. At inference time, the two modes assume complementary roles: the AR mode continuously processes the incoming speech stream and deter-mines when to say , while the diffusion mode is triggered at appropriate moments to generate and iteratively refine what to say in parallel. Experiments on public benchmarks evaluate Diffuplex along two complementary dimensions: re-sponse quality and conversational behavior. With thinking enabled, Diffuplex achieves an overall score of 86.9 across the 9 VoiceBench subsets (Chen et al., 2024), approaching the strongest leader-board systems while finalizing an average of 13.0 tokens per forward pass, compared with one token for the evaluated AR baselines. Meanwhile, Diffuplex exhibits responsive full-duplex behavior, with turn-taking and barge-in latency comparable to strong full-duplex models, while exceeding existing open-source systems by more than 20 points in spoken intelligence. Our contributions are threefold. First, we show that diffusion-based spoken language modeling de-livers both strong linguistic capabilities and exceptional generation speed, averaging 1,872 tokens per second on a single GB300 GPU—far outpacing its AR counterparts. Second, we demonstrate that conversational control and response generation can be learned separately within a shared back-bone: an AR policy tracks streaming speech, while diffusion generates response content. Third, we show how to translate fast parallel text generation into full-duplex spoken interaction, enabling rapid responses to conversational events such as turn-taking and barge-in. We believe these contributions lay the groundwork for building both fast and intelligent spoken agents, paving the way for seamless and highly capable voice interactions. 2Arxiv Preprint 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 SPOKEN LANGUAGE MODELS 

Turn-based spoken language models extend text LLMs with speech encoders or discrete acoustic tokens, enabling unified speech understanding and generation (Wang et al., 2026a). AudioPaLM transfers linguistic knowledge from a text LLM to speech tasks, while SpeechGPT develops cross-modal instruction-following through modality adaptation and speech instruction tuning (Rubenstein et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023). More recent systems, such as Qwen2.5-Omni, jointly generate text and speech through specialized reasoning and synthesis modules (Xu et al., 2025a). Despite their strong linguistic capabilities, these models largely retain a turn-oriented interaction protocol and generate response content autoregressively. Full-duplex models instead process user and agent speech as concurrent streams, allowing interrup-tions, overlaps, and backchannels without explicit turn segmentation (Wang et al., 2026b). Moshi models the two speakers with parallel audio-token streams, while OmniFlatten serializes multimodal dialogue events into a unified sequence (D´ efossez et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Freeze-Omni fur-ther connects streaming speech interfaces to a frozen text LLM to preserve its linguistic capabilities (Wang et al., 2024a). PersonaPlex adds role and voice control to a full-duplex speech model, Lychee-FD addresses acoustic–semantic interference, and Listen-Write-Speak provides a visible text chan-nel during real-time conversation (Roy et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). These systems achieve responsive interaction, but typically bind semantic generation and conversational control to the same frame-synchronous AR process. Diffuplex instead separates these functions: an AR policy tracks conversational timing, whereas a DLM generates response content along an independent computational axis. 2.2 DIFFUSION LANGUAGE MODELS WITH SPOKEN INPUT 

Diffusion language models generate text through iterative refinement rather than strictly left-to-right decoding. Early work explored continuous diffusion for controllable text generation (Li et al., 2022), while masked discrete diffusion substantially narrowed the likelihood gap with AR models (Sahoo et al., 2024). LLaDA demonstrated that diffusion language models can scale to general-purpose pre-training and instruction following (Nie et al., 2025). Most relevant to our work, DiffusionGemma converts a pretrained AR mixture-of-experts model into a discrete DLM that refines 256-token can-vases in parallel while retaining AR capabilities (DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026). The extension of DLMs to spoken input remains comparatively underexplored. Recent works have applied diffusion decoders to automatic speech recognition (ASR), either training a speech-conditioned diffusion decoder from scratch or attaching a frozen encoder or DLM for parallel tran-script decoding (Baas et al., 2022; Kwon et al., 2025; Yen et al., 2026; Khurdula et al., 2026). DIFFA moves beyond transcription by first aligning speech through ASR and subsequently training speech-conditioned instruction following; DIFFA-2 further scales this framework to general audio understanding (Zhou et al., 2026a;b). These studies establish that DLMs can understand spoken input, but do not address full-duplex interaction or explicitly separate conversational timing from response generation. Diffuplex closes this gap by combining a streaming AR interaction policy with parallel diffusion-based response generation. Cross-modal distillation has also been explored to align the capabilities of speech models with text teachers (Cao et al., 2026). 

# 3 METHOD 

Overview. Diffuplex follows a three-stage training curriculum. In Stage 1, we perform speech–text modality alignment using ASR data, enabling a pretrained text diffusion language model to condition on continuous speech representations. In Stage 2, we train the model on spoken-instruction–text-response pairs to acquire instruction-following capabilities, thereby learning what to say . In Stage 3, we preserve the capabilities acquired in Stage 2 while introducing an AR interaction mode that learns a conversational policy, i.e., when to say .At inference time, the model operates along two decoupled computational axes. In Section 3.4, we introduce denoising strategies designed to enable low-latency and fluid speech-to-speech interaction. 3.1 STAGE 1: M ODALITY ALIGNMENT 

We construct the speech frontend using a Parakeet encoder (NVIDIA, 2025) followed by a ran-domly initialized projection layer that maps acoustic representations into the embedding space of DiffusionGemma (DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026). Given these speech embeddings and textual instruction as conditioning context, the model reconstructs the complete ASR transcript through it-erative denoising. We first warm up the projection layer on LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015), then jointly optimize the projection layer and speech encoder on an English ASR mixture drawn primarily from Granary (Rao Koluguri et al., 2025). The diffusion language model remains frozen throughout this stage, requiring the speech frontend to produce representations compatible with its pretrained textual latent space. 

Auxiliary loss. Recent work suggests that an auxiliary CTC objective can improve diffusion-based ASR performance (Khurdula et al., 2026). We did not observe consistent gains in our setting and instead employ an auxiliary AR next-token loss alongside the diffusion objective. The resulting Stage 1 model achieves a word error rate (WER) of 2.3 on LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) test-clean and 4.2 on test-other. 3.2 STAGE 2: S POKEN INSTRUCTION -FOLLOWING 

We sample 600K speech-instruction examples from the Nemotron-Omni SFT corpus (NVIDIA, 2026) and mix them with 5% ASR replay, drawn from 37.5K transcription examples. To strengthen the capability without thinking, 70% of responses directly supervise final answers without explicit 

<think> content. The speech encoder, projection layer, and DLM are jointly optimized using the same diffusion and auxiliary AR objectives as in Stage 1, while the MoE router remains frozen. Joint adaptation exposes an empirical trade-off. With a small learning rate, long-form generation of DLM remains coherent, but the model only weakly grounds its responses in the spoken input. Increasing the learning rate improves speech adaptation but causes the pretrained language distribu-tion to drift, resulting in repetition and incorrect termination. This tension cannot be addressed by setting different learning rates for three modules, or replaying text-only data. 

Text-Anchored distillation. We therefore retain a frozen copy of the pretrained DLM as a tex-tual anchor. For each paired training example, the trainable student receives the spoken request, whereas the frozen teacher receives its written counterpart. Then we minimize forward KL diver-gence between their output distributions for both the diffusion canvas and the causal AR readout. 4Arxiv Preprint This cross-modal supervision effectively transfers the anchor teacher’s textual capabilities to the speech-conditioned student. 3.3 STAGE 3: F ULL -DUPLEX INTERACTION MODELING 

Natural conversation does not arrive as a sequence of pre-segmented turns with explicit boundaries. We therefore learn a streaming interaction policy directly from conversational speech data. The user audio is treated as a continuous stream and divided into 80-ms chunks. At each step, the model autoregressively predicts one interaction state conditioned only on the observed speech prefix and previous states. In Diffuplex, we represent this policy using four states: <listen> , <speak> , <bos> , <eos> .The first two indicate whether the model should listen or retain the conversational floor, whereas 

<bos> and <eos> denote taking and yielding the floor, respectively. These transition events are sparse and therefore receive larger loss weights than the persistent states:

As the model cannot anticipate user’s behavior, the <bos> and <eos> targets include a short reaction delay that allows sufficient acoustic evidence to accumulate. Causality of encoder. Stage 3 reuses speech encoder from Stage 2 but operates under strict causal constraints: a 10-second trailing window without lookahead aligned with streaming inference. To bridge this representation shift, a lightweight LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2021) is introduced, but is not activated when computing diffusion context. This design preserves encoding quality while meeting online constraints, without requiring a separate causal encoder or suffering train-test mismatch. 3.4 INFERENCE AND DENOISE 

When the AR branch emits <bos> , the speech-encoder features accumulated so far are prefilled, together with the text prompt, through the DLM’s causal pass to build its key–value cache, and the response is generated as a sequence of 256-token canvases that attend to this cache. Each denois-ing pass is one bidirectional forward over the canvas. We largely follow the inference recipe of DiffusionGemma (DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026): at every pass its entropy-bounded sampler selects the lowest-entropy positions whose mutual-information bound stays below an entropy bound 

β=0 .1, keeps their sampled tokens for the next pass, and fully re-noises all non-selected positions; the sampling temperature decays linearly across the budget of at most N =48 passes. Denoising terminates early once the highest-probability predictions are identical across two consecutive passes and the average entropy over the canvas falls below a confidence threshold γ. The converged canvas is committed and appended to the cache before the next one is opened. Because the original recipe was designed for offline text generation, we depart from it in two respects that are essential for spo-ken interaction. First, the opening of the response is committed long before the canvas converges, so that speech synthesis can start within a few passes ( anchored prefix ). Second, the denoising schedule is shortened so that fewer passes separate consecutive speech packets ( denoising schedule ). 

Anchored prefix. Under the original decoding scenario, the first word of the answer is final only when the whole canvas has converged, typically after tens of passes. The leading positions, however, settle much earlier. We therefore commit the first n=8 positions of the first canvas after at most K

passes and stream them to the synthesizer while the rest keeps denoising. Position 0 is seeded with the argmax of the causal AR readout at the prefill, which is trained with the auxiliary AR loss and costs nothing extra. Denoising schedule. The pretrained schedule (temperature 0.8 → 0.4, γ=0 .005 ) spends its last passes confirming an already converged canvas. We anneal from 0.7 to 0.2 and relax γ to 0.025 ,and cut any response whose canvas ends in a trailing run of ≥ 32 tokens drawn from at most two distinct tokens, a degenerate loop that a sharper schedule occasionally produces. Committed text is streamed to an interleaved text–speech TTS model (Du et al., 2025), the anchored prefix forming the first packet, so synthesis of the opening overlaps with the denoising of the remainder. The AR interaction policy and diffusion response generator share the same backbone parameters but maintain separate KV caches. The AR cache advances causally with incoming audio to support turn-taking decisions; once the policy emits <bos> , the observed speech context is separately prefilled to initialize the diffusion cache. This keeps the policy’s streaming history distinct from the context used to denoise the response. 3.5 SPEECH SYNTHESIZER 

As the first n tokens are finalized early in the denoising process, Diffuplex is agnostic to the choice of speech synthesizer. These tokens can be immediately passed to any high-quality TTS model while the remaining canvas continues to be refined; consequently, the TTS model itself need not provide native streaming inference. In this work, we adopt CosyVoice 3 (Du et al., 2025) for its synthesis quality and voice-cloning capability. When voice cloning is unnecessary, it can be replaced with a smaller single-speaker TTS model to further reduce first-audio latency. This modular interface allows synthesis quality, speaker flexibility, and latency to be traded off without retraining Diffuplex. 

User interruption. When the AR interaction policy emits <eos> , any remaining response audio is immediately truncated, allowing Diffuplex to yield naturally to the user. If the user interrupts within one second, we discard this unspoken canvas rather than appending it to the KV cache for the next interaction. Consequently, only content that has effectively entered the conversation is retained in the context, preventing frequent interruptions or hesitations from corrupting conversation context.

5.2 RESULTS ON CONVERSATION BEHAVIOR 

We report two variants of our model because we find that adding the Fisher conversational cor-pus (Cieri et al., 2004) into Stage 3 training noticeably changes the learned conversation policy. In-cluding Fisher favors aggressive, low-latency turn transitions; without Fisher, the model focuses on semantically informed turn-taking. The remaining baselines are representative full-duplex spoken dialogue models proposed in recent years. We evaluate all systems on turn-taking, user interruption, and pause handling, where higher TOR and lower latency or pause-handling scores indicate better performance. First-audio latency spans the Stage 3 AR turn decision, response denoising, and TTS. As shown in Table 2, Diffuplex achieves the strongest turn-taking accuracy on both benchmark ver-sions while correctly responding to user interruptions. Diffuplex with Fisher substantially reduces both turn-taking and interruption latency, but this responsiveness comes at the cost of weaker pause handling. A similar pattern appears in Moshi and PersonaPlex, both of which use Fisher-style con-versational data: they enter turns rapidly, yet are more likely to interpret a temporary user pause as the end of a turn. These results expose a fundamental trade-off between aggressive response timing and semantic turn understanding. Training on fast-paced conversational data encourages the model to respond to acoustic silence, whereas excluding such data allows Diffuplex to wait for stronger se-mantic evidence before taking the floor. Our two variants therefore provide distinct operating points between interaction latency and robustness to natural hesitation and disfluency. 5.3 TPF AND TPS A NALYSIS

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Diffuplex, a full-duplex spoken language model that decouples conversational control from semantic generation. Diffuplex utilize AR decoding to continuously determine when to speak ,while a speech-conditioned DLM independently generates what to say through parallel refinement. This separation preserves the linguistic capabilities of a strong text model without binding semantic computation to the physical audio frame rate. Together with anchored-prefix decoding and modular speech synthesis, Diffuplex enables low-latency interaction while retaining strong spoken intelli-gence. Experiments across spoken understanding and full-duplex dialogue benchmarks demonstrate that Diffuplex substantially narrows the capability gap between responsive full-duplex systems and intelligent turn-based models. We hope this two-axis formulation provides a useful foundation for future spoken agents that are both conversationally natural and cognitively capable. 10 Arxiv Preprint

# B MORE DISCUSSION 

Can Stage 3 be trained without the listening-mode LoRA? The interaction head can be trained without the adapter, but the resulting policy mainly learns an acoustic voice-activity detector: it reacts to speech and silence while failing to distinguish a within-turn hesitation from a true turn ending. In particular, it does not handle pauses reliably. The listening-mode LoRA gives the causal speech pathway enough flexibility to use conversational context for turn decisions while the diffusion response pathway remains fixed. 16 Arxiv Preprint 

Why is there no direct multi-token-prediction (MTP) baseline? Our efficiency metric counts finalized answer-text tokens per backbone forward pass. Diffuplex reaches 10.15 on spoken Al-pacaEval, above the fixed proposal horizons of representative text MTP configurations, such as four-token prediction (Gloeckle et al., 2024); this is a comparison with those configurations, not a universal upper bound on MTP. Among the speech-input MTP studies most relevant to our setting, ParaASR applies a six-token proposal to acoustically constrained transcription (Lin et al., 2026b), whereas VocalNet and PRIME-Speech apply MTP to speech or codec tokens rather than paralleliz-ing the answer-text stream (Wang et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2026). Their output units and tasks differ from open-ended spoken question answering, so their reported token rates cannot be placed directly alongside our answer-text TPF. A promising solution is using MTP to replace the early prefix in Diffuplex, which might result in lower first-audio latency. We view it as our future work. 

# C TRAINING DETAILS 

We train the speech-conditioned response model in three phases: Stage 1a, Stage 1b, and Stage 2. The backbone is DiffusionGemma-26B-A4B-it (DiffusionGemma Team et al., 2026), paired with a Parakeet speech encoder (NVIDIA, 2025) and a projection layer that maps speech features into the backbone’s embedding space. These response-model phases use a 256-token diffusion canvas and an auxiliary autoregressive (AR) next-token objective. Audio is converted to 16-kHz mono waveforms before feature extraction. The training seed is 42. C.1 STAGE 1A: P ROJECTOR WARM -UP 

We train the projection layer while freezing the speech encoder and language model. The data com-prise the LibriSpeech (Panayotov et al., 2015) train-clean-100, train-clean-360, and train-other-500 partitions, totaling 281,099 utterances (approximately 960 hours). Targets are lowercase transcripts without punctuation. We train for two epochs, or 1,098 optimizer updates, with global batch size 512. The AR loss has weight 0.1 relative to the diffusion loss. We use AdamW with peak learning rate 3 × 10 −4, 28 warm-up updates, and cosine decay to 3 × 10 −5.C.2 STAGE 1B: S PEECH ENCODER ADAPTATION 

Starting from Stage 1a, we unfreeze the speech encoder and continue training it with the projector while keeping the language model frozen. The data are a 32-source English ASR mixture drawn primarily from Granary (Rao Koluguri et al., 2025), with additional internal ASR and LibriSpeech data. Unlike Stage 1a, the transcription targets retain punctuation and capitalization. We train for 4,032 updates with global batch size 512, corresponding to approximately 2.06 million sampled presentations. The AR loss weight is 0.05. We use AdamW with peak learning rate 2 × 10 −5, 403 warm-up updates, and cosine decay to 10 −5.C.3 STAGE 2: S POKEN INSTRUCTION TUNING 

Starting from the Stage 1b speech frontend, Stage 2 adapts the model to spoken requests. Its training pool contains 600,000 speech-instruction examples selected from Nemotron-Omni SFT (NVIDIA, 2026) and 37,500 ASR replay examples. The two tasks are sampled with probabilities 95% and 5%. We optimize the speech encoder, projector, and diffusion language model, keeping the MoE router frozen. The diffusion and AR loss weights are both 1.0. To retain the pretrained model’s textual capabilities, a frozen copy of the language model receives the written form of each speech-instruction example; forward KL losses between teacher and student are applied to the diffusion canvas and AR readout with weights 3.0 and 1.0, respectively. Stage 2 uses a maximum padded sequence length of 8,192 tokens and global batch size 128. We use AdamW with peak learning rates 10 −5 for the speech frontend and 3 × 10 −6 for the language model, 200 warm-up updates, and cosine decay. The selected joint-training checkpoint is after 2,266 optimizer updates. Stages 1a, 1b, and 2 use 64 GPUs with BF16 computation, FP32 optimizer parameters, and gradient-norm clipping at 1.0. 17 Arxiv Preprint C.4 STAGE 3: F ULL -DUPLEX INTERACTION TRAINING 

Data. The main Stage 3 mixture combines 120 hours of clean synthetic conversations with 70 hours of internal conversational corpus. The clean data provide clearly defined turns and pauses; the internal data add natural hesitations, backchannels, and user interruptions. We also train a variant with Fisher telephone conversations (Cieri et al., 2004) to expose the policy to faster, spontaneous turn exchanges. For the noise-augmented recipe, we add stationary fan and office ambience selected from DNS5 (Dubey et al., 2023) and DEMAND (Thiemann et al., 2013) to the clean synthetic recordings. We also mix quiet pink/white noise and short nonspeech events such as doors, typing, and chair movement. A small fraction of the recordings retains its original silence. 

Optimization. Starting from the Stage 2 checkpoint, we train the four-state interaction head and a listening-mode LoRA adapter (Hu et al., 2021), while keeping the response generator fixed. The policy is trained with cross-entropy on 80-ms state labels, with extra weight on the sparse start- and stop-speaking transitions. We use AdamW with peak learning rate 10 −3, 50 warm-up updates, and cosine decay to 10 −4 over 3,000 updates. Global batch size is 32 on two nodes with eight GPUs each.
