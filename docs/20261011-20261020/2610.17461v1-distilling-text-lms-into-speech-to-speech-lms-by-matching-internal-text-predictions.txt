# ABSTRACT 

Text language models (LMs) acquire broad linguistic and factual capabilities through large-scale pretraining, while comparable capabilities remain difficult to learn from speech alone. Recent work has shown that knowledge distillation from pretrained text LMs offers a promising way to transfer their linguistic and factual capabilities to speech models. However, existing distillation approaches have largely focused on speech-input, text-output models. This idea has yet to be explored for speech-to-speech models, which operate on both speech input and speech output. In this work we introduce a speech-to-speech pretraining method that creates an internal text interface for knowledge transfer while preserving the speech-only input and output. The model first consumes speech context with-out its transcription, switches to text prediction, and then uses the predicted text to lead future speech generation word by word. It also provides an interface for knowledge distillation: the internal text predictions can be directly distilled from the base text LM. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of text LM distillation to a speech-to-speech LM. Under matched training condi-tions, the proposed sequence construction substantially improves the generative perplexity of spoken continuations over speech-only and interleaved speech-text models, approaching the performance of a text-leading inner-monologue model with external ASR prompt transcription. More importantly, distillation improves factual performance under both text and speech evaluation, indicating better re-tention of knowledge from the base text language model.

Interleaved models (Nguyen et al., 2025; Chou et al., 2023) train on sequences that alternate between speech and text spans from the same utterance, typically switching modali-ties at word boundaries, and can perform speech-to-speech inference without any text input. In this setting, text provides an additional training signal, but speech generation does not condition on text, resulting in worse linguistic performance (Chou et al., 2026). Text-leading, or inner-monologue, models (D´ efossez et al., 2024) instead generate text ahead of speech so text predictions directly con-dition subsequent speech generation. In prompted continuation, however, this typically requires the transcript of the speech prompt at inference to maintain the text-leading stream. Table 1 summarizes this tradeoff. This raises a question: how can we use text to guide linguistic prediction in SLMs without requiring prompt text at inference? We introduce Text-Guided Speech LM (TGSLM), which approaches this question by separating fu-ture speech prediction into its linguistic content and its acoustic realization, as shown in the bottom part of Fig. 1. Rather than predicting future speech directly from the speech context, we decompose generation into first predicting the future linguistic content in text and then generating the corre-sponding speech conditioned on the predicted text. During training, we insert a switch token at a randomly selected word boundary in the speech sequence. Before the switch, the model consumes speech directly. After the switch, it generates the continuation word by word, first predicting each text word and then generating its corresponding spoken realization. At inference, the switch token explicitly triggers the transition to text-guided speech generation. This construction captures com-plementary advantages of interleaved and text-leading models: text directly guides future speech generation, while inference requires no transcription of the speech prompt. The TGSLM prediction structure substantially improves generative perplexity (genPPL). TGSLM has a lower genPPL for spoken continuations than speech-only baselines, approaching the perfor-mance of a text-leading model without requiring the prompt transcript at inference. These results suggest that text provides a more effective target for learning the linguistic content of future speech, since the continuation is learned directly in text rather than through the speech prediction objective. The explicit text prediction also provides a direct interface for distillation from the pretrained text LM. We distill the frozen text backbone into TGSLM by minimizing the KL divergence be-tween their next-token distributions at text-prediction positions and refer to the resulting model as TGSLM-distill (combined with text replay, Sec. 3.3). Distillation improves factual performance un-der both text-to-text and speech-to-speech evaluation, showing that capabilities from the pretrained text model can be better maintained during speech adaptation. Our main contributions are: • We study a speech-to-speech pretraining formulation (TGSLM) in which we predict fu-ture text before its corresponding speech, while the input remains speech-only at inference. Under matched training conditions, this formulation substantially improves spoken con-tinuation modeling over speech-only and interleaved baselines and approaches an inner-monologue model with an ASR-transcribed prompt text. • We distill a pretrained text LM into TGSLM’s intermediate text predictions. Distillation improves factual performance in both speech-to-speech and text-to-text evaluation, indi-cating better retention of the pretrained LM’s capabilities during speech adaptation. To the 2best of our knowledge, this is the first application of distillation from text LMs to speech-to-speech LMs. • Additionally, we evaluate sensitivity to paralinguistic cues and find that models with inter-nal text prediction and distillation remain comparably sensitive to emotional prosody when prompted with speech sharing the same linguistic content. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 PURE SPEECH LANGUAGE MODELS 

Pure SLMs learn to model speech without using text as part of the objective. Early work on genera-tive spoken language modeling showed that speech can be modeled autoregressively by discretizing self-supervised speech representations into “semantic” tokens (Lakhotia et al., 2021). 1 TWIST ini-tializes a pure SLM from a pretrained text LM, which improves performance on linguistic evalua-tions(Hassid et al., 2023). SLMs over continuous speech features combine autoregressive generation with a generative head, using conditional flow matching (Chou et al., 2025) or diffusion (Rouard et al., 2026), to jointly learn linguistic and acoustic information. Llama-Mimi flattens semantic to-kens and acoustic tokens (the remaining RVQ codebooks, which capture acoustic detail needed for waveform reconstruction) into a single autoregressive sequence (Sugiura et al., 2025). These ap-proaches provide ways of learning pure speech models, either without text supervision or with text pretraining, but their performance on tasks involving linguistic content remains limited (Chou et al., 2026). This motivates introducing explicit text supervision during speech pretraining. 2.2 SPEECH -T EXT LANGUAGE MODELS 

Speech-text language models directly predict both speech and text sequences. Interleaved SLMs alternate between speech and text spans of the same utterance, typically switching modalities at word boundaries (Chou et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2025). In this formulation, text provides an additional training signal, but is not used explicitly when generating a speech continuation. Another family is inner-monologue modeling, where text prediction precedes and guides speech generation. Spectron follows a speech prompt → prompt transcription → text continuation →

speech continuation structure (Nachmani et al., 2024). Moshi uses a word-aligned inner monologue in which text precedes the corresponding generated audio (D´ efossez et al., 2024). FLM-Audio uses sentence-level monologues that let text run ahead of speech (Yao et al., 2025). Thinker-Talker ar-chitectures such as Qwen2.5-Omni (Xu et al., 2025) modularize a related text-before-speech depen-dency, with a separate Talker generating speech from the Thinker’s text and hidden representations. TGSLM combines complementary aspects of interleaved and inner-monologue models. At word boundaries, rather than switching between speech and text spans, TGSLM transitions to text-guided generation: similar to inner-monologue models, it first predicts the text and then generates the cor-responding speech realization. Moshi (D´ efossez et al., 2024) and FLM-Audio (Yao et al., 2025) use related speech-input, text-guided speech generation structures. However, they use it in dialogue post-training, where it is learned together with conversational behaviors such as turn-taking and full-duplex interaction. We instead use it in speech pretraining, isolating its effect on linguistic modeling from dialogue training. 2.3 KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER FROM TEXT LANGUAGE MODELS 

As text language models acquire broad linguistic and factual capabilities, a natural question is how to transfer these capabilities to speech models. Most prior distillation methods are speech-input, text-output models. BLSP-KD, DiVA, and SALAD follow this general paradigm, using speech-text pairs to align speech-conditioned predictions or representations with their text-conditioned counter-parts (Wang et al., 2024; Held et al., 2025; Cuervo et al., 2026). SALAD further studies forgetting of text capabilities during speech adaptation and combines cross-modal distillation with text replay.

TGSLM instead introduces an explicit text prediction interface within a speech-to-speech model. This allows the pretrained text LM to directly supervise TGSLM’s next-token predictions, while the model still takes speech as input and produces speech as its final output.

3.1 SPEECH REPRESENTATION AND GENERATION OBJECTIVE 

We follow the speech representation and generation objective of Flow-SLM (Chou et al., 2025; 2026). Given an input waveform, the Mimi tokenizer (D´ efossez et al., 2024) produces synchronized discrete and continuous speech representations. Generative perplexity (genPPL). We use genPPL to evaluate the linguistic quality of open-ended speech continuations, following (Chou et al., 2026; Maimon et al., 2025a). Given a speech prompt, each model generates five continuations. We transcribe the generated speech with Whisper 

small.en (Radford et al., 2023) and score the continuation transcript with OLMo-3 7B (OLMo Team, 2025), conditioned on the prompt transcript. Lower genPPL indicates that the generated con-tinuation is more likely under the reference text LM. We score only the continuation tokens so that genPPL reflects continuation quality rather than the likelihood of the given prompt. To identify the continuation in the ASR transcription, we locate the prompt boundary by finding the minimum char-acter edit distance between the ASR transcription and the prompt text, following (Chou et al., 2026). We use the intersection of utterances with non-empty continuations across all compared models, so that every model is evaluated on the same subset. 

Auto-BLEU. Following prior work (Lakhotia et al., 2021; Lin et al., 2025; Maimon et al., 2025a), we additionally report bigram auto-BLEU to measure repetition. Auto-BLEU computes the fraction of generated N-grams ( N = 2 ) that appear more than once within the utterance. This complements generative perplexity, since repetition can result in an artificially low perplexity under a reference language model (Holtzman et al., 2020; Welleck et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2026). 

StoryCloze. We evaluate spoken StoryCloze (Maimon et al., 2025a; Lu et al., 2026; Hassid et al., 2023) by comparing candidate likelihoods, computed over text-token predictions only (Sec. 3.4). 

Factual knowledge. GenPPL averages over all continuation tokens and may not fully reflect whether specific factual knowledge is retained. We evaluate factual knowledge using Known-1000 (Meng et al., 2022), 4 which tests factual completion through open-ended continuation and more closely matches our generation setting than question-answering benchmarks. For each prompt, we generate five spoken continuations and transcribe them with Whisper small.en . Following Spectron (Nachmani et al., 2024), a sample is considered correct if the answer appears in its tran-scription. We count an example as correct under Pass@ K if at least one of the first K generated samples is correct. 

Emotion sensitivity. We evaluate emotion sensitivity on Gemini-Emo using the prompt triplets described in Sec. 4.1. For each generated sample, we apply the emotion2vec-plus-large (Ma et al., 2024) classifier to the generated continuation, with happy, angry, and sad emotion classes. 

Acoustic quality and speaker similarity. We evaluate acoustic quality using UTMOS-v2 (Baba et al., 2024). We measure speaker similarity between the speech prompt and generated continuation using WavLM-large-TDNN, following (He et al., 2024; Le et al., 2023). 5

A natural question is whether distillation reduces sensitivity to paralinguistic information as the model is trained to mimic the behavior of a text LM. We evaluate this using Gemini-Emo together with an emotion classifier, with results shown in Table 4. TGSLM and TGSLM-distill achieve similar emotion classification accuracy, showing limited degradation in emotion sensitivity from distillation in this evaluation. TGSLM-Large-distill performs slightly worse than Llama-Mimi, but remains broadly comparable. Models based on more limited acoustic representations ( External models with limited acoustic in-formation row) show substantially more class-imbalanced emotion predictions, which we attribute to the loss of acoustic information during tokenization, as these models use either only semantic tokens or semantic tokens plus pitch tokens. We view this as a preliminary study of paralinguistic sensitivity rather than a comprehensive eval-uation of the generated continuation, as Gemini-Emo uses a single emotion classifier and primarily probes whether acoustic emotion cues in the prompt are reflected in the generated speech. It does not test whether distillation toward a text-only teacher shifts the lexical content toward neutral content. 

# 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduce TGSLM, a speech-to-speech LM that uses internally predicted text to guide speech generation while maintaining speech-only input and output at inference. The TGSLM internal text construction substantially improves generative perplexity and provides an interface for text-LM dis-tillation, which improves factual knowledge retention in both speech-to-speech and text-to-text eval-uation. An emotion sensitivity evaluation suggests that distillation has limited impact on this aspect of generation. TGSLM currently relies on word-level speech-text alignments to construct the training sequences. We have not studied whether this alignment requirement can be eliminated. One possible direc-tion is to predict text and speech at the sentence level, which could provide text guidance without fine-grained alignment. We also focus on pretraining in this work and leave post-training, such as instruction tuning or dialogue adaptation, for future work. 9AI USE STATEMENT

A.1 ALIGNMENT FILTERING RULES 

First, we require the final aligned word to reach at least 80% of the audio duration. Second, we require that no more than 5% of aligned words have effectively zero duration. We exclude utterances whose transcripts exceed 7.5 Qwen3-0.6B text tokens per second. A.2 LIKELIHOOD MEASURE 

For likelihood-based evaluation, we consume the given text and speech continuation using the same alternating procedure and accumulate likelihood only at text-token positions. Because the text-speech alignment is reconstructed online, the strict top-1 boundary criterion can occasionally fail to trigger enough speech boundaries, leaving some text tokens unscored. Only in this case, we use a fallback boundary criterion and accept <s-eow> whenever it appears among the top-3 predic-tions of the first discrete speech-token head. This fallback is used only for likelihood-based scoring (StoryCloze) and is not applied during generation. A.3 VAD PROTOCOL FOR KNOWN -1000 We observe that abruptly ending a synthesized waveform can introduce tokenizer artifacts in the final speech frames, creating an artificial discontinuity at the end of the input. To reduce this effect, we first trim trailing silence using voice activity detection (VAD) (Hwang et al., 2023) and then pad each waveform so that its duration is a multiple of the tokenizer frame duration. We use a frame duration of 80 ms for Mimi and 40 ms for HuBERT-based (Hsu et al., 2021) representations (Maimon et al., 2025a; Nguyen et al., 2025). A.4 GEMINI -E MO PROMPTS 

For Gemini-Emo, we first use Gemini-3.7-Flash to generate triplets of sentences expressing happy, sad, and angry emotions while sharing the same linguistically neutral prefix. Table 5 shows an example. We construct the shared prefix so that its text alone does not indicate the target emotion. We then use Gemini-3.1-Flash-TTS-Preview to synthesize each sentence with the corresponding emotional prosody. Detailed prompts are provided in Table 6. We obtain word-level speech-text alignments using the same Whisper-based alignment method (Yeh et al., 2025) and extract the prefix as the speech prompt for continuation generation. We classify the full synthesized utterances with emotion2vec-plus-large (Ma et al., 2024), restricting the prediction to the happy, sad, and angry classes, and retain only triplets in which all three utterances are classified as their intended emotions.
