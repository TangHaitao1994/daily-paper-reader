## Abstract 

Full-duplex spoken language models (FD-SLMs) support simultaneous listening and speaking, but often lag behind half-duplex spo-ken language models (HD-SLMs) in general capabilities. We propose Dialogue-to-Duplex (D2) , a framework that converts pretrained HD-SLMs into high-capability FD-SLMs at fine temporal granularity. D2 adapts the original audio encoder to streaming through decoder-free representation distillation and learns full-duplex interaction through supervised fine-tuning. We systematically study the effects of chunk size on full-duplex interaction, general capabilities, and inference computation. We apply D2 to two distinct HD-SLM architec-tures, Qwen3-Omni and LLaMA-Omni 2. At a 12.5 Hz update rate, Qwen3-Omni-D2 achieves 99.2% turn-taking success with 521-ms latency, 99.5% interruption success, and a state-of-the-art VoiceBench score of 70.1. 

## 1 Introduction 

People continuously listen while speaking, in-terrupt each other, and respond without explicit turn boundaries (Sacks et al., 1974; Levinson and Torreira, 2015). Modeling these dynamics re-mains a central challenge for spoken dialogue sys-tems (Skantze, 2021). Full-duplex spoken language models (FD-SLMs), including Moshi (Défossez et al., 2024) and MiniCPM-o 4.5 (Cui et al., 2026), can perceive user audio while generating speech, supporting interruptions, overlapping speech, and low-latency turn taking. Despite these interaction capabilities, native FD-SLMs often lag behind strong half-duplex spoken language models (HD-SLMs) in audio understand-ing, knowledge, reasoning, and instruction follow-ing (Chen et al., 2026). HD-SLMs such as Qwen3-Omni (Xu et al., 2025) and Kimi-Audio (Ding et al.,

In summary, our contributions are as follows:

Decoder-free streaming adaptation. We reduce encoder latency through representation distillation without requiring the original training decoder or transcription labels. 

Chunk-size trade-offs. We systematically study the effects of chunk size on full-duplex interaction, general capabilities, and inference computation. 

## 2 Related Work 

2.1 Half-Duplex Spoken Language Models 

HD-SLMs combine speech understanding and generation with a language-model backbone. Earlier systems include SpeechGPT and Au-dioPaLM (Zhang et al., 2023; Rubenstein et al., 2023), while VITA and Mini-Omni target low-latency or streaming speech interaction (Fu et al., 2024; Xie and Wu, 2024). Recent models such as GLM-4-Voice, LLaMA-Omni 2, Kimi-Audio, Qwen3-Omni, and Qwen3.5-Omni provide capable pretrained backbones for conversion to full-duplex operation (Zeng et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2025b; Ding et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Qwen, 2026). 

2.2 Full-Duplex Spoken Language Models 

Systems such as LSLM, Freeze-Omni, Moshi, and PersonaPlex support simultaneous listening and speaking, including interruption handling and over-lapping speech (Ma et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024a; Défossez et al., 2024; Roy et al., 2026). MiniCPM-o 4.5 combines full-duplex interaction with stronger general capabilities, but uses a 1-s chunk duration and degrades substantially when this duration is reduced to 0.1 s (Cui et al., 2026). This motivates conversion methods that retain strong capabilities at smaller chunk durations. 

2.3 Conversion Strategy 

OmniFlatten converts a pretrained text LLM into a spoken-dialogue model through speech-modality alignment followed by full-duplex dialogue train-ing (Zhang et al., 2025). D2 starts from an HD-SLM that already possesses speech understanding and generation capabilities. BayLing-Duplex (Fang et al., 2026) similarly converts an HD-SLM, GLM-4-Voice, to full-duplex operation. It retains a speech tokenizer requiring input blocks of up to 4 s and uses a large 800-ms chunk duration, resulting in laten-cies of several seconds. It also uses an addi-tional DPO stage to stabilize turn-taking (Rafailov et al., 2023). D2 adapts the original audio encoder to low-latency streaming through distillation and learns full-duplex interaction through supervised fine-tuning, supporting chunk durations as short as 80–100 ms.

3.2 Dialogue-to-Duplex (D2) 

The formulations above show that HD and FD mod-els share a similar generation objective; they dif-fer in the temporal granularity of the interaction sequence. An HD-SLM operates over variable-length, turn-defined units, whereas an FD-SLM operates over smaller chunks of duration k∆.This suggests viewing full-duplex interaction as ordinary dialogue conducted at a finer temporal granularity. As k decreases, the model observes and responds to the user more frequently, enabling increasingly fine-grained real-time interaction. D2 converts pretrained HD-SLMs into FD-SLMs. Finer interaction granularity introduces two challenges. First, the audio encoder must operate with limited future context. Second, the model must learn to make conversational decisions at a finer temporal scale. D2 addresses these challenges through streaming encoder distillation followed by D2 supervised fine-tuning .

3.3 Stage I: Encoder Distillation 

Audio encoders in pretrained HD-SLMs are typ-ically non-streaming or blockwise streaming and rely on future acoustic context, making them unsuit-able for low-latency full-duplex inference. Chunk-wise attention and memory-based streaming en-coders have been widely studied for low-latency speech recognition (Shi et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2021). However, directly replacing the encoder with a streaming encoder may introduce a repre-sentation mismatch with the pretrained language model. We instead convert the original encoder into a streaming student and distill it from the unchanged offline encoder (Hinton et al., 2015; Chang et al., 2022). For each newly available chunk of dura-tion k∆, the student recomputes the convolutional frontend using only the audio available up to the current chunk, while bidirectional self-attention in the Transformer is replaced with block-causal attention. Attention remains bidirectional within each chunk, but is causal across chunks. ## 4 Experimental Setup 

We describe the HD-SLM backbones, datasets, training procedure, and evaluation benchmarks used for D2. 

4.1 Half-Duplex Models 

We apply D2 to Qwen3-Omni and LLaMA-Omni 2 (Xu et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025b), whose streaming speech decoders support low-latency out-put. The temporal resolution of their audio repre-sentations determines ∆ used in D2. 

Qwen3-Omni. Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B uses a Mixture-of-Experts Thinker–Talker architecture with an Audio Transformer (AuT) encoder, a vi-sion encoder, and a streaming codec decoder. D2 uses only its audio input pathway. The AuT convolutional frontend downsamples the input to 12.5 Hz ( ∆ = 80 ms). Its Transformer attends 4bidirectionally within audio blocks; Stage I adapts this encoder to streaming. The Thinker gener-ates text, while the Talker produces 16-codebook speech frames conditioned on text embeddings and Thinker hidden states. Code2Wav incrementally converts these frames into waveforms. 

LLaMA-Omni 2. We use the bilingual 7B vari-ant, built on Qwen2.5. Its Whisper-large-v3 en-coder feeds a speech adapter that concatenates five consecutive encoder frames and projects them into the language model’s embedding space. The re-sulting representations have a rate of 10 Hz ( ∆ = 100 ms). A separate autoregressive TTS model generates speech conditioned on text-token embed-dings and language-model hidden states, support-ing streaming speech output. 

4.2 Datasets Stage I: Encoder distillation. We use the Lib-riSpeech train-clean-100 and train-clean-360 splits (Panayotov et al., 2015) as speech data, together with the audio tracks from AVQA (Yang et al., 2022) as general audio data. AVQA provides non-speech events and environmental sounds, such as rainfall and traffic. 

Stage II: D2 training. Figure 3 illustrates how we construct full-duplex training sequences from the turn-based conversations in InstructS2S-200K (Fang et al., 2025a) and SODA (Kim et al., 2023). We also include AVQA to preserve gen-eral audio understanding. We resynthesize model responses using Kokoro TTS (Hexgrad, 2025) to provide a consistent output voice. To construct each training sequence, we con-catenate complete user–model turn pairs in their original order until the sequence spans at least 720 s of audio. At each transition to the next user turn, we sample one of three transition types: in-terruption with 50% probability, direct concate-nation with 30%, or a silent gap with 20%. An 

INTERRUPT target placed after user-speech onset teaches the model to stop its current response, while a RESPONSE target placed after user-speech offset indicates that a new response should begin. An audio EOS target terminates model speech either at its natural end or upon interruption. 4.3 Implementation Details Stage I: Encoder distillation. We train each streaming audio encoder for 30,000 updates with a batch size of four, corresponding to approximately 338 hours of LibriSpeech and 66.4 hours of AVQA. We use constant learning rates of 5 × 10 −5 for the convolutional frontend and 2 × 10 −6 for the fully fine-tuned attention layers. The remaining encoder components use LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) with a learning rate of 1 × 10 −4.

Stage II: D2 training. We train each D2 model for 10,000 updates with a batch size of four, corresponding to approximately 380 hours of InstructS2S-200K, 200 hours of SODA, and 100 hours of AVQA. We use Transformer-XL-style training (Dai et al., 2019) with 90-s training seg-ments and a detached 90-s KV cache carried over from the preceding segment. Text cross-entropy weights are 1 for lexical tokens, 0.05 for PAD , and 20 for RESPONSE and 

INTERRUPT . Ordinary speech-code targets have weight 1, while audio EOS has weight 20 for Qwen3-Omni and 3 for LLaMA-Omni 2. Train-able modules, LoRA configurations, learning rates, schedules, and hardware are detailed in Ap-pendix C.

## 7 Conclusion 

We proposed Dialogue-to-Duplex (D2) , a frame-work for converting HD-SLMs into FD-SLMs through streaming encoder distillation and full-duplex supervised fine-tuning, using several hun-dred hours of training data per stage. D2 enables low-latency streaming and strong full-duplex inter-action while retaining substantial general capabili-ties of the original HD-SLM. Across two different HD-SLM architectures, D2 achieves strong results on full-duplex interaction benchmarks and state-of-the-art VoiceBench performance among evaluated full-duplex models, narrowing the capability gap between FD-SLMs and HD-SLMs. 

## Limitations 

First, the speech synthesis module contributes a large portion of the end-to-end latency in D2. Re-ducing speech-generation latency often leads to degraded speech quality or unstable generation, which restricts the choice of HD-SLM backbones to models with sufficiently low-latency speech syn-thesis. Second, we construct full-duplex training data from turn-based conversations because high-quality publicly available full-duplex dialogue data is currently limited. Finally, pause handling re-mains limited, particularly for Qwen3-Omni-D2 at small chunk durations. 8Ethical Considerations 

Full-duplex spoken language models raise privacy and consent concerns because they continuously process incoming audio, including while gener-ating speech. Their natural, low-latency inter-actions may also facilitate impersonation, social engineering, and deceptive conversations. Fur-thermore, D2 may inherit biases and harmful behaviors from pretrained models. Responsible deployment should therefore consider informed user consent, transparency regarding AI-generated speech, privacy-preserving audio processing, and safeguards against misuse.
