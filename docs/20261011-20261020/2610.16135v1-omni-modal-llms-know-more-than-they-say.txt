ABSTRACT
Omni-modal large language models (Omni-LLMs) extend LLMs to process het-
erogeneous modalities (e.g., vision, audio, and IMU signals) alongside language,
enabling a broader range of multimodal tasks. Yet we find that these models of-
ten fail to express information from non-textual modalities even when they per-
ceive it correctly. We term this disconnect knows-but-cannot-say. To investi-
gate this phenomenon, we introduce Token-level Modality Alignment (TMA),
which measures the alignment between individual modality representations and
response representations. Our analysis shows that non-textual representations are
less aligned with response tokens and receive less attention than textual repre-
sentations, suggesting a potential explanation for uneven modality preferences.
Motivated by these findings, we propose OMNI-CATALYST, a simple perceive →
infer strategy that complements the original modality inputs with self-generated
text, making non-textual knowledge more accessible. Across seven Omni-LLMs,
OMNI-CATALYST consistently improves performance (+7.29%) and yields more
balanced modality preferences, with further gains from diverse training strate-
gies. Further analysis demonstrates that the knowledge extraction phases increase
TMA, suggesting that improved alignment makes non-textual information more
accessible during response generation. In summary, our findings uncover how
Omni-LLMs can better express modality knowledge through language. Our
code is available at https://github.com/naver-ai/OmniLLM.

INTRODUCTION
Omni-modal large-language models (Omni-LLMs) (Han et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024; Xie &
Wu, 2024; Hurst et al., 2024; ChameleonTeam, 2024; Zhan et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025; Li et al.,
2025b; Inclusion et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025c; Liu et al., 2025b; Chen et al., 2025a; Xu et al., 2025a;
QwenTeam, 2026; LongcatTeam et al., 2026; GemmaTeam et al., 2026; NAVERTeam, 2026; Luo
et al., 2026a; Nguyen et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026) integrate diverse sensory modalities within a sin-
gle language-centric reasoning system1. Their design broadly resembles the organization of human
perception, in which specialized pathways process distinct sensory streams before integrating them
for higher-level cognition (McClure, 1916; Stokes, 2021; Beck, 2025). Omni-LLMs encode in-
puts such as vision and audio through modality-specific encoders, then project these representations

into a shared space for the underlying language model to generate a response. While this unified
architecture enables richer interaction across sensory channels, Omni-LLMs often still underper-
form modality-specialized counterparts on the very tasks those models are designed for (Zhu et al.,
2025). This raises a fundamental question: why do Omni-LLMs fail to fully benefit from the addi-
tional modalities available to them? Limited training data and suboptimal training strategies may
partly explain this gap. However, another possible explanation may be that the limitation lies not
only in perception itself, but also in how effectively Omni-LLMs can access perceived information
during the subsequent generation process.
In this work, we investigate the gap between what Omni-LLMs perceive and what they use to gen-
erate an answer. Figure 1 presents a motivating illustration of this discrepancy and how it evolves
as the number of input modalities increases. As shown in Figure 1a, the Omni-LLM can correctly
extract relevant modality information but fails to use it when directly answering the multimodal rea-
soning question. Remarkably, once the same modality-specific information is explicitly elicited in
textual form, the model recovers the correct answer. This suggests a knows-but-cannot-say prob-
lem (Orgad et al., 2025; Lu et al., 2026): the model knows the relevant information at the perception
level, but that knowledge fails to reach the generation path unless it is re-expressed in text. Cru-
cially, this problem intensifies as additional modalities are introduced (see Figure 1b), precisely the
regime Omni-LLMs are built for. Downstream accuracy collapses with each added modality while
perception accuracy holds, isolating the bottleneck in read-out rather than encoding.
Our study investigates the hidden modality knowledge problem through cross-modality represen-
tational alignment. Although Omni-LLMs are extensively trained to align heterogeneous modality
representations (Liang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Chowdhury et al., 2026; Li
et al., 2026), irreducible misalignment persists (Liang et al., 2022; F¨urst et al., 2022; Yaras et al.,
2024; Fonseca et al., 2025; Li et al., 2025a; Grassucci et al., 2026; Schrodi et al., 2025; Eslami &
de Melo, 2025; Yan et al., 2026a; Gr¨oger et al., 2026; Chowers et al., 2026). To effectively charac-
terize this hidden gap, we introduce Token-level Modality Alignment (TMA), which complements
existing global and local alignment measures by quantifying how input modality information inter-
acts with language generation at a token-level. In §3, we find that TMA effectively explains why
Omni-LLMs cannot fully access non-text modality representations. Notably, modality tokens with
higher TMA show greater sensitivity in downstream performance, but not for knowledge extraction,
and this effect is exacerbated with more modalities.
Such representational mismatch induces prevailing modality imbalance issue, in which Omni-LLMs
rely on modalities that are easier to access rather than those most relevant to the query (Chen et al.,
2025b; Wang et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026b; Zheng et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025c; Huang et al.,
2022; Tjandrasuwita et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2026; Reilly et al., 2026). To address this issue, we
explore in §4 a simple strategy that renders modality-specific information into texts before answer
generation. We propose OMNI-CATALYST for both training-free and trainable settings and show that
complementary, self-generated modality descriptions substantially improve omni-modal reasoning
performance (from §5.1 to §6.2). Importantly, while OMNI-CATALYST is particularly beneficial for
complementing weaker modalities in multimodal inputs (e.g., audio given vision-audio), it also gen-
eralizes to single-modality settings, consistently improving performance across diverse modalities
such as fMRI, IMU, and point clouds. The self-generated descriptions preserve modality-specific
semantics while residing in the same representational space as the subsequent answering tokens,
thereby bridging heterogeneous modality inputs and language generation. Overall, our results sug-
gest that how much Omni-LLMs can make encoded information usable during generation matters.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
• Diagnosing omni-modal representations: We trace modality imbalance in Omni-LLMs through
representational misalignment between modality-specific inputs and generation representations,
using our proposed Token-level Modality Alignment (TMA) metric.
• Extracting modality knowledge into the generation space: We propose OMNI-CATALYST,
which self-generates descriptions for weaker modalities, steering Omni-LLMs toward the correct
answer with their representations acting as an effective semantic and modality intermediate.
• Attaining modality-balanced gains: Across diverse Omni-LLMs and benchmarks, OMNI-
CATALYST improves omni-modal reasoning by 7.29% and increases TMA and attention in the
knowledge-extraction phase with enhanced modality balance.

BACKGROUND
Omni-LLMs. Omni-LLMs integrate heterogeneous modalities within a unified architecture to sup-
port multimodal understanding and reasoning (Zhu et al., 2025; Koutoupis et al., 2026; Cicchetti
et al., 2026). Early efforts such as VAST (Chen et al., 2023) established large-scale vision-audio-
subtitle-text pretraining, while One-LLM (Han et al., 2024) progressively incorporated additional
modalities through mixture-of-experts projection. Subsequent studies show that modality-specific
or joint omni-modal fine-tuning alone does not guarantee genuine omni-modal capability (Zhu et al.,
2025), and that bimodal supervision provides limited transfer to omni-modal reasoning (Liu et al.,
2026). NExT-OMNI (Luo et al., 2026b) extends the setting to any-to-any generation using discrete
flow matching, More recently, inference-time methods have sought to improve modality use without
additional training: OmniXR (Chen et al., 2025b) explicitly extracts modality information before
reasoning and replaces the modalities, ThinkOmni (Guan et al., 2026) delegates reasoning to exter-
nal text-only models (Yang et al., 2025a; Guo et al., 2025), and CoM (Luo et al., 2026c) decomposes
inference into planner, reasoner, and decider roles. While these approaches improve omni-modal in-
ference through architectural, training, or prompting strategies, our work instead investigates why
non-textual information can remain underutilized despite being encoded by the model.
Knowing without saying. A growing body of work reveals a gap between information encoded
internally and information expressed in model outputs. In language models, intermediate activa-
tions can decode answer-related information even when greedy decoding produces an incorrect
answer (Orgad et al., 2025). Similar discrepancies arise in vision-language models: models may
attend to correct visual evidence despite answering incorrectly (Liu et al., 2025a), encode spatial in-
formation that is linearly decodable but not reflected in predictions (Liao et al., 2026), or internally
capture negation and spatial/multilingual information that does not reliably reach the output (Lu
et al., 2026; An et al., 2026). Zhang et al. (2026) further shows that useful latent information can
be silenced without influencing predictions. Complementarily, Gou et al. (2026) decouple percep-
tion from reasoning using an external captioner, demonstrating that richer visual descriptions can
improve reasoning. These findings motivate our central question: whether Omni-LLMs similarly
know information in non-textual representations but fail to effectively use it during generation.
Modality imbalance. Omni-LLMs are designed to integrate heterogeneous modalities, yet their
representations and inference behavior need not be balanced. Representationally, modality-specific
embeddings can remain misaligned despite being mapped into a shared latent space, a phenomenon
known as the modality gap. Prior work addresses this gap through alignment and uniformity objec-
tives (U+A) (Wang & Isola, 2020), post-hoc embedding shifts (MG) (Liang et al., 2022), and rep-
resentation standardization (I0T) (An et al., 2025). However, reducing representational gaps alone
does not necessarily improve downstream performance. Behaviorally, Yan et al. (2026b) show that
Omni-LLMs may strongly favor visual information even when audio, image, and text are equally
available, while HumanOmni-Speaker (Bai et al., 2026) finds reliance on static visual cues over
audio-visual alignment in multi-speaker settings. Together, these results highlight that represen-
tational alignment and modality utilization are distinct; our work connects the two by measuring
token-level modality alignment and examining how it relates to downstream use.
3
TOKEN-LEVEL MODALITY ALIGNMENT AND GENERATION
ACCESSIBILITY
In this section, we investigate how Omni-LLMs access encoded modality information during re-
sponse generation.
Two considerations guide our analysis.
First, useful modality information
must be accessible during generation, so alignment among input representations alone is insuffi-
cient. Second, answering a query requires selectively using relevant tokens; high average alignment
only across samples, at a dataset-level, may obscure whether those tokens are accessible. Existing
encoder-level multimodal model measures primarily characterize alignment between modalities in
a shared latent space (Liang et al., 2022; Huh et al., 2024). However, such encoder-level align-
ment may not directly apply to generative Omni-LLMs, whose ultimate responses come from the
decoder’s internal representations and support more than two modalities. We therefore introduce
a token-level diagnostic metric that measures modality alignment between arbitrary input modal-
ity representations and the representations underlying response generation, and examine what this
alignment implies about how modality knowledge is accessed.

3.1
MODALITY REPRESENTATIONAL MISALIGNMENT
Evaluation of Omni-LLM modality representations. We first highlight the limitations of ex-
isting metrics.
Representative modality alignment (or gap) metrics, such as centroid distance
(CD) (Liang et al., 2022) and CKNNA (Huh et al., 2024) (Figure 2), are developed for dual en-
coders, where 1) each modality sample yields a full embedding; 2) paired embeddings (e.g., an
image and its caption) are trained to be semantically aligned. That means, each modality spans
numerous tokens (often thousands for video); hence, a per-sample embedding may blur which
tokens are task-relevant.
Second, input modalities are complementary rather than semantically
similar: an audio track is not a paraphrase of the video, nor is the text query. While Qin et al.
(2025) emphasize alignment to the decoder, they aggregate the final alignment score across tokens.

METHOD: OMNI-CATALYST
From TMA diagnosis to methodology design. Our analysis in §3 showed that Omni-LLMs strug-
gle to use retained modality information during generation, especially as the number of modalities
increases. TMA characterizes this accessibility at the token level, highlighting the importance of
selectively accessing modality information relevant to the response. Since text tokens align more
closely with response representations, OMNI-CATALYST complements sensory inputs with self-
generated textual descriptions. These descriptions express modality knowledge in the model’s na-
tive generation space while preserving the original sensory representations, providing an additional
pathway for relevant information to inform the response.
Preliminary. Conventional generative Omni-LLMs (Yang et al., 2024; GemmaTeam et al., 2026)
learn a function f(X,q) →y, where X = {Xm}m∈M denotes modality inputs, q the query, and y =
(y1,...,yT ) the response. Each modality-specific encoder maps its input to a latent representation,
which is then processed by a shared language model: hm = Em(Xm),
y = fLLM({hm}m∈M,q),
where Em and hm denote the encoder and latent representation for modality m.
4.1
SELF-GENERATING MODALITY DESCRIPTIONS
We introduce a simple training-free formulation of OMNI-CATALYST that complements each non-
textual representation with a self-generated modality description: dm = fLLM(Xm;pdesc),
y =
fLLM({hm,dm}m∈M,q;pans), where pdesc and pans denote the prompts for description and answer
generation, respectively. Both stages use the same underlying LLM. Crucially, the generated de-
scription does not replace hm; rather, it provides an additional textual pathway derived from the
same modality information. Thus, the description need not exhaustively reconstruct the original
input, but should preserve task-relevant information in a form that is more directly compatible with
the LLM’s language-based processing. From the perspective of TMA, this intervention exposes
modality information through a representation that is more compatible with subsequent language
generation, while preserving the original sensory representation.
Generating descriptions in a separate pass incurs additional inference cost. We therefore also con-
sider a single-pass procedure in which the modality descriptions and the final response are generated
jointly: (d,y) = f(X,q;pdesc ○pans), where d = {dm}m∈M, ○denotes prompt concatenation, and
both d and y are generated conditioned on the same modality inputs and query. Thus, the two vari-
ants of OMNI-CATALYST only differ in whether modality descriptions are generated separately or
jointly with the final response. Most existing Omni-modal models employ LLMs as their foundation
models and therefore naturally support text generation, which our method relies on. Models based
on alternative foundation architectures (Xie et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2026b) could
likewise adapt our method by generating modality “descriptions” in a form compatible with their
respective generation processes.

6.2
DOES BRIDGING THE GAP IMPROVE REASONING AND MODALITY BALANCE?
We first examine whether making modality-specific information accessible during generation im-
proves omni-modal reasoning and modality balance. In Figure 9, the pre-trained Omni-LLM (Base-
line) achieves low accuracy (35%) with an unbalanced modality preference, while existing modality-
gap mitigation methods, MG (Liang et al., 2022) and I0T (An et al., 2025), leave both performance
and modality preference largely unchanged. This suggests that reducing representational dispar-
ity alone does not necessarily make heterogeneous modality information equally accessible during
generation. As an oracle, ground-truth audio descriptions (GA) move substantially toward higher
performance and more balanced preference, yielding a 13% performance gain. OMNI-CATALYST
follows the same direction: its single-pass (SA, SV, SE) and two-pass (TA, TV, TE) variants gen-
erally improve both reasoning performance and preference balance. The effect is particularly pro-
nounced for audio descriptions (SA/TA), which shift modality preference toward a more balanced
distribution (+10%) while improving accuracy by up to 8% (for SA).
These gains depend on preserving the original modality information rather than replacing it with
text, since masking the original non-textual modalities with their descriptions (MA, MV, ME) (Chen
et al., 2025b) degrades performance. Meanwhile, Self-CoT, which gives the model a second chance
to self-correct the initial answer, yields only marginal improvements in performance (+3%) and
preference balance (+1%), suggesting that OMNI-CATALYST’s gains are not simply due to additional
inference. Taken together, OMNI-CATALYST helps Omni-LLMs better express information from
heterogeneous modalities through language.
7
CONCLUSION
Omni-LLMs can know more than they say. We find that non-textual modalities can encode use-
ful information that models fail to use effectively when generating answers. TMA makes this gap
visible at the token-level: non-textual representations are less aligned with generated response to-
kens and receive substantially less attention. This suggests that modality imbalance is not neces-
sarily a problem of missing knowledge, but of making that knowledge accessible to the language
space used for answer generation. OMNI-CATALYST addresses this gap with a simple intervention
by self-generating textual descriptions of non-textual inputs. These descriptions increase modality
alignment and make information already present in the inputs more available during answer gener-
ation. Across models and benchmarks, audio descriptions yield particularly consistent gains in both
performance and modality balance. We can automate the procedure in a learning process, where
perceive-only training produces the strongest descriptions and performance among the strategies we
evaluate.
Limitations and future work. Our experiments cover only the modalities supported by current
Omni-LLMs. Nevertheless, TMA and OMNI-CATALYST are modality-agnostic and can extend to
emerging modalities such as tactile (Tu et al., 2026), visual odometry (Hilscher et al., 2026), and
olfactory (Kim et al., 2026). Our study also leaves the root causes of modality imbalance unre-
9


solved. Possible sources include the LLM-centric architecture, multimodal pretraining data, and
the difficulty of aligning heterogeneous representations. More broadly, our findings suggest that
progress toward omni-modal intelligence depends on how effectively models use information across
modalities, beyond how many modalities they support.

A
AI USE STATEMENT
We used AI tools to edit and style the manuscript, identify relevant literature, check the bibliography
for potential hallucinations or inaccuracies, assist with writing proofs in the supplementary materi-
als, reconstruct the single-modality-to-text evaluation dataset, and edit software code. The authors
developed and verified all research ideas, conceptual development, methodological decisions, exper-
imental design, analyses, and conclusions without AI assistance. The authors reviewed and verified
all AI-assisted text, proofs, and code and take full responsibility for the final content of this work.
B
ETHICS STATEMENT
By simply encouraging Omni-LLMs to describe non-textual modality inputs, our approach improves
the balance of modality usage and mitigates over-reliance on a single dominant modality. We do
not anticipate direct negative societal impacts beyond those inherent to the underlying pretrained
models, whose biases our method neither introduces nor fully removes.
C
REPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT
We provide sufficient details to reproduce our experiments, including model configurations, datasets,
evaluation protocols, implementation details, and training settings. We describe all prompts, hyper-
parameters, and experimental procedures in the paper and supplementary materials. We use publicly
available datasets and pretrained models, and provide the code and configuration files necessary to
reproduce our main results. For trained variants, we additionally report the training data, objectives,
and optimization settings. We will release the code and other necessary resources upon publication.

Because these datasets do not follow the standard Omni-LLM benchmark format, we reconstruct
them into a common reasoning-oriented multiple-choice format. Table S1 summarizes the resulting
task construction, including the input modality, source annotation, and target category. For each

source, action, or object type). The category is not required verbatim in the source text, so the
mapping requires interpretation beyond surface-level vocabulary matching. We select the highest-
scoring category as the correct answer, sample three non-matching categories as distractors, and
discard instances when we cannot obtain sufficiently distinct distractors. Each resulting example
therefore contains four text options, with correctness determined by exact option matching.

Training details. For trained variants of OMNI-CATALYST, we implement LoRA (Hu et al., 2021)
with rank 16 and α = 32 and AdamW (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2019) using weight decay 0.01 and
ε = 10−7. We use a learning rate of 10−6 for Gemma and 3 × 10−6 for Qwen. STaR-based train-
ing (Zelikman et al., 2022) runs for four outer iterations with two epochs each and early stopping
with patience 5 based on self-generated validation accuracy. Descriptions are greedily decoded with
up to 48 new tokens, and images are capped at 100,352 pixels. We use seven VALID shards for
training and two for validation, with at most four images and audio clips per sample and 2k MCQs
per iteration. We evaluate 100 validation examples every 200 optimizer steps and train on two
A100-SXM-80GB GPUs.
Self-training objectives. Our training variants use self-generated modality descriptions as super-
vision rather than relying on human-annotated reasoning traces. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022),
R3V (Cheng et al., 2025), and SD-Zero (He et al., 2026) differ in how they generate, refine, filter,
and learn from self-generated supervision. We adapt these strategies to generate descriptions of non-
textual inputs, thereby providing a textual representation of modality-specific information that can
be used during generation. Commonly, the model’s own binary reward r(ˆd) = 1[arg maxy′ pθ(y′ ∣
X, ˆd,q) = y] determines whether a generated description is useful for learning. Each outer iteration
is initialized from the original checkpoint, following Zelikman et al. (2022).
Distinctively, STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) iteratively generates and filters rationales before
fine-tuning on successful reasoning trajectories, while subsequent multimodal extensions such as
R3V (Cheng et al., 2025) and SD-Zero (He et al., 2026) use self-generated supervision to improve
reasoning without gold traces. The detailed objectives for the three self-training strategies are given

Figures S16 and S17 present additional successful and unsuccessful examples of training-free
OMNI-CATALYST, together with the corresponding decoded TMA. In each panel, we highlight the
image and audio tokens with the highest TMA values in red and the lowest in green, using bounding
boxes on video frames and vertical markers on audio waveforms. We also report their TMA values
and the response tokens closest to them in embedding space. In the successful examples (Fig-
ure S16), the lowest-TMA tokens consistently correspond to background image regions or silent
audio segments, whereas the highest-TMA tokens correspond to visual regions or speech segments
relevant to the correct answer. These include the newspaper and crossword-related speech in the first
example, and the pedestrian walking behind the others and the dialogue identifying the target in the
second. This spatial and temporal alignment suggests that token-level TMA reflects the relevance
of the input evidence to the generated response. In the unsuccessful examples (Figure S17), this
correspondence is less apparent. When a question requires reasoning beyond directly observable
evidence, such as inferring off-screen sounds, intentions, or future events, the highest-TMA token
