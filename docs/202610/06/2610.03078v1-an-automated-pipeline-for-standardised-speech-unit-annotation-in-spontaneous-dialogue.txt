Abstract
Quantifying conversational dynamics requires reliable iden-
tification of interactional units and their temporal boundaries, but
speech activity alone does not distinguish conversational turns
from listener feedback or within-turn pauses.
We present an
automated pipeline for extracting turns and backchannels from
separate-channel recordings of spontaneous dyadic conversation,
designed to provide a consistent first-pass annotation for subse-
quent human review. The pipeline combines voice activity de-
tection, channel-energy filtering, temporal merging, automatic
speech recognition, and context-based post-processing. We eval-
uated the pipeline on 99 ten-minute Danish conversations from
33 dyads using segment-level detection reliability and temporal
boundary error. Conversations were recorded under both normal
and asymmetric listening conditions. In the latter, speech-shaped
noise was delivered to one participant through bone-conduction
headphones. Overall detection reliability was F1 = 0.621, with
similar performance for turns (F1 = 0.624) and backchannels
(F1 = 0.618). For successfully matched segments, median ab-
solute onset and offset errors were 0.150 and 0.160 s for turns
and 0.130 and 0.180 s for backchannels, respectively. Mean er-
rors were substantially larger for turn boundaries, indicating a
smaller number of large boundary mismatches. Performance did
not differ significantly across the two experimental listening con-
ditions. In a four-conversation case study, pipeline–human agree-
ment was lower and more variable than human inter-annotator
agreement and varied across parameter settings. These results
support the pipeline as an automated first pass within a semi-
automated annotation workflow, providing a consistent basis for
more standardised and reproducible annotation of conversational
dynamics.

1. Introduction
Spontaneous conversation is a highly coordinated form of so-
cial interaction in which speakers continuously manage when to
speak, when to yield the floor, and how to respond - all while
listening to another person speaking [1, 2, 3, 4].
Conversa-
tional dynamics can be quantified through both the occurrence
of interactional events, such as turns and backchannels, and their
temporal organization, including turn duration, transition timing,
pauses, and overlap. These measures characterize how interlocu-
tors distribute and coordinate their contributions over time and
have been associated with social connection, rapport, and inter-
action quality [5, 6, 7]. They are also relevant to applied areas
such as hearing and clinical research [8, 9]. Reliable identifica-
tion of these interaction-level units from recordings is therefore

an important methodological step in the analysis of natural con-
versation.
Extracting such units directly from conversational audio is
challenging. While voice activity detection (VAD) can identify
regions containing speech, speech activity does not map directly
onto conversational structure. Pauses can occur within an ongo-
ing turn, speaker transitions can occur with little or no silence,
and genuine contributions from two speakers can overlap [10, 2].
In separate-channel recordings, speech from one participant may
also leak into the interlocutor’s microphone, creating apparent
speech activity on both channels [11]. An automated method
must therefore distinguish recording artefacts and fragmented
speech activity from interactionally meaningful speaker contri-
butions. This distinction is particularly important because con-
versational turns are not defined by acoustic boundaries alone.
Turn transitions are jointly organized and can depend on syntac-
tic, lexical, prosodic, and temporal information [1, 12, 3, 13].
Consequently, a pause does not necessarily terminate a turn, and
a new speech segment does not necessarily represent a new at-
tempt to take the floor. Segmentation based only on speech ac-
tivity can therefore produce acoustically valid regions that do not
correspond to the units required for analysis of conversational
dynamics.
Listener responses introduce an additional classification
problem. Backchannels, for example, provide feedback to the
current speaker without attempting to take the conversational
floor [14, 15]. They may occur during an ongoing turn and vary
in lexical form, duration, and complexity [16, 15]. Some consist
of short conventional responses such as mhm or yeah, whereas
others contain multiple units and extend beyond the duration ex-
pected of a minimal response [15]. Hence, backchannels cannot
be identified using duration or a fixed lexical dictionary alone, as
their classification depends on conversation context.
How these units are operationalized directly affects down-
stream measures of conversational dynamics. Correct classifica-
tion determines which events contribute to measures such as turn
and backchannel frequency, while temporal boundary detection
affects measures of turn duration, transition timing, pauses, and
overlap. Reliable analysis therefore requires both accurate iden-
tification of conversational units and sufficiently precise temporal
segmentation. Differences in these operational criteria can also
limit comparison across studies. Consistent segmentation crite-
ria are therefore important for both annotation reliability and the
reproducibility of quantitative conversation research [10, 4, 15].
These requirements create a gap between the output of
general speech-processing tools and the interaction-level la-
bels needed for conversation analysis.
VAD estimates when
speech is present, while automatic speech recognition (ASR) es-
timates what was said. Neither alone determines whether de-
tected speech is a continuation of an existing turn, a new floor-

holding contribution, or listener feedback. Recent work has be-
gun to address this problem at the transcript level.
Natural-
Turn [17] is a deterministic post-processing method that trans-
forms speaker-specific ASR output into more naturalistic conver-
sational turns. It merges successive utterances belonging to the
same floor-holding contribution and separates listener speech oc-
curring within these turns into non-primary contributions, includ-
ing backchannels and longer secondary turns. Backchannels are
identified using configurable lexical cues and simple rules based
on the content and length of the listener contribution. In this way,
NaturalTurn enables the reconstruction of conversational struc-
ture from already transcribed speech without requiring annotated
training data.
The problem that our pipeline aims to solve begins at an
earlier stage of processing. In separate-channel recordings of
face-to-face conversation, the speaker-specific speech segments
from which such transcripts are constructed must first be identi-
fied. Cross-channel leakage can produce apparent speech activ-
ity on both channels, while natural within-turn pauses can frag-
ment a single conversational contribution into multiple detected
speech segments [11, 18, 19].
Deriving turns and backchan-
nels from the audio therefore requires cross-channel leakage
to be distinguished from target-speaker activity and fragmented
speech segments to be merged before conversational units can
be classified. Unlike NaturalTurn, which takes speaker-specific
word-level ASR output as its input, our pipeline derives speaker-
specific temporal units directly from the audio, with transcription
used subsequently to help classify these units.
The present study therefore develops an automated audio-to-
annotation pipeline for extracting turns and backchannels from
separate-channel recordings of spontaneous dyadic conversation,
where participants converse freely without a prescribed topic or
task. The pipeline combines VAD, channel-energy filtering, tem-
poral merging, automatic transcription, and conversational-unit
classification. Backchannels are identified from the transcribed
speech and its conversational context rather than from a prede-
fined list of backchannel expressions or simple thresholding. The
resulting annotations are intended as a consistent first pass for
subsequent human review.
For the present analysis, a turn is defined as a continuous
contribution by one participant while holding the conversational
floor. Within-turn pauses, hesitations, and filled pauses do not
necessarily terminate the turn. A backchannel is defined as a
listener contribution that provides feedback to the current speaker
without attempting to take the conversational floor [20, 15].
We evaluate the pipeline on 99 ten-minute face-to-face Dan-
ish conversations from 33 dyads.
The evaluation addresses
two aspects required for quantifying conversational dynamics:
whether turns and backchannels are correctly identified, and how
accurately their temporal boundaries are located. We addition-
ally examine performance across listening conditions and param-
eter settings. A subset of four conversations independently anno-
tated by a second human annotator provides an initial benchmark
against human inter-annotator agreement.
2. Methods
2.1. Conversation unit definitions
Two conversational units were defined, in order to guide the hu-
man annotators:
• Turn: A continuous stretch of speech produced by a single
participant before yielding the conversational floor.
Minor
pauses, hesitations, and filled pauses within the same floor-
holding contribution do not constitute separate turns.
• Backchannel: A listener-produced utterance that provides
feedback relevant to the ongoing turn without attempting to
take the conversational floor. Backchannels may be lexical
(e.g., yes, okay) or non-lexical (e.g., uh-huh, mm), and may
vary in form, duration, and function [15].
Two additional terms were used for the technical implemen-
tation of the annotation pipeline:
• Crosstalk: Acoustic leakage in which speech produced by one
participant is captured on the interlocutor’s microphone chan-
nel. Crosstalk is treated as a recording artefact and is distin-
guished from genuine conversational overlap, in which both
participants produce speech simultaneously.
• Overlapped turn: An algorithmic label assigned to a candi-
date turn that is fully temporally contained within a turn pro-
duced by the interlocutor. The label describes the temporal
relationship between the two segments and does not, by itself,
imply successful or attempted floor-taking.
2.2. Human reference annotation
Human reference annotations were produced manually using the
conversational-unit definitions described in Section 2.1. One
annotator annotated all 99 conversations and these annotations
served as the human reference for the main pipeline evaluation.
The two participant audio channels were imported separately
into ELAN. During annotation, the annotator could inspect both
waveforms and switch between the two audio channels, allowing
contributions from one participant to be interpreted in relation
to the speech of the interlocutor. Each conversational unit was
manually labelled as a turn or backchannel, and its onset and
offset were marked on the corresponding participant channel by
listening to the audio while inspecting the waveform.
To investigate inter-annotator agreement, a second annotator
independently annotated four conversations from distinct dyads
using the same definitions and annotation procedure.
These
annotations were compared with the corresponding annotations
from the primary annotator using the same segment-matching
criteria subsequently used for pipeline – human evaluation.
2.3. Pipeline architecture
The automated annotation pipeline transforms separate audio
channels from each participant into a structured set of speech
events labelled as turns or backchannels. The pipeline consists of
seven stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The stages combine speech
activity detection, channel-energy filtering, automatic transcrip-
tion, and rule-based post-processing to produce an initial anno-
tation that can subsequently be reviewed by a human annotator.
In Stage I, mild audio preprocessing is applied separately
to each participant’s original audio channel. A 5th-order Butter-
worth high-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 60 Hz is applied
using the scipy package [21]. This removes low-frequency sig-
nal components while otherwise limiting modification of the au-
dio prior to voice activity detection and channel-energy compar-
ison.
In Stage II, speech activity is detected separately in each par-
ticipant’s audio channel using rVAD [22]. rVAD was selected be-
cause it is an unsupervised and computationally lightweight VAD
method designed for robust speech activity detection across vari-
able acoustic conditions, without requiring task-specific training
data. This makes it suitable as an initial speech-detection stage
in the present pipeline, where subsequent processing further re-
solves cross-channel leakage and conversational structure.


Speech activity is estimated on 25-ms frames with a 10-ms
step size. The rVAD decision threshold, denoted τVAD, controls
the criterion used by the algorithm to classify frames as speech
or non-speech. The pipeline uses the default τVAD = 0.4. The
sensitivity of pipeline performance to this parameter is examined
separately in the parameter analysis.
In Stage III, an energy-based filter is applied to reduce
crosstalk between the two participant channels. For each speech
segment k identified by rVAD, the root-mean-square (RMS) am-
plitude is calculated as

In Stage IV, VAD segments are merged into longer candi-
date conversational units using a 3-s forward-looking window.
Same-speaker segments separated by ≤0.5 s are merged di-
rectly. Segments separated by longer gaps may also be merged
when their combined speech duration equals or exceeds the in-
tervening gap, preventing longer contributions from being frag-
mented by pauses in speech activity.
At this stage, merged
segments are capped at 60 s for computational efficiency dur-
ing subsequent Whisper transcription. This is a processing con-
straint rather than a maximum conversational-unit duration, and
no corresponding duration limit is imposed on the final turns or
backchannels.
Segments shorter than 1.0 s are treated as short utterances
for the purposes of this merging procedure. When a short utter-
ance from an interlocutor occurs between two segments from the
same speaker, the algorithm can bridge across it and continue the
original speaker’s candidate unit. The intervening utterance is
nevertheless retained as a separate candidate event, allowing po-
tential backchannels and overlapping speech to be classified in
subsequent stages.
The 0.5-s gap threshold is a theory-informed criterion rather
than a definition of a turn boundary. Danish has comparatively
long response offsets in question–response sequences [2], while
backchannels can consist of multiple units and extend beyond
500 ms [15]. The merging procedure therefore allows tempo-
ral flexibility while reducing fragmentation introduced by frame-
level speech activity detection.
In Stage V, stronger audio preprocessing is applied to the
original audio channels prior to automatic transcription, to pro-
vide a more consistent input signal for ASR. The preprocess-
ing consists of the 60-Hz high-pass filter described in Stage
I, followed by stationary spectral-gating noise reduction using
the noisereduce package [23]. The resulting signal is then
loudness-normalized to −23 LUFS following EBU R 128 [24].
Loudness normalization is skipped when the signal is already
within ±3 LU of the target, and the applied gain is capped at
+20 dB to avoid excessive amplification.
Finally, signal am-
plitude is limited to ±0.95 (approximately −0.45 dBFS) using
numpy.clip [25]. The processed audio is used only for the
subsequent transcription stage. Speech-event boundaries remain
those obtained from the previous stages.
In Stage VI, the preprocessed speech segments are tran-
scribed using the multilingual Whisper-large-v3 ASR
model [26]. In the pipeline, the transcription serves as an inter-
mediate representation for the initial classification of candidate
segments rather than as the final annotation output.

and are subsequently re-evaluated using the criteria described in

In Stage VII, the preliminary classifications are refined us-
ing the temporal context of the two speakers. A segment initially
classified as a turn is relabelled as an overlapped turn when it is
fully contained within a turn produced by the interlocutor.
The algorithm then performs a final context-based merging
step. Two turns from the same speaker may be merged when
the second begins within 3.0 s of the current turn. Merging is
allowed when no intervening segment is present, or when the
intervening segments consist only of backchannels or overlapped
turns with durations of at most 1.0 s. These intervening segments
are retained as separate annotations rather than absorbed into the
merged turn.
Before evaluation, the pipeline output and human reference
annotations were both subjected to the same context-based nor-
malization. Same-speaker turns were merged unless they were
separated by either a backchannel from that speaker or a turn
from the other interlocutor. Backchannels that did not overlap
with an interlocutor contribution were relabelled as turns, while
turns fully contained within an interlocutor turn were relabelled
as backchannels. These merging and relabelling rules were ap-


plied repeatedly until no further changes occurred, up to a maxi-
mum of 20 iterations.
Experiments were conducted using GPUs with at least 16 GB
of VRAM. Whisper-large-v3 inference was performed on the
GPU, whereas rVAD processing was CPU-based. Processing a
10-min conversation through the complete pipeline required ap-
proximately 2 min on average, i.e., approximately 5× real-time
processing.

2.4. Dataset and recording protocol
The dataset was collected as part of a larger multimodal study
of spontaneous dialog between same-gender dyads. Participants
were native Danish speakers between 18 and 35 years of age,
with normal hearing and no reported communication difficulties,
and were unacquainted prior to the experiment. The maximum
age difference within a dyad was four years. Each dyad com-
pleted three 10-min face-to-face conversations in Danish, result-
ing in approximately 30 min of conversational speech per dyad.
Participants were not assigned a topic or conversational task and
were instructed to converse freely. Speech was recorded sep-
arately for each participant using head-worn directional micro-
phones (DPA 4188) at 48 kHz.
The three conversations were conducted in different listen-
ing conditions. In the noise-free condition, neither participant
received experimental auditory interference. The two other con-
versations were conducted in an asymmetric-noise condition. In
these, speech-shaped noise was delivered to only one partici-
pant through bone-conduction headphones, while the interlocu-
tor conversed without auditory interference. The order of con-
ditions was counterbalanced across dyads. Participants were in-
formed that noise could occur during the experiment but were
not told in advance when or to whom it would be presented, and
were instructed not to disclose the manipulation during the con-
versation.
The level of the noise was calibrated using masked detection
thresholds obtained in an independent sample of 10 participants
aged 18–35 years. The level matched that which was required
to achieve 70% detection of a spoken digit presented at 45 dBA.
Importantly, the noise from the bone-conduction headphones did
not interfere with the recorded audio signal. Thus, the asymmet-
ric noise condition represents variation in the listening conditions
under which speech was produced, rather than variation in back-
ground noise within the recordings themselves.
The present analysis includes 99 conversations from 33
dyads, corresponding to approximately 16.5 hours of conversa-
tional audio. Four conversations from distinct dyads were addi-
tionally annotated by a second human annotator for evaluation of
inter-annotator agreement.
2.5. Evaluation Metrics
2.5.1. Detection reliability
Detection reliability was evaluated by matching automatically
estimated segments to the human reference annotations using
temporal intersection over union (IoU). For a reference seg-
ment Sref,i and an estimated segment Sest,j, IoU was defined
as
IoU(i, j) = dur (Sref,i ∩Sest,j)
dur (Sref,i ∪Sest,j).
(6)
Example: if a reference turn extends from 10.0
to 12.0 s and the corresponding estimated turn ex-
tends from 10.5 to 12.5 s, their intersection is 1.5 s
and their union is 2.5 s, giving an IoU of 1.5/2.5 =
0.60. Similarly, for a short backchannel extending
from 10.0 to 10.5 s in the reference annotation and
from 10.4 to 10.9 s in the pipeline output, the inter-
section is 0.1 s and the union is 0.9 s, giving an IoU
of approximately 0.11.
Candidate matches were restricted to segments assigned to
the same speaker and the same conversational-unit type. A refer-
ence and estimate segment were considered a match when their
IoU was at least 0.50 for turns or 0.10 for backchannels. A lower
threshold was used for backchannels because their shorter dura-
tions make IoU more sensitive to small absolute differences in
onset and offset timing.
When multiple candidate segments satisfied the overlap cri-
terion, competing matches were resolved by retaining the candi-
date with the highest IoU, such that each segment contributed to
at most one match. Matched reference segments were counted as
true positives (TP), unmatched reference segments as false nega-
tives (FN), and unmatched estimated segments as false positives
(FP). Precision, recall, and F1 scores were calculated from these


classifications for turns and backchannels separately. An overall
F1 score was additionally calculated across turns and backchan-
nels combined.

For successfully matched segments, temporal precision was eval-
uated by comparing the estimated onset and offset boundaries
with those of the corresponding human reference segment. Ab-
solute onset and offset errors were defined as
∆ton,i = |tref,on,i −test,on,i| ,
(7)
and
∆toff,i = |tref,off,i −test,off,i| ,

Example: if a human reference segment spans 2.0–
3.0 s and the matched pipeline segment spans 2.1–
2.9 s, the onset and offset errors are both 0.1 s.
Temporal precision was summarized from the pooled abso-
lute boundary errors of successfully matched segments across all
conversations, separately for turn onsets, turn offsets, backchan-
nel onsets, and backchannel offsets. The median absolute error
(MedAE) and interquartile range (IQR) were used to describe the
typical error and its spread. Mean absolute error (MAE) and stan-
dard deviation (SD) were additionally reported to characterize
the influence of larger errors. For comparisons between listening
conditions, MedAE was calculated separately for each conversa-
tion to provide one temporal-error measure per conversation.
3. Results
3.1. Effect of context-based post-processing
Figure 2 illustrates the effect of context-based post-processing
(Stage VII) on one example conversation.
Before post-
processing, the pipeline output is considerably more fragmented
than the manual annotation. After post-processing, many of these
fragments are joined into longer same-speaker units, and the
broader pattern of speaker alternation is visually similar to that of
the manual annotation. Agreement for individual backchannels
was less consistent, with some manually annotated backchannels
not captured by the pipeline.
For this example, post-processing reduced the manual an-
notation from 69 to 55 segments and the pipeline output from
125 to 58 segments (Table 1). Across all 99 conversations, the
mean number of segments per conversation decreased from 186.6
to 172.2 for the manual annotations (7.7%) and from 158.8 to
154.1 for the pipeline (3.0%). The corresponding mean number
of turns decreased from 78.8 to 71.8 for the manual annotations
and from 51.4 to 49.3 for the pipeline, while the mean number of
backchannels decreased from 107.8 to 100.4 and from 107.5 to
104.8, respectively.
Together, the example illustrates the intended use of the
pipeline as an assistive first pass. It provides a structured approx-
imation of the conversation that preserves the broader turn-taking
pattern, while leaving individual events available for subsequent
human review. This study did not directly evaluate annotation
time, and potential annotation efficiency gains are therefore not
quantified here.

3.2. Overall pipeline performance
Across the full set of 99 conversations, pipeline-generated turns
and backchannels were evaluated against the human reference
annotations using the matching criteria described in Section 2.5.
Mean detection reliability was similar for turns (F1 = 0.624 ±
0.143) and backchannels (F1 = 0.618 ± 0.160), with an overall
F1 score of 0.621 ± 0.151 across conversations (Table 2).
For successfully matched segments, absolute boundary er-
rors were smaller for backchannels than for turns. Median ab-
solute errors were 0.130 s for backchannel onsets and 0.180 s for
backchannel offsets, compared with 0.150 s and 0.160 s for turn
onsets and offsets, respectively. Mean absolute errors showed a
larger difference, with values of 0.994 s and 0.984 s for turn on-
sets and offsets, compared with 0.152 s and 0.219 s for backchan-
nels.
These absolute errors should be interpreted in relation
to segment duration, as backchannels are typically substantially
shorter than turns. Nevertheless, the large difference between
MedAE and MAE within the turn category indicates that most
matched turn boundaries had relatively small errors, while a
smaller number of large errors increased the mean.

3.4. Case study:
Benchmarking against inter-annotator
agreement
To examine sensitivity to parameter selection and benchmark
pipeline performance against human inter-annotator agreement,
we conducted a case study using four conversations indepen-
dently annotated by two human annotators. The same segment-
matching criteria used for pipeline–human evaluation were ap-
plied to the human annotations, providing a directly comparable
measure of segment-level agreement.
We evaluated 80 parameter combinations, comprising 10 val-
ues of the rVAD threshold (τVAD; 0.1–1.0) and eight values of
the entropy threshold (τH; 0.5–3.0). Applied to each of the four
conversations, this resulted in 320 pipeline runs.
Across the 320 runs, mean pipeline detection reliability was
F1 = 0.626 ± 0.225 overall, 0.668 ± 0.179 for turns, and
0.585±0.256 for backchannels (Table 3). Human inter-annotator
agreement on the same four conversations was higher, with F1 =
0.845±0.118 overall, 0.868±0.051 for turns, and 0.821±0.168
for backchannels.
Temporal differences were also smaller between human an-
notators. Median absolute boundary errors for the pipeline were
0.190 s and 0.210 s for turn onsets and offsets, respectively, com-
pared with 0.080 s and 0.110 s between human annotators. For
backchannels, the corresponding values were 0.140 s and 0.200 s
for the pipeline and 0.050 s and 0.040 s between human annota-
tors.
As shown in Fig. 4, pipeline performance also varied sub-
stantially across parameter combinations. The pipeline distribu-
tions were much broader than the inter-annotator distributions,
particularly for backchannel F1 and temporal boundary errors,
and included a greater number of large-error cases. One con-
versation produced a turn F1 of 1.0 for some parameter settings.
This conversation contained long stretches of speech from one
participant with few interruptions or short responses from the
interlocutor, resulting in a comparatively simple turn structure.
Overall, performance varied substantially across parameter set-
tings and across the four conversations included in the case study.
No inferential statistical comparison was performed between
pipeline-human and human–human agreement. The 320 pipeline
observations represent repeated parameter combinations applied
to the same four conversations and are therefore not independent
observations, while the human benchmark is based on only those
four conversations. Inferential tests treating these values as inde-
pendent samples would therefore overstate the effective sample
size.

for use within a semi-automated annotation workflow. A cen-
tral challenge in large-scale conversation research is not only
identifying conversational units, but doing so consistently across
recordings.
Manual annotation requires repeated judgements
about turn boundaries and interactional function, which can be
time-consuming and may vary between annotators.
An auto-
mated first pass offers a way to apply the same operational
criteria systematically across a corpus, with subsequent human
review to resolve ambiguous cases.
This can support more
standardized and reproducible annotation while retaining human
judgement where interactional context is important.
Across 99 Danish conversations, the pipeline achieved simi-
lar segment-level detection reliability for turns and backchannels,
with an overall F1 score of approximately 0.62. For successfully
matched segments, median temporal errors were relatively small,
with median absolute errors of 0.150 s and 0.160 s for turn onsets
and offsets, respectively. Mean absolute errors were substantially
larger, at approximately 1 s for both boundaries. The mean there-
fore does not represent the typical boundary difference between
matched pipeline and human segments. Instead, the distribution
contains a smaller number of substantially larger errors. Such
errors may arise in part from the temporal complexity of sponta-
neous speech, including hesitations, filled pauses, and disfluen-
cies [27], but they also identify cases in which the segmentation
procedure requires further improvement. Backchannel bound-
aries showed smaller differences between median and mean er-
rors, indicating fewer extreme timing errors.
Pipeline performance did not differ significantly between the
noise-free and asymmetric-noise listening conditions.
Impor-
tantly, the experimental manipulation altered the listening con-
text under which participants produced speech rather than intro-
ducing noise into the recorded microphone signal. These results
therefore show that the pipeline performance was comparable
across the recordings obtained from the two experimental listen-
ing conditions.
The four-conversation case study provided a complementary
comparison with human inter-annotator agreement.
Pipeline-
human agreement was numerically lower and more variable than
human-human agreement for both detection and temporal preci-
sion. These comparisons were descriptive rather than inferen-
tial. The 320 pipeline runs represent repeated parameter com-
binations applied to the same four conversations and cannot be
treated as independent observations, while the human compari-
son was based on only four conversations. The case study should
therefore be interpreted as a descriptive benchmark rather than as
a statistical comparison of pipeline and human performance.
The case study also highlights an important issue in evaluat-
ing automated conversation annotation: human reference annota-
tions themselves contain variability. The two annotators differed
in both segment classification and temporal boundary placement.
Classifying turns and backchannels can require judgement about
conversational context and communicative function [28, 29]. Hu-
man agreement should therefore not be treated as a strict perfor-
mance ceiling but a descriptive benchmark. A larger, system-
atic study of inter-annotator agreement is needed to characterize
variability in both conversational-unit classification and temporal
boundary estimation.
Performance also varied across pipeline parameter settings,
particularly for backchannel detection. This indicates sensitivity
to the rVAD and entropy thresholds and reinforces the impor-
tance of parameter selection. Backchannels present a particu-
lar challenge because they are often short and may contain little
lexical information. A further limitation is the reliance on au-
tomatic transcription for conversational-unit classification. Short
or non-lexical backchannels may be omitted or inaccurately tran-
scribed by the ASR system, which can affect the lexical-entropy
measure and consequently the assigned turn or backchannel la-
bel. Transcription accuracy was not evaluated separately in the
present study. Future work should evaluate ASR performance
specifically for these short conversational responses and evaluate
parameter selection on independent data.
Two additional limitations concern the practical use and gen-
eralizability of the pipeline. Annotation time was not measured
directly, so the extent to which the proposed workflow reduces
manual annotation effort remains to be quantified. The present
evaluation was also limited to dyadic, separate-channel Danish
conversation. Future work should examine annotation efficiency
and test the approach across languages, recording configurations,
and interactional settings, including multi-party conversation.

5. Conclusion
This study presents an automated pipeline for identifying turns
and backchannels in spontaneous dyadic conversation, designed
for use within a semi-automated annotation workflow. Across
99 conversations, the pipeline provided a consistent first-pass
segmentation with comparable detection reliability for turns
and backchannels and relatively small median temporal errors
for successfully matched segments. Although performance re-
mained more variable than human inter-annotator agreement and
was sensitive to parameter selection, the pipeline provides a con-
sistent starting point for human review. By applying the same
operational criteria across recordings, the pipeline can support
more standardized and reproducible annotation procedures.
