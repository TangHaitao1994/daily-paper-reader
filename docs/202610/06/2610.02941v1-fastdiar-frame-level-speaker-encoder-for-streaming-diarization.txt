ABSTRACT
Real-time conversational agents require speaker diarization
that streams and runs on a CPU. Most systems apply an
utterance-level speaker encoder to short, heavily overlapping
chunks, which wastes computation and leaves the model op-
timized for the wrong task. We instead turn a state-of-the-art
speaker recognition architecture into a causal frame-level
encoder that reads the stream once and emits one embed-
ding every 80 ms from a bounded two-second window of
past audio, and pair it with online clustering that gates every
update on the self-similarity of the stream. Trained only by
distillation from an utterance-level teacher on simulated and
out-of-domain mixtures, and evaluated with one fixed set of
hyperparameters, the system is the most accurate streaming
diarizer on low-overlap benchmarks at sub-second latency,
degrades far less than cache-based systems as the number of
speakers grows, and runs five times faster than real time on a
single CPU thread.
Index Terms— Speaker Diarization, Speaker Recogni-
tion, Streaming Speaker Diarization
1. INTRODUCTION
Speaker diarization answers who speaks when and is a core
component of spoken conversational systems. Personalized
automatic speech recognition (ASR), voice activity detec-
tion (VAD), and speech enhancement all depend on knowing
which speaker is active, and a live agent must decide while
the conversation unfolds, so low-latency streaming is a key
requirement. GPUs are often unavailable, either because the
system runs on a low-power device or because the GPU is
busy with ASR, a large language model, or speech synthesis,
which makes CPU diarization desirable.
Most diarization systems are built around a speaker en-
coder [1, 2], applied off the shelf to short, heavily overlap-
ping chunks. That is convenient but costly: consecutive win-
dows re-encode every sample many times over, and verifica-
tion training assumes one speaker per utterance, so the en-
coder is never asked to forget a speaker or localize a change.

Offline diarization is dominated by cascaded pipelines
combining local segmentation with clustering, such as Pyan-
note [3] and DiariZen [4], while end-to-end models predict
activities directly with permutation-invariant training [5], at-
tractors [6] or arrival-time ordering [7]. All of them need the
whole recording before emitting labels, so none can stream.
Existing streaming systems show recurring trade-offs.
Streaming Sortformer [8] keeps an arrival-order speaker
cache and is accurate, but it is capped at four speakers and
its 117.7M parameters are too slow on CPU. The speaker-
tracing buffer [9] re-processes buffered frames at every step,
BW-EDA-EEND [10] is linear in time but degrades beyond
two speakers, and EEND-GLA [11] lifts the speaker-count
limit without reporting a CPU budget. On the cascaded side,
diart [12] attains low latency on CPU with incremental clus-
tering over local segmentation, but is less accurate.
The encoder in front of the clustering usually stays
segment-level. UIS-RNN [13] makes clustering supervised
but reads d-vectors from windows with 50% overlap, and
Turn-to-Diarize [14] ties output resolution to a turn detector.
Two works reach frame rate: [15] distils an utterance-level
teacher into a student that pools over a centred window, and
LS-EEND [16, 17] learns a causal frame-wise encoder jointly
with its attractor decoder. Neither gives a streaming encoder
with a bounded past: the first reads the future, the second
carries the whole recording in its recurrent state.
In this work we present FASTDIAR (FrAme-level speaker
encoder for STreaming DIARization). Our contributions are
(i) a causal frame-level speaker encoder that needs no chunk-
ing, no temporal pooling and no recomputation, and sum-
marizes a two-second past window, (ii) an extension of in-
cremental online clustering where an embedding-confidence
gate decides which frames may shape a speaker centroid,
and (iii) FASTDIAR – the most accurate streaming diarizer
on low-overlap benchmarks at sub-second latency, far more
robust than cache-based systems as the speaker count grows,
and five times faster than real time on one CPU thread
with no real diarization corpus in training.
The encoder
is a drop-in front end whose embeddings can feed other
speaker-aware models.

2. METHOD
2.1. Streaming speaker encoder
We take ReDimNet2 [18] as the baseline, a leading speaker
recognition architecture with a low FLOPs budget. It alter-
nates 2D convolutional blocks over a channel-frequency-time
feature map with 1D blocks that operate on the same map af-
ter reshaping, and it produces a single utterance-level embed-
ding through temporal pooling. We address three properties
of this design that conflict with streaming diarization.
(1) Causal convolutions. We pad every 2D convolution only
on the left of the time axis, so the output at frame t depends
on frames up to t. This removes access to future context.
(2) Fully connected 1D blocks.
The 1D attention blocks
share temporal information across the whole utterance, giv-
ing an unbounded receptive field. That helps verification and
hurts diarization, where the encoder must forget the current
speaker and follow a new one. We replace them with fully
connected blocks, which mix frequency bins and channels
without extending the temporal receptive field.
(3) Frame-level embeddings. Temporal pooling aggregates
statistics over the whole utterance and makes the past recep-
tive field unbounded. In streaming diarization each embed-
ding must instead rely on a limited number of past frames, so
speaker changes are handled promptly and cluster boundaries
stay sharp. We remove the pooling layer and project the en-
coder output directly to a d = 192 dimensional embedding
at every step. Raising the time stride of the final block from
1 to 2 lowers the FLOPs count and makes each embedding
summarize 80 ms of audio rather than 40 ms.
The resulting encoder has a receptive field of two seconds
of past audio. It reads the stream once and emits one ℓ2-
normalized embedding et every τ = 80 ms, with no chunk-
ing and no recomputation of already seen samples. Drop-
ping pooling bounds our past and lets an embedding forget a
speaker, at a verification cost quantified in Section 4.
2.2. Online clustering
The algorithm is built around embedding confidence, the co-
sine similarity ⟨et, et−D⟩between the current embedding and
the one D frames back. High confidence means the embed-
ding has been stable over the last D frames and the encoder
has enough same-speaker context. Low confidence signals a
speaker change, silence or noise. Only confident frames up-
date the cluster model, so centroids are never degraded, while
the rest are still labelled. Without it, an embedding straddling
a speaker change would drag both centroids together.

the ℓ2-normalized median mk of their embeddings, which re-
sists outliers better than the mean. A confident frame joins the
closest cluster when similarity exceeds θclust, and opens a new

θmerge and fuses the connected components, repairing over-
segmentation. The same merge can be optionally used over
the whole recording at end of stream, as a label correction.

emitted with their closest cluster. The assignment step fol-
lows incremental centroid clustering as used in online linking
[20] and in diart [12], which map each embedding to the near-
est centroid and open a speaker when the distance exceeds a
threshold. What we add is the confidence gate. Confidence
has been used in diarization before, but as a segment-level
score computed after clustering, from the similarity of a seg-
ment to its assigned centroid, in order to flag likely errors for
downstream consumers [21]. Ours needs no centroid and no
label, arrives with the frame itself, and decides prospectively
which frames may update a speaker model. The three thresh-
olds are tuned once on the development data of Section 3.1
and then kept fixed for every experiment.

3. EXPERIMENTS
3.1. Datasets
Training. VoxCeleb2 [22] contains one speaker per utter-
ance, so multi-speaker examples are simulated on the fly.
With probability 0.75 we draw crops x1 and x2 from two
different speakers, normalize both to unit RMS, and arrange
them over T = 8 s in one of two layouts.
The first has
one speaker change at T/2 preceded by an overlap of o ms,
log o ∼N(µ, σ2) truncated to [150, 4000] ms and excluded
from the loss. The second alternates x1 →x2 →x1 with
segments of at least one second. The interferer is scaled to a
signal-to-interference ratio from U(−5, 5) dB, and the mix-
ture is RMS-normalized so level carries no cue. We augment
with MUSAN [23] and simulated room impulse responses
[24] through WeSpeaker [25]. We further use the medium
split of LibriHeavyMix [26], keeping unaugmented mixtures
of two or three speakers in which one covers 20–90% of
the audio. VoxCeleb2 is sampled 75% of the time and Lib-
riHeavyMix 25%, with the VoxConverse and NOTSOFAR
development sets used to select the checkpoint and tune the
three clustering thresholds.
Benchmarks. We assess diarization on the evaluation sets
of NOTSOFAR [27] (single channel), VoxConverse v0.3
[28], DIHARD3 [29], AMI [30] (IHM-MIX), MSDWild [31]
(few), and RAMC [32]. They cover multiple languages, var-
ious distances and 2 to 21 speakers per recording. Streaming
Sortformer is capped at four speakers, so for the three cor-
pora crossing that boundary, we also report DER split at it.
Speaker recognition is measured on VoxCeleb1-E [33].

dent, as in [15], but maximize cosine similarity [34] rather
than minimize squared error. The teacher embeds the clean,
unaugmented crop of speaker s as zs, and the student embeds
the augmented mixture frame by frame. The objective is

3.3. Baseline models
We compare against open-source streaming systems: Stream-
ing Sortformer [8] v2.1 at 1040 ms latency, diart [12] at
1000 ms with recommended thresholds for DIHARD3, AMI
and VoxConverse, and LS-EEND [16] at 1000 ms. All are
reproduced with released code and checkpoints. LS-EEND
ships one model per corpus: we use its DIHARD3 check-
point by default, the strongest on our development data,
and its AMI checkpoint for that test, so it is in-domain on
DIHARD3 and AMI and out-of-domain elsewhere. To iso-
late the encoder we build ReDimNet2-B6-S, the unmodified
ReDimNet2-B6 applied to two-second windows advanced by
80 ms, reproducing our receptive field and embedding rate
and feeding the same VAD and clustering.
3.4. Evaluation protocol

and without overlap, and split it by the number of speakers.

tem and its label leaving it, fixed at M = 12 frames, that
is 960 ms, for every frame of every recording. It covers the
D = 10 frame look-back the system fills before it can emit at
all. The real-time factor (RTF) is processing time over in-

4. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Diarization accuracy. Results in Table 1 vary greatly un-
der each protocol.
Without overlap, FASTDIAR leads on
NOTSOFAR, VoxConverse and RAMC, and trails only the in-
domain LS-EEND on AMI. With overlap counted, Streaming
Sortformer moves ahead of it on NOTSOFAR, DIHARD3,
AMI and MSDWild, and FASTDIAR keeps VoxConverse and
RAMC, the corpora with little or no overlap.
Speaker count. Streaming Sortformer is built around a cache
of at most four speakers and degrades sharply beyond it: ex-
cluding overlap, its DER grows by a factor of 2.3 to 4.4 across
the four-speaker boundary, against 1.2 to 1.5 for FASTDIAR,
which leads the ≥5 column on NOTSOFAR and VoxCon-
verse. Nothing in the encoder or the clustering is tied to a
speaker count, which a streaming system rarely knows.
Baselines and efficiency. LS-EEND leads on DIHARD3 and
AMI, its two in-domain corpora, and falls behind on the other
four, while FASTDIAR beats diart almost everywhere. On
one CPU thread FASTDIAR, diart and LS-EEND all run sev-
eral times faster than real time, while Streaming Sortformer
needs a GPU. FASTDIAR has the lowest algorithm latency.
Generalization. FASTDIAR trains on simulated VoxCeleb2
mixtures and LibriHeavyMix, with no real diarization corpus.
Tuning the checkpoint and thresholds on their development
sets leaves VoxConverse and NOTSOFAR not fully unseen,
while the other four run untouched. The price shows on DI-
HARD3, where FASTDIAR is last under both protocols. Our
simulated mixtures cover only a fraction of the scenarios it
targets, and every system ahead of it has seen DIHARD3:
LS-EEND trains on it, Sortformer on a mixture including its
development set, and diart tunes its thresholds there.
Encoder trade-off. As Table 2 shows, the chunked model
keeps verification accuracy but re-encodes every sample
many times, which rules out streaming on CPU. The pro-
posed encoder is more than 25 times faster, but it cannot pool
over an utterance, so its EER is around 3 times worse. Distil-
lation leaves the student in the teacher’s space, so the two are
directly comparable. Embedding one side of every trial with

Limitations.
FASTDIAR’s main limitation is overlapped
speech: it assigns one label per frame, so every overlapped
region counts as a miss. This explains most of the gap be-
tween the halves of Table 1, and it comes from the decoding
stage, not the encoder, which is trained on frames with one
active speaker. In future work we plan to keep overlapped
frames in the objective and predict several labels per frame,
changing neither the encoder nor its inference cost.
5. CONCLUSION
We presented FASTDIAR, a streaming diarization system
built on a causal frame-level speaker encoder that reads the
stream once and emits an embedding every 80 ms from a
two-second past window. Removing temporal pooling costs
verification accuracy but buys sharp speaker-change localiza-
tion and five times real-time speed on one CPU thread. With
confidence-gated online clustering at a fixed 960 ms delay, it
is the most accurate streaming diarizer on low-overlap bench-
marks, degrades far less than cache-based systems beyond
four speakers, and needs no diarization corpus in training.
The encoder is the reusable part: any real-time model need-
ing frame-level speaker identity can read the same stream,
and the diarizer here is only one of its consumers.
