ABSTRACT 

Delayed Streams Modeling (DSM) enables LLM-based stream-ing automatic speech recognition (ASR) by aligning acoustic and text streams on a common timeline. DSM adds the padding token <p> and the word-start token <w> to the LLM vocabulary and predicts them together with normal text tokens using the same softmax. We first show that <w> can be removed while maintaining competitive recognition performance. Based on this result, we propose Factorized DSM (F-DSM), which separates the waiting probability for <p> from the distribution over the original LLM vocabulary. This factorization removes ASR-specific tokens from the text prediction space and allows the large-vocabulary softmax to be skipped on waiting steps. Exper-iments on the Corpus of Spontaneous Japanese and LibriSpeech show that F-DSM achieves better recognition performance than DSM. It also greatly reduces GPU memory use while main-taining similar training throughput, provides a small inference speed improvement through softmax skipping, and reduces the degradation in text-only perplexity observed with DSM. 

Index Terms — Delayed Streams Modeling, streaming au-tomatic speech recognition, large language models, Hybrid Au-toregressive Transducer 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Streaming automatic speech recognition (ASR) processes in-coming speech step by step and produces text as soon as enough acoustic information is available [1, 2]. At each step, the model must decide whether to output a text token or wait for more speech. In Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) [3] and Recurrent Neural Network-Transducer (RNN-T) [4], this “no output” state is modeled by a blank symbol, which plays a different role from normal text tokens. Recently, large language models (LLMs) [5, 6] have been widely studied for ASR because of their strong language mod-eling ability [7–9]. However, standard LLMs are designed to generate text autoregressively and do not have a natural mecha-nism to control when a token should be emitted while speech is continuously arriving. Delayed Streams Modeling (DSM) addresses this problem by using the same decoder-only LLM to process time-aligned acoustic and text streams [10, 11]. In addition to normal text to-kens, DSM adds two special tokens to the LLM vocabulary. The padding token <p> means that the model should wait without pro-ducing text, while the word-start token <w> marks the beginning of a word. These special tokens are predicted together with nor-mal text tokens using the same softmax. This simple design allows an LLM to perform streaming ASR, but it also places ASR-specific timing tokens and normal text tokens in the same output distribution. In a time-aligned sequence, many steps have no text output and therefore use <p> 

as the target. As a result, <p> appears much more often than any individual text token. Still, DSM computes the projection and softmax over the full vocabulary even when the model only needs to wait. In addition, <p> and <w> are included in the soft-max normalization, so these ASR-specific tokens compete with the distribution over the original LLM vocabulary learned during pretraining. Streaming ASR therefore involves two distinct de-cisions: whether to emit a token and, if so, which token to emit. DSM couples these decisions in a single softmax, motivating us to model timing and lexical prediction separately. We first study whether <w> is necessary for speech recogni-tion. Our experiments show that removing <w> still gives com-petitive recognition performance. This result suggests that the ASR-specific output space of DSM can be simplified to only one special token, <p> . The remaining <p> can be viewed as a tim-ing symbol rather than a normal text token, similar to the blank symbol in CTC and RNN-T. Based on this view, we propose Fac-torized DSM (F-DSM), inspired by the Hybrid Autoregressive Transducer (HAT) [12]. F-DSM separates the waiting probabil-ity for <p> from the distribution over the original LLM vocabu-lary. With this factorization, the text prediction head keeps the same output space as the pretrained LLM and does not include any ASR-specific token. In addition, when the model decides to wait, it does not need to compute the large-vocabulary pro-jection and softmax. F-DSM therefore separates ASR-specific timing prediction from the original text-token prediction of the LLM while keeping the streaming framework of DSM. We evaluate F-DSM on Japanese and English using the Cor-pus of Spontaneous Japanese (CSJ) [13] and LibriSpeech [14]. F-DSM achieves better recognition performance than the origi-nal DSM. During training, it greatly reduces GPU memory use while keeping similar training throughput, resulting in more memory-efficient training. During inference, skipping the large-vocabulary softmax on waiting steps provides a small speed improvement. We also evaluate text-only language modeling. Compared with the original DSM, F-DSM reduces the degra-dation in perplexity (PPL) of the pretrained LLM, showing that separating ASR-specific tokens from the original LLM vocabu-lary better preserves its language modeling behavior. These results show that treating <p> separately from nor-mal text tokens is useful not only for reducing computation but also for preserving the original language modeling ability of the LLM. The factorized design requires only a small change to DSM while keeping its streaming framework unchanged. This makes F-DSM a simple and effective approach for LLM-based streaming ASR. arXiv:2610.04333v1 [eess.AS] 3 Oct 2026 Fig. 1 . 2. PRELIMINARIES 2.1. Delayed Streams Modeling 

DSM [10] is a method for streaming speech recognition with a decoder-only LLM. As shown on the left side of Fig. 1, DSM aligns acoustic and text streams in time and shifts the text stream by a fixed delay. This allows the model to predict the next token sequentially using acoustic information and previous text. In addition to normal text tokens, DSM adds two special to-kens to the LLM vocabulary. The padding token <p> represents waiting without producing text, while the word-start token <w> 

marks the beginning of a word. Let V denote the original LLM vocabulary. The output vocabulary of DSM is defined as 

VDSM = V ∪ { <p> , <w> }. (1) Let t index aligned time frames, yt be the output token, and ht ∈

Rd be the decoder hidden state. DSM predicts each token v ∈VDSM as 

PDSM (yt = v | ht) = Softmax( WDSM ht)v , (2) where WDSM ∈ R(|V| +2) ×d. This full-vocabulary projection is computed at every frame, including waiting frames. The word-start token <w> was introduced to explicitly mark the transition from padding to text and support timestamp ex-traction during inference [10, 11]. However, its necessity has not been established through an ablation that removes it. ASR systems can provide word-level timestamps without a dedicated word-start token [15], suggesting that <w> may also be unneces-sary in DSM. This motivates our ablation of <w> for recognition accuracy. 

2.2. Hybrid Autoregressive Transducer 

HAT [12] factorizes the output distribution of RNN-T into a blank probability and a conditional distribution over non-blank labels [16, 17]. This separates the decision of whether to emit a label from the decision of which label to emit. At acoustic frame t after u emitted labels, let ht,u be HAT’s joint hidden state, bt,u its blank probability, and qt,u (v) its con-ditional distribution over non-blank labels v ∈ V . Then

(3) This factorization motivates treating the waiting token <p> sepa-rately from text tokens in DSM. 

3. FACTORIZED DELAYED STREAMS MODELING 3.1. Overview 

Building on the DSM framework and the HAT factorization de-scribed in Sec. 2, we propose F-DSM, which removes <w> and separates the waiting probability for <p> from the distribution over the original LLM vocabulary. Following HAT and RNN-T, we feed back only emitted text tokens to the LLM decoder for autoregressive prediction, exclud-ing the waiting token <p> .

4. EXPERIMENTS 4.1. Experimental Setup 

We use CSJ for Japanese experiments and LibriSpeech for En-glish experiments. The base LLM is Qwen3.5-0.8B [19], and the acoustic encoder is the Fast-Conformer [20] encoder from Nemotron-3.5-ASR-Streaming-0.6B [2, 21]. The encoder oper-ates at a frame rate of 12.5 Hz. A randomly initialized two-layer MLP is used as an audio projection adapter between the acoustic encoder and the LLM. The speech and text in both datasets are aligned using Qwen3-Aligner [9], and the alignments are represented at the 12.5-Hz frame rate of the acoustic encoder. During training, the target delay is uniformly sampled from 160, 320, 480, 640, and 800 ms. Following Voxtral Realtime, we use Ada RMSNorm to condition the model on the target delay [11]. We compare DSM w/ <w> , DSM w/o <w> , and F-DSM. The second removes only <w> from DSM, while F-DSM further fac-torizes the prediction of <p> as described in Sec. 3. For the two DSM baselines, we apply z-loss regularization, following the setting reported to be effective in Voxtral Realtime [11]. F-DSM does not use z-loss regularization because <p> is not included in the LLM vocabulary. Training is performed on four NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a global batch size of 128. Each model is trained for a total of 100,000 steps using a three-stage training schedule. For the first 5,000 steps, only the audio projection adapter and the newly added prediction parameters are updated. For the next 5,000 steps, the acoustic encoder is additionally updated. For the re-maining 90,000 steps, the acoustic encoder, adapter, prediction parameters, and LoRA [22] parameters are updated. The base LLM parameters remain frozen, and the LLM is adapted using LoRA. We use AdamW [23] with zero weight decay. For all mod-els, we use the learning-rate setting that gives the best perfor-mance for DSM w/ <w> . The learning rates are 3 × 10 −4 for the language-model adapters, 10 −5 for the acoustic encoder, 10 −4

for the audio projection and delay-conditioning modules, and

for the streaming-token parameters.

4.2. Speech Recognition Accuracy 

Table 1 compares the three models at a target delay of 480 ms. Removing <w> improves CER on CSJ while changing WER only slightly on LibriSpeech, indicating that explicit word-start pre-diction is not required for competitive recognition in our set-ting. F-DSM further improves the average CER from 6.60% to 6.45% on CSJ and the average WER from 6.61% to 6.17% on LibriSpeech. Fig. 2 compares F-DSM with DSM w/ <w> at target delays of 160–800 ms. F-DSM achieves lower error rates at every eval-uated delay on both datasets, with the largest gap at 160 ms. For F-DSM, error rates decrease as the delay increases to 480 ms, with little further change at longer delays. On CSJ, DSM’s CER rises at 800 ms, whereas F-DSM’s CER remains stable. These results show that F-DSM improves recognition across the evalu-ated delay settings, including the shortest target delay.

4.4. Inference Efficiency 

As described in Sec. 3, during streaming inference, F-DSM first uses the padding prediction head to determine whether the model should output <p> . When <p> is selected, the projection and soft-max over the large LLM vocabulary can be skipped. In our experiments, F-DSM skips the large-vocabulary soft-max on 74.70% of decoding steps on CSJ and 66.89% on Lib-riSpeech, as shown in Table 3. Thus, F-DSM avoids vocabulary computation on most decoding steps. F-DSM achieves the lowest RTF on both datasets (0.811 on CSJ and 0.759 on LibriSpeech), indicating a modest improvement in end-to-end inference speed. The gain is limited because the acoustic encoder and LLM de-coder still need to be computed.

4.5. Analysis of LLM Forgetting 

Fine-tuning LLMs on downstream tasks can lead to catastrophic forgetting of previously learned capabilities [24]. We therefore evaluate text-only PPL to investigate how ASR adaptation af-fects the original language modeling capability of the LLM. The results are shown in Table 4. F-DSM achieves the lowest raw PPL among the adapted models on both datasets, substantially mitigating the degrada-tion observed with DSM. The DSM baselines also assign a large amount of probability mass to ASR-specific tokens during text-only evaluation. On CSJ, the probability mass assigned to special tokens is 51.77% for DSM w/ <w> and 77.97% for DSM w/o <w> , whereas it is only 0.002183% for F-DSM. A similar trend is observed on LibriSpeech. Even after renormalizing the probability distribution over text tokens only, F-DSM achieves the lowest PPL. However, its advantage over both DSM baselines shrinks substantially, and all adapted models still have much higher PPL than the original LLM. These results suggest that gradients from <p> prediction may not be completely isolated from the LLM, even in F-DSM. Jointly providing audio and text embeddings to the LLM may also degrade its language modeling capability. 

5. CONCLUSION 

We proposed F-DSM, which separates the waiting probability for <p> from text-token prediction over the original LLM vo-cabulary. Experiments on CSJ and LibriSpeech showed that the word-start token <w> is not required for competitive recognition accuracy and that F-DSM outperforms the original DSM. F-DSM also greatly reduces GPU memory use while maintaining similar training throughput, and skips the large-vocabulary softmax on 74.70% of decoding steps on CSJ and 66.89% on LibriSpeech with modest gains in end-to-end inference speed. In addition, F-DSM reduces the degradation in text-only PPL observed with DSM, although a gap from the base LLM remains. Separating waiting from text prediction thus improves LLM-based stream-ing ASR. Future work will explore mechanisms to skip LLM inference itself, aiming to improve performance by further miti-gating catastrophic forgetting and to reduce computational cost. 6. REFERENCES
