# Speaker Tracking: Segment-online Multi-talker Organization with a Varying Number of Speakers 

Vahid A. Kalkhorani , Daniel Wong , Jacob Donley , Ashutosh Pandey , Buye Xu , and DeLiang Wang 

Abstract —Speaker tracking is the task of separating and following multiple speakers over time. It must address overlapped speech, speech onset and offset, talker identity, and time-varying speaker count. We propose a segment-online, modular framework for single- and multi-channel speaker tracking. The proposed system first performs speaker separation in each segment and computes speech activity via voice activity detection (VAD). To generate speaker tracks over time, we introduce a two-stage sequential organization strategy: Overlap-based stitching for continuous grouping and memory-based speaker verification for discontinuous grouping. For speaker separation, we employ complex spectral mapping to estimate the real and imaginary spectrograms of underlying speakers. The proposed system achieves state-of-the-art segment-online tracking performance on the LibriCSS and AMI datasets. Our framework significantly reduces diarization error rate (DER) and concatenated minimum-permutation word error rate (cpWER) compared to other methods. 

Index Terms —Speaker tracking, speaker separation, speaker diarization, computational auditory scene analysis, complex spectral mapping. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Speaker tracking is the broad task of separating overlapped talkers and following them over an extended period. Inspired by auditory scene analysis principles [1], we formulate speaker tracking as two related processes: simultaneous grouping or organization, and sequential organization (see also [2]). The simultaneous process deals with an acoustic mixture over a short time, or a segment of typically a few seconds long. When multiple speakers are active concurrently, simultaneous organi-zation aims to separate this mixture into simultaneous streams, each corresponding to one speaker in the segment. On the other hand, sequential organization integrates simultaneous streams across segments to form speaker tracks, each corresponding to a distinct speaker over a longer period of time (such as a meeting). Sequential organization provides the mechanism of grouping the discontinuous utterances of the same talker. Human listeners are remarkably adept at tracking speakers in noisy and dynamic environments [3], which is essentially the cocktail party problem [4]. In contrast, computational speaker tracking remains a formidable challenge [5, 6]. Real-world acoustic scenes include background noise and room

Overlapped speech from concurrent talkers further complicates the task by blending the talker characteristics of overlapped speakers. In addition, speaker tracking has to maintain talker identity across discontinuous time periods, hence requiring a form of memory for extended durations and speaker modeling, and detecting new speakers. Speaker tracking is related to, but broader than, speaker separation and speaker diarization. Speaker separation focuses on isolating concurrent voices within a specific segment, corresponding to simultaneous organization [7]. Speaker di-arization is capable of linking same-talker utterances across time [8, 9]. A recent diarization study also uses the term speaker tracking to denote following many speakers over time [10]. However, diarization systems usually do not deal with overlapped speech, or label it as belonging to more than one talker without separating the underlying signals. Speaker tracking inherently addresses both tasks, separating overlapped speech and diarizing separated speech. The objective of speaker tracking is to generate the separated and enhanced speech signal of each speaker throughout their presence in an acoustic scene. Deep-learning-based speaker separation has witnessed major advances in recent years [7]. Models such as deep cluster-ing [11] and permutation invariant training (PIT) [12] have suc-cessfully addressed the permutation ambiguity issue in talker-independent separation in monaural settings. Subsequently, time-domain methods, including Conv-TasNet [13], were intro-duced to replace the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with learnable encoders. A recent trend is the resurgence of time-frequency (T-F) domain approaches based on complex spectral mapping (CSM), which trains a deep neural network (DNN) to predict real and imaginary spectrograms [14]. CSM can fundamentally represent both signal magnitude and phase, and hence naturally encodes inter-channel phase relations of sound sources in multi-channel recordings, providing an important spatial dimension. CSM models, such as TF-GridNet [15], SpatialNet [16], and TF-CrossNet [17], have achieved state-of-the-art speaker separation performance. To move toward real-world applicability, subsequent work has proposed continuous speaker separation (CSS) to separate speaker streams in a conversational setting [18–23]. CSS pro-cesses a sliding sequence of partially overlapped segments and outputs a fixed number of overlap-free streams, irrespective of the total speaker count. CSS addresses simultaneous grouping within each segment and stitches the same-talker signal across consecutive segments, but does not perform sequential grouping across discontinuous segments. Consequently, a single output stream may contain speech from multiple speakers at different arXiv:2610.05693v1 [eess.AS] 5 Oct 2026 2

times. Such sequential grouping requires a separate diarization backend. Previous systems have combined CSS and speaker diarization [24–27]. Yoshioka et al. [24] integrate CSS with online audio-visual diarization in a separate-recognize-diarize pipeline, but rely on a camera feed and a prior list of meeting attendees. Von Neumann et al. [26] combine CSS with offline d-vector clustering and ASR-supported speaker turn detection, which achieves strong concatenated minimum-permutation word error rate (cpWER) on LibriCSS, but operates offline. Traditional speaker diarization pipelines use voice activity de-tection (VAD), embedding vector extraction, and clustering [28– 30]. While effective offline, these systems often assume a fixed speaker count and struggle with speaker overlaps. Overlap-aware methods, including end-to-end neural diarization (EEND) models, jointly perform detection and attribution [31–33], with extensions to unknown speaker counts [34–36]. Nevertheless, diarization models do not separate overlapped speech signals, limiting their utility for downstream tasks such as ASR. An alternative to CSS, termed speaker separation via neural diarization (SSND) [37], employs an offline EEND to estimate speech activities and condition a downstream speaker separation model. Assuming a maximum of two overlapped talkers at each time-frame, the speaker separation model in this framework outputs two continuous streams regardless of the total speaker count. SSND extracts speaker embeddings from non-overlapped segments identified by the diarization module. These embeddings, combined with the diarization results, guide the assignment of separated signals to the output streams as a post-processing step. DCF-DS [38] further integrates diarization and separation through a deep cascade fusion mechanism, allowing each module to condition on the other, and was recently extended to exploit multi-channel spatial information [39]. However, like SSND, DCF-DS is an offline model and needs prior speaker diarization results. Despite these advances, a significant research gap remains between the current separation and diarization results and online speaker tracking. Existing systems often rely on offline backends for sequential organization or require prior speaker enrollment to guide separation. Little prior research aims to provide a unified system for single-channel and multi-channel segment-online speaker tracking without assuming a fixed number of speakers. Addressing these limitations would require a broad framework that deals with both simultaneous and sequential organization. Sequential organization would need to be mediated by a form of working memory, referring to temporary storage of information for performing complex tasks [40]. This paper tackles the speaker tracking challenge by developing a segment-online, modular framework capable of both simultaneous and sequential organization with an unknown number of speakers. Simultaneous organization within a segment will be conducted through speaker separation based on complex spectral mapping, producing simultaneous streams. We propose to address sequential organization across different segments in two stages. The first stage performs overlap-based stitching to organize simultaneous streams, to extend simultaneous streams across consecutive segments. The second stage sequentially organizes discontinuous simultaneous streams into speaker tracks by introducing a working memory mechanism based on speaker verification. The proposed two-stage approach addresses the limitations of cascade systems: Stitching based on spectral overlap maintains the temporal continuity of speech from one segment to the next, and memory-driven verification maintains talker identity over extended periods of no speech activity. Unlike the SSND and DCF-DS models, our framework groups the discontinuous speech signals of the same talker without a separate offline backend or prior speaker enrollment. We present both single-channel and multi-channel tracking algorithms. In single-channel scenarios, tracking relies on spectral, temporal, and talker cues. In the multi-channel setup, tracking further exploits spatial cues to improve both simultaneous and sequential organization. We systematically evaluate the proposed framework on the LibriCSS [18] and AMI [41] datasets. The primary contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. First, we propose a segment-online speaker tracking framework that integrates speaker separation and sequential organization in a modular fashion. Second, we propose a two-stage sequential organization approach, where a working mem-ory mechanism is utilized to organize temporally-discontinuous utterances into distinct speakers. Third, the proposed single-channel and multi-channel speaker tracking algorithms advance the state-of-the-art performance on the LibriCSS and AMI datasets. The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section II describes the proposed speaker tracking framework. Section III presents the experimental setup. Section IV describes the evaluation and comparison results. Section V concludes the paper. II. M ODEL DESCRIPTION

Following separation, we compute frame-level activity traces for each separated source using a pre-trained VAD model, denoted as FVAD in Fig. 1. These activity labels are used to remove speaker separation leakage errors, determine which speakers are active in the overlap interval between consecutive segments, and to restrict comparisons during overlap-based stitching. C. Sequential organization in consecutive segments via stitch-ing

Existing buffer-based methods commonly store mixture signals or embeddings for repeated evaluations. Examples include the speaker-tracing buffer in SA-EEND [45], block-wise EEND [46], and the arrival-order speaker cache in streaming Sortformer [47]. These methods concatenate incom-ing features with buffered representations and reprocess the entire sequence for each new chunk. This repeated processing increases computational cost and introduces variable-length inputs that require variable chunk-size training [46]. In addition, feature buffers may contain overlapped speech depending on the frame selection policy, and the total number of output speakers may be restricted. In contrast, our WM mechanism maintains compact, speaker-specific embedding profiles extracted from separated speech. Our framework only limits the maximum number of simultaneously active speakers (i.e., K), and allows the total number of global speaker tracks to grow dynamically. Leveraging continuous grouping via stitching and discontinuous grouping via speaker profiling, our approach completely avoids repeated inference over past segments. 5

E. Implementation details 

We adopt TF-CrossNet [17] as the speaker separation model. TF-CrossNet performs CSM in the T-F domain and predicts the real and imaginary parts of each speaker’s spectrogram. The TF-CrossNet architecture comprises five modules: (i) an encoder layer to project mixture real and imaginary (RI) representations; (ii) a global multi-head self-attention (GMHSA) module to model cross-frame and cross-band dependencies; (iii) a cross-band module to capture inter-frequency correlations across all channels; (iv) a narrow-band module to model fine-grained structure within adjacent frequency bins; and (v) a decoder that yields separated spectrograms, which are converted back to time-domain waveforms via inverse STFT [17]. TF-CrossNet also incorporates random-chunk positional encoding (RCPE) to mitigate performance degradation on long utterances and improve temporal generalization [17]; however, we do not utilize RCPE here as we operate on relatively short segments. See [17] for further details. For evaluations on the AMI dataset, the speaker separation model is further fine-tuned on the AMI training set for 10 epochs following training on a simulated dataset (see Sec. III-A). We utilize three pre-trained VAD models: Pyannote [48], Silero VAD 1, and WebRTC 2. Pyannote employs a neural sequence labeling architecture trained on a diverse set of con-versational recordings and provides frame-level speech activity scores. Silero is a lightweight, real-time model optimized for low-latency applications, producing binary decisions at high temporal resolution. WebRTC is a signal processing based model that classifies short frames as speech or non-speech using a Gaussian mixture model, and is computationally efficient. For the stitching mechanism for continuous grouping, we use the negative scale-invariant signal-to-distortion ratio (SI-SDR) as the distance measure D in Eq. 7, and set the threshold γ to 

−5 dB .We experiment with five speaker verification models: NeMo [49], ReDimNet [44], ReDimNet2 [50], Speech-Brain [51], and Wespeaker [52]. Each model extracts a fixed-dimensional embedding from a speech segment, which is then compared to previously-tracked speakers the using cosine similarity in Eq. 10. If the maximal similarity score exceeds a predefined threshold, the speech segment is added to the track of the closest talker; otherwise, a new track is initiated. To determine the optimal threshold θ for each model, we evaluate speaker verification accuracy on 3000 speaker pairs randomly sampled from LibriSpeech development subset [53], with positive (same-speaker) and negative (different-speaker) pairs sampled with equal probability. We then perform a grid search over θ ranging from 0.05 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, and select the threshold that yields the highest verification accuracy. For ReDimNet, we utilize the pre-trained M-sized model, which is trained on the combined VoxBlink2 [54], VoxCeleb2 [55], and CN-Celeb [56] datasets 3, and for ReDimNet2 we select the pre-trained B6 model which is trained on VoxCeleb2 dataset 4.

A. Datasets 

To train the proposed speaker tracking system, we generate simulated mixtures that reflect the acoustic characteristics of two evaluation datasets: the LibriCSS [18] and the AMI [41] meeting datasets. For both configurations, each mixture includes at most four speakers drawn from the train-clean-100 and 

train-clean-360 subsets of LibriSpeech [53]. Room impulse responses (RIRs) are synthesized using the gpuRIR [57] toolkit, with 100,000 and 2,500 rooms simulated for the training and validation sets, respectively. For the LibriCSS simulation, room dimensions are sampled uniformly with lengths and widths ranging from 3 to 8 m and heights from 3 to 4 m. The reverberation time (RT 60 ) is drawn from a truncated Gaussian distribution ( μ = 0 .4 s , σ = 0 .12 s )bounded between 0.1 and 0.6 s. We construct a 7-channel microphone array corresponding to the LibriCSS hardware geometry (radius 4.25 cm ), positioned within 1.0 m of the room center at an elevation between 1.0 and 1.5 m. Speakers are distributed at radial distances of 0.5 to 4.5 m from the array, with heights ranging from 1.0 to 1.5 m. To ensure sufficient spatial separability, a minimum azimuth difference of 5◦ is maintained across all sources. Following the LibriCSS recipe [18] 5, we simulate meeting-style conversations where two to four speakers each provide two utterances from the LibriSpeech training set [53]. Each conversation is simulated as follows. First, an utterance is randomly drawn from the set of the utterances from all speakers in a conversation. Then a silence of a random duration between 0.5 s to 3.0 s is added with a 26% probability. If a silence is added, the next utterance is drawn from the set of the remaining utterances. If no silence is added, the next utterance is drawn from the set of the remaining utterances with the condition that the drawn utterance cannot be from the same talker as the current utterance. Then the current and next utterance are mixed with a speaker overlap ratio randomly selected from 0% to 45%. This process continues until all the utterances of the set are used up. The simulated meetings are partitioned into 

8 s segments for speaker separation model training. We evaluate speaker tracking in both session- and section-wise scenarios. In session-wise evaluation, each ten-minute mini-session signal is processed directly. For section-wise evaluation, we utilize the pre-segmented LibriCSS sections. These sections range from 5 to 120 seconds in length and contain between 2 and 10 utterances from up to 8 distinct speakers. For AMI data simulation, we generate RIRs modeling standard conference rooms. Room dimensions are sampled uniformly with lengths and widths spanning 3 to 10 m and heights from 3 to 4 m. RT 60 is drawn from the same truncated Gaussian distribution but bounded between 0.2 and 0.7 s to reflect heavier reverberation. We simulate a single microphone

A. Results on LibriCSS 

We first evaluate our system on the LibriCSS dataset using DER, and cpWER as metrics. Table I reports DER results across all overlap conditions. Existing comparison approaches fall into two categories: (i) systems that apply a separation or beamforming front-end followed by diarization, and (ii) systems that directly perform diarization, i.e., without front-end separation. The first category includes mask-based MVDR [18], MIMO-BF-MISO [61], and MISO-BF-MISO [19], which combine spatial filtering and clustering-based diarization (i.e., x-vector + spectral clustering). While effective in reducing overlap-caused errors, these systems are not designed for online operation. In addition, LlaSep [62] performs generative separation us-ing an autoregressive audio language model and determines speaker activity from the separated streams via energy-based thresholding, achieving an average DER of 23 .43 % . The second category consists of diarization models without speaker separation, including x-vector + SC, AHC, VBx [25], and SC-EEND [37]. These models are simpler but tend to degrade more severely under high overlap conditions due to the absence of a separation stage. Competitive methods include RPN and TS-VAD [25], which leverage speaker-specific enrollment and target speaker detection networks. These methods achieve low DER (e.g., 7.6 % for TS-VAD) but rely on oracle knowledge of the number of speakers, an assumption not made in our framework. The proposed single-channel model achieves 4.63% DER. This represents an absolute reduction of more than 13.5% relative to the x-vector + SC baseline [25] ( 18 .19 % ). The proposed seven-channel model further reduces the DER to the new state-of-the-art score of 3.38%, outperforming the previous best MC-EEND [37] ( 4.68 % ), despite MC-EEND operating offline. To isolate the effects of speaker separation from subsequent sequential organization, we report results under an “Oracle” condition. Compared to DCF-DS [38], our segment-online system avoids full-utterance context dependencies. DCF-DS requires a global diarization prior to segment the audio before window-based processing. TMS runs an online ASR system on the raw mixture to generate preliminary transcripts as text prompts. It then isolates the acoustic signal that aligns with the prompt, treating other voices as background interference. This method involves a large language model and operates offline. 

B. Results on AMI 

We now evaluate our system on the AMI IHM-Mix meeting corpus using DER as the metric. Compared to LibriCSS, the AMI corpus introduces severe challenges due to spontaneous conversational dynamics and real-room acoustics. AMI contains frequent short speech bursts that complicate speaker tracking. Furthermore, real-world reverberation and background noise degrade speaker separation quality. Additionally, AMI meetings average 33 minutes in duration, posing significant challenges for online diarization systems to maintain speaker identity over extended periods. Table III reports the performances of the proposed and comparison systems. Among offline methods, AED-EEND-EE with Conformer [64] achieves the lowest DER of 13 .00 % .Among online methods, our speaker tracking system obtains the best DER of 18.43%, outperforming LS-EEND [36] and other online alternatives. These results demonstrate the robustness of our speaker tracking framework in realistic meeting scenarios.

We have proposed a segment-online system for speaker tracking. The system performs speaker separation for si-multaneous grouping, yielding the estimates of overlapped speakers. These estimates are further organized sequentially into coherent speakers, each corresponding to a distinct talker. Sequential organization is performed in two stages. The first stage employs overlap-based stitching for continuous organization and the second stage uses working memory based on speaker verification for discontinuous organization. Experimental results show our system advances the state-of-the-art in speaker diarization on LibriCSS. On AMI, we achieve the best diarization results in the segment-online setting. Our system also obtains better speaker-attributed ASR results than other methods, and competitive performance to offline methods. For future work, we plan to explore frame-online processing to enhance real-time processing capability. We also plan to address track fragmentation and false-alarm errors that degrade speaker count accuracy as revealed in Fig. 2. VI. A CKNOWLEDGMENT
