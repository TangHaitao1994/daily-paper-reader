ABSTRACT
The choice of post-training data for large language models substantially affects
downstream performance. Gradient-based data selection is a popular approach
that ranks training data by how well their gradients align with those of a small
validation set. However, ranking with full-parameter gradients requires an expen-
sive backward pass on every sample, making computation intractable for large
candidate pools. This raises a natural question: can we approximate full-gradient
features at a fraction of the cost? Conveniently, we find that output-layer gradients
suffice for effective data selection, yet require only the cheaper forward pass. We
implement this as LESSER, a drop-in wrapper for selection methods that reduces
the feature-extraction FLOP cost by 9.7× for SFT and 3.0× for RL benchmarks,
while tracking full-gradient performance on downstream tasks. Empirically, we
find that even when output-layer and full gradients rank individual samples differ-
ently, they select batches with aligned gradients.

INTRODUCTION
The choice of post-training data substantially shapes the performance of large language models
(LLMs) (Guo et al., 2025; Ouyang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023). In particular, training on small,
high-quality subsets tailored to a target task can match or even outperform training on a larger, gen-
eral pool (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2024). However, identifying which subset to use is
non-trivial, as the utility of each sample depends on the model and target task, and selection can be-
come expensive when the candidate pool grows. These challenges have motivated the development
of data selection methods for identifying targeted, high-quality subsets (Killamsetty et al., 2021b).
A common approach to targeted data selection maps candidate and query examples into a shared
feature space and selects candidates by their similarity to the queries (Zhang et al., 2025). When

choosing features, gradients are a natural choice because they describe how each training sample
would update the model. This principle underlies modern LLM data selection methods such as
LESS (Xia et al., 2024) and GIST (Min et al., 2026) for SFT, GradAlign (Yang et al., 2026) for RL,
and GRACE (Panigrahi et al., 2025) for distillation teacher selection.
However, full-parameter gradients are costly to compute at scale: each candidate requires a back-
ward pass through the LLM. For large candidate pools, extraction can cost more than training on the
selected data itself (Yin & Rush, 2024). This has motivated more compute-efficient features, such
as RDS+ (Ivison et al., 2025), which uses hidden-state features, and proxy-model methods such
as Yang et al. (2024b), which use smaller models to guide selection. Despite their cost, however,
gradient-based features often yield superior subsets (Nayak et al., 2026). We thus aim to approxi-
mate gradients at a fraction of the cost.
In particular, output-layer gradients are attractive because they can be computed directly from last-
layer hidden states and output probabilities available during a forward pass, without backpropagating
through the model. These features have also proved useful in other settings, including active learning
for vision models (Ash et al., 2019), data valuation (Deng et al., 2025), and data attribution (Ran
et al., 2026) methods. We find that they are also effective for post-training data selection.

OUR CONTRIBUTIONS
We propose LESSER (Last-layer Error Signals for Scalable Example Ranking), a drop-in wrapper
for gradient-based data selection methods that only swaps in output-layer gradients, leaving the
selection rule unchanged. We implement this for LESS in SFT data selection, for GradAlign in RL
problem selection, and for GRACE in distillation teacher selection.
LESSER is cost-efficient.
Extracting output-layer gradients is cost-efficient, requiring 9.7×
fewer FLOPs on SFT and 3.0× fewer FLOPs on RL. This effect is more pronounced in our wall-
clock experiments (Section 4), where LESSER reduces feature-extraction time by 17.8× in SFT,
the added scoring time per selection round by 3.5× in RL, and feature-extraction time by 3.9–5.4×
in distillation teacher selection.
LESSER tracks full-gradient performance.
Across a variety of setups, output-layer gradients
find subsets that reach downstream performance similar to that of subsets found with full gradients.
• SFT. Across five SFT tasks and four LLMs, the absolute downstream score gap between
LESSER and LESS averages only 1.3 points, growing to 2.2 against RDS+, and to 2.6 against
a random selection baseline. Relative to LESS-selected batches, the centered gradient cosine
similarity is 0.29 for LESSER, compared with 0.03 for RDS+ and approximately zero for Ran-
dom. At the base model, the batch gradients of LESSER selections thus align with those of
LESS more than other baselines do.
• RL. In RL, the problems LESSER and GradAlign select in the first round have a Jaccard simi-
larity of 0.53, compared to the random baseline’s 0.075. After training, LESSER and GradAlign
reach the same final test accuracy.
• Distillation. Across three different distillation settings, LESSER consistently selects the same
top teacher from a pool of 10 to 14 teachers as the unmodified GRACE. This suggests that
GRACE’s teacher choice is robust to replacing full gradients with output-layer gradients.

RELATED WORK
Targeted data selection.
Data selection methods differ in the notion of usefulness they aim to
capture. Quality filtering and diversity-based curation seek examples that are broadly useful for
instruction tuning (Chen et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2023). Other methods select
data relative to a reference distribution or dataset: DSIR (Xie et al., 2023) matches a target text
distribution for pretraining, while CRAIG (Mirzasoleiman et al., 2020) constructs weighted sub-
sets that approximate the full training-set gradient. Selection can also be tied directly to a training
objective: GLISTER (Killamsetty et al., 2021b) optimizes held-out validation performance, while
GRAD-MATCH (Killamsetty et al., 2021a) matches the gradient of the training or validation set.

For targeted post-training, LESS (Xia et al., 2024) uses gradient alignment and RDS+ (Ivison et al.,
2025) uses hidden-state similarity. GIST (Min et al., 2026) projects gradients into a subspace de-
fined by validation gradients, while GradAlign (Yang et al., 2026) uses policy-gradient alignment
for online RL selection. Systematic evaluations show that selection gains vary across datasets and
budgets (Diddee & Ippolito, 2025; Nayak et al., 2026), and that the cost of selection can outweigh
its training gains (Yin & Rush, 2024). We instead focus on the efficiency of data selection pipelines,
and show that output-layer gradients are a cost-efficient substitute for full-gradient features.
Efficient gradient representations.
Gradient-based attribution connects training examples to
their effects on model predictions. Koh & Liang (2017) use influence functions to estimate the
effect of upweighting a training example, while TracIn accumulates gradient inner products across
training checkpoints (Pruthi et al., 2020). To make attribution scalable, TRAK (Park et al., 2023)
combines model linearization with random projection, while LoGra (Choe et al., 2025) exploits the
outer-product structure of layer gradients to reduce projection costs. These approaches compress
gradient features but still require backpropagation. Restricting the gradient to the last layer is an-
other approach, used in GLISTER (Killamsetty et al., 2021b) and GRAD-MATCH (Killamsetty
et al., 2021a) for subset selection and in BADGE (Ash et al., 2019) for active learning.
Closely related, For-Value (Deng et al., 2025) derives a forward-only valuation score from output-
gradient inner products, while RISE (Ran et al., 2026) combines sketches of this signal with an
additional feature that maps prediction residuals into hidden-state space. Both methods target val-
uation or attribution, which score individual examples, and treat the output-layer score as the value
itself or as an approximation of full-gradient influence, respectively. We instead keep existing SFT,
RL, and distillation teacher selectors (Panigrahi et al., 2025) unchanged, replace only their gradient
features, and evaluate the selections downstream. In SFT especially, output-layer and full gradients
rank individual examples differently, yet their selections perform similarly downstream. This dif-
fers from the per-example view taken by prior work, under which weaker agreement on individual
examples should yield weaker selections; Section 5 explains it through the aligned gradients of the
selected batches.

BACKGROUND
2.1
FEATURE-BASED DATA SELECTION
We consider targeted data selection from a training pool D = {p1, . . . , pN} using a small query set
Q = {q1, . . . , qM} drawn from the target test distribution. We refer to examples in Q as queries,
since they specify the target task and guide which training examples are selected. Given a budget k,
the goal is to choose a subset S ⊆D of size k such that training on S improves performance on the
target distribution, with Q serving as the available proxy for this target during selection.
Which subset S should we choose? Many targeted data selection methods answer this question
using a feature-based pipeline. Each candidate p ∈D and query q ∈Q is mapped to a feature
vector ϕp and ϕq in a shared representation space. A similarity function sim(ϕp, ϕq) then measures
how relevant candidate p is to query q. This separates two design choices: the feature representation
and the selection rule, which together are used to extract a size-k training subset from the pool.
The same basic pipeline applies across SFT and RL (Figure 2): (1) compute features for the can-
didate pool and query set; (2) compare candidate and query features to obtain relevance scores; (3)
apply a selection rule to these scores to choose a size-k subset S; and (4) train the model on the
selected subset. The precise selection rule can differ across settings. In this work, we hold the
selection procedure fixed within each setting and study the choice of feature representation.
2.2
GRADIENT FEATURES
The feature-based view leaves open the central question of what information the representation ϕp
should preserve. A natural goal is to represent each candidate according to how training on it would
affect performance on the target task. We express this effect through a loss function, whose precise
form depends on the post-training setting.

LESSER replaces the gradient representation within this pipeline.
In SFT, each example p = (x, y) consists of a prompt x and target response y. Let πθ(y | x)
denote the model’s probability of the response under parameters θ. The supervised response loss is
Lp(θ) = −log πθ(y | x). The same loss is defined for pool and query examples. In RL, an example
p is instead a problem whose sampled responses and rewards determine a policy-optimization loss
Lp(θ). Following the online selection setup of GradAlign (Yang et al., 2026), this loss is constructed
from rollouts under the current policy and changes as the policy is updated. In either setting, each
candidate and query is therefore associated with a scalar training objective, giving us a common way
to describe the effect of training on that example.
Gradients provide a natural feature for these objectives: they describe the local parameter update
induced by an example, and their alignment has a direct first-order interpretation in terms of how
training on one example affects another. For a candidate p ∈D and query q ∈Q, define
Gp = ∇θLp(θ),
Gq = ∇θLq(θ).
Consider taking one SGD step on candidate p with learning rate η > 0. Then θ′ = θ −ηGp, and a
first-order Taylor expansion of the query loss gives
Lq(θ′) −Lq(θ) ≈∇θLq(θ)⊤(θ′ −θ) = −ηG⊤
q Gp.
Thus, when G⊤
q Gp > 0, training on p is predicted to reduce the loss on query q to first order.
Standard gradient inner products then give a natural notion of similarity. Because gradient mag-
nitude can vary with factors such as sequence length, methods such as LESS (Xia et al., 2024)
further perform normalization or use cosine similarity instead. Moreover, since Gp ∈R|θ| has one
coordinate per model parameter, gradient features are often projected to a lower-dimensional space
for efficient storage and similarity computation (Park et al., 2023; Xia et al., 2024). However, the
main drawback of gradient features is computational cost: obtaining a full-model gradient requires
backpropagating through the entire model for every scored example.

in settings such as active learning (Ash et al., 2019) and data attribution (Deng et al., 2025; Ran

by methods such as LESS with output-layer gradients while leaving the selection rule unchanged.
Crucially, in our SFT and RL settings, these output-layer gradients can be computed from forward-

LESSER IS EFFECTIVE AT TARGETED DATA SELECTION
We evaluate LESSER across targeted SFT, RL, and distillation teacher selection. We compare its
effectiveness with the corresponding full-gradient methods and measure the resulting computational
savings.
SFT.
We take 197,196 Tulu V2 (Ivison et al., 2023) instruction–response samples as our candidate
pool, and take TyDiQA, MMLU-Pro, GSM8K, Codex, and BBH as our query and test sets, following
prior work (Ivison et al., 2025; Nayak et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2024). For data-selection methods, we
use LESSER, LESS, Random (wherein samples are randomly chosen), and RDS+ (hidden-state
based), where selection is done with a standard round-robin rule until k samples are chosen. For
selection sizes k ∈{1,000, 5,000, 10,000}, we fully fine-tune Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.2-3B, Qwen3-
4B-Base, and OLMo3-7B on each selected subset with three seeds. We show Llama-2-7B in the
main text and report the full model–task–budget grid in Appendix C.1. Baseline results are from
Nayak et al. (2026) except for MMLU-Pro, where we rerun all methods under our evaluation setup.
Another caveat is that LESSER uses only the base model, while LESS uses Adam-preconditioned
LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) gradients at four warmup checkpoints for computational tractability and
empirical improvements. The two pipelines therefore differ beyond the feature, but LESSER is
much cheaper and more convenient to extract. Appendix A.1 gives the full training protocol and
replication checks.

loss should increase as we move from higher- to lower-similarity bins, giving a positive Spearman
correlation between bin index and query loss.

RL.
We evaluate LESSER in two GradAlign settings: avoiding problems with corrupted rewards
and selecting problems during online RL training. In both settings, we replace only the full-gradient
representation with output-layer gradients while keeping the selection procedure fixed. We first test
whether LESSER can avoid problems whose rewards carry no useful learning signal. Following
Yang et al. (2026), we corrupt the rewards of half the candidate problems with coin flips and select
a subset against AMC22 queries.
Finding 3: LESSER closely matches full policy gradients in avoiding problems with corrupted
rewards. LESSER selects 29.7% corrupted problems, close to 27.3% for full gradients and well
below Random (Figure 5). AccGreedy, which selects problems whose empirical accuracy is closest
to 50%, instead favors corrupted problems because their coin-flip rewards push empirical accuracy
toward 50%. Appendix A.2.2 gives the full protocol.
Another setting is in online RL, where in our case data selection happens after every 10 policy
gradient updates. We target Countdown using a mixed-domain training pool in which only 11.8%
of the problems are from Countdown, with the remainder from WebInstruct (Yue et al., 2024) and
DAPO (Yu et al., 2026). Across five selection rounds, we compare LESSER with full-gradient
GradAlign, dynamic sampling (Yu et al., 2026), which retains problems with nonzero rollout ad-
vantage, and Random, while training Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct with GRPO (Shao et al., 2024).

LESSER closely matches full policy gradients in targeted online RL training.

Cost analysis.
We give detailed accounting in Appendix A.4, with highlights in Table 1 and below.
SFT.
LESSER extracts features with a single forward pass, whereas the LESS setup used in our
experiments extracts LoRA-gradient features with full backpropagation at four checkpoints. Count-
ing FLOPs using LESS’s configuration, this reduces feature-extraction FLOPs by 9.7× and, ex-
trapolating our A100 measurements to the full candidate pool, wall-clock extraction time by 17.8×.
Even against a single LESS checkpoint, extraction is 4.4× faster in wall clock. Feature extraction
accounts for 97% to 99.7% of LESS’s combined extraction and fine-tuning FLOPs across our tested
budgets, making it the dominant computational cost.
RL.
Full-gradient GradAlign requires a separate forward–backward scoring pass after rollout gen-
eration, whereas LESSER scores with projected output-layer features, which need only a forward
pass. This reduces scoring FLOPs by 3.0×. On the responses of one selection round, the added
scoring time falls from 63.3 to 18.1 minutes on one A100, a 3.5× reduction.
Distillation.
Full-gradient GRACE backpropagates through the student for every teacher response,
whereas LESSER requires only a forward pass. Across the three teacher-selection settings, this
reduces feature-extraction time by 3.9 to 5.4× while also lowering peak GPU memory.

WHY ARE OUTPUT-LAYER GRADIENTS EFFECTIVE?
Since LESSER often matches full-gradient methods downstream, we ask why it can do so using
just the output-layer gradient. In GRACE the two methods rank teachers almost identically and in
RL they largely agree, but in SFT their selections differ. We therefore ask whether LESSER and
LESS might still update the model in similar directions. At the base model where selection happens,
we find that batches selected by LESSER and LESS produce aligned full-model training gradients,
much more so than RDS+ or Random. In addition, both LESSER and LESS achieve most of their
query-loss decrease within the first ten updates.

In SFT, we test whether the similar downstream

At k = 5,000 on Llama-2-7B, the selected sets have mean Jaccard similarity 0.037 (Appendix E).
In an audit across the 20 model–task pairs, output-layer and full-gradient rankings have median
Spearman correlation 0.52 (Appendix F.1). This level of disagreement is specific to SFT: in RL, the
two selections share 69% of their problems in the first round, while in GRACE the statistics used for
teacher selection remain highly correlated (Appendix F.4). This leaves the SFT result unexplained,
so we turn to how the selected examples affect training and ask whether the different selections
induce similar updates.
The selected batches align.
We first characterize how a selected batch affects training. For a batch
S, let GS denote its full-model gradient at the base model θ0. Our goal is to compare the training
directions induced by two selections, which requires us to exclude gradient components that are
common across the candidate pool. In particular, even independently sampled random batches can
have high cosine similarity because their gradients share the population mean. We observe this effect
empirically in Appendix F.2. We therefore subtract the expected gradient µ of a random batch and
compare the centered gradients GS −µ and GS′ −µ using the centered batch-gradient alignment
defined in Appendix F.2. This measures whether two selections emphasize similar training directions
beyond random sampling.
We evaluate gradient alignment using the actual training batch size of 128 across 20 model–task
pairs (Figure 7a). As a reference for the scale of this measurement, we first compare different LESS
batches drawn from the same k = 5,000 selection; their median alignment is 0.53. Against this
reference, LESSER batches show substantial alignment with LESS, with a median alignment of
0.29. In contrast, the median alignment with LESS is 0.03 for RDS+ and approximately zero for
Random. Thus, under the batch size used in training, LESSER batches align with LESS batches
far more than RDS+ or random batches do.
The effect of selection happens early in training.
Above, we showed that LESSER and LESS
select batches whose full-model gradients are aligned at the base model, despite selecting largely
different examples. The remaining question is when selection affects the subsequent training run.
We therefore track query loss throughout the full 80-update SFT runs.
The query-loss gains from selection are concentrated early in training. By step 10, LESSER and
LESS have achieved a median 84% and 82%, respectively, of their maximum query-loss decrease
over the full 80 updates, compared with 35% for Random (Figure 7b). This connects the first-order
selection at the base model to the reduction in query loss.

Discussion.
Our results suggest three broader lessons.
First, compared to hidden-state features, output-layer gradients preserve more of the signal used by
full-gradient data-selection methods at a similar extraction cost. They are also more directly tied to
the optimization objective, making them a natural starting point when extraction cost matters.
Second, agreement is strongest at the level each pipeline acts on. SFT trains on batch-aggregated
gradients, RL selects problems after aggregating sampled responses, and GRACE ranks teachers

Third, full-gradient fidelity may therefore be the wrong objective for evaluating a selection proxy.

Limitations.
Our experiments span multiple models, tasks, and selection settings, but do not cover
all post-training regimes or selection objectives. We also focus primarily on near-initialization train-
ing effects, leaving a fuller understanding of how output-layer gradient signals evolve over longer
training trajectories to future work. Our SFT comparison is between complete pipelines rather than
a matched-checkpoint ablation, so LESSER and LESS differ in more than just their features. Nev-
ertheless, LESSER needs neither warmup training nor backpropagation, and it remains competitive
with this more expensive pipeline.
Future Work.
Future work could develop diagnostics that predict, before running selection, when
output-layer gradients will recover the rankings or update directions induced by full gradients. A
second direction is to identify which computations inside the model make this agreement possible,
for example by tracing how selection-relevant information is transformed across layers. Finally, a
stronger theory could characterize when such agreement should emerge under realistic, structured
gradient distributions.
Conclusion.
We study whether output-layer gradients can replace full-gradient features for data
selection in LLM post-training. Across SFT, RL, and distillation teacher selection, they often match
full-gradient methods downstream at substantially lower cost. In RL and GRACE, the resulting
selection statistics largely agree, while in SFT, different rankings nevertheless induce aligned batch
updates. Output-layer gradients therefore offer a simple, efficient alternative to full gradients.

Setup.
We evaluate these per-example scores on 20 model–task pairs. For each pair, we sample up

Setup.
We compare the full-model gradients induced by batches from different selections at the
base model θ0. Let GS be the gradient of the recipe’s token-averaged loss on batch S, and let µ
be the expected gradient of a uniformly sampled batch of the same size. To measure alignment
beyond this shared random-batch direction, we use the centered batch-gradient alignment between
the batches S of one method and the batches S′ of another

µ = E[GS] is unknown, we estimate it from the random samples that are not part of the comparison,

For each of the 20 model–task pairs, we sample 1,024 examples from the k = 5,000 selections of
LESSER, LESS, RDS+, and Random. We also draw three additional uniform samples of 1,024
examples from the candidate pool, giving four random samples in total.
We use these samples to construct two baselines in Figure 7a. The Random baseline compares each
random sample with LESS, while the LESS baseline compares two disjoint batches drawn from the
same LESS selection.
Findings.
Raw batch gradients can be strongly aligned even under random sampling: at batch size
128, two random batches have median cosine 0.81 across the 20 model–task pairs. Thus, raw cosine
largely reflects a direction shared by typical training batches, rather than the effect of selection itself.
We therefore center each batch gradient by the expected random-batch gradient.
Under this centered measure, LESSER batches remain substantially aligned with LESS batches.
At batch size 128, their similarity is positive in 17 of 20 pairs and typically exceeds the RDS+ and
random baselines (Figure 7a). Across batch sizes 64–512, the median over model–task pairs of the
ratio between the LESSER–LESS similarity and the similarity between two disjoint LESS batches
is 0.58–0.66.

F.4
RL AND GRACE STATISTICS
RL.
We analyze the first selection round of the Countdown experiment, restricting to the 1,835 of
5,120 candidate problems with nonzero advantage. Each problem aggregates 16 sampled responses,
and we compare its LESSER and full-gradient scores at the base policy. The two rankings have
Spearman correlation ρ = 0.69. At the selection budget, they select 69% of the same problems, a
Jaccard similarity of 0.53 against 0.075 for two random selections.
GRACE.
We analyze the 14 teachers in the GSM8K experiment with the Llama-3.2-1B student.
For each teacher, GRACE summarizes the student’s gradients on 2,048 teacher responses using
statistics of their spectrum and pairwise similarities. Replacing full gradients with output-layer gra-
dients largely preserves these teacher-level quantities: across teachers, spectral entropy and effective
rank have Spearman correlations 0.94 and 0.93, while the remaining aggregate statistics have corre-
lations 0.75–0.98. This agreement does not hold at the response level: the 64 most central responses
under the two representations have Jaccard similarity only 0.12.
