ABSTRACT
Large audio-language models (LALMs) are sensitive to input pertur-
bations, such as noise, waveform corruption, and adversarial injec-
tions. We propose AnchorPrompt, an efficient adaptation method that
keeps the model frozen and learns a single block of prompt vectors
inserted at the decoder input, between the audio and question embed-
dings. We train these vectors through self-distillation over diverse
audio and text perturbations. To improve answer consistency and
mitigate hallucination, we use the model’s prediction on the clean
recording as the target for answerable inputs, and assign a refusal
target when the audio lacks sufficient evidence to answer. Further-
more, AnchorPrompt is perturbation-agnostic at inference, requiring
no prior detection of perturbations and enabling zero-shot transfer
to unseen distortions. We evaluate three LALMs across three bench-
marks and show that AnchorPrompt improves answer consistency
in most tested conditions. Clean accuracy improves in six of nine
model–benchmark pairs, with minimal impact on the remainder of
1.2% at most. Crucially, AnchorPrompt reduces hallucinations under
severe audio corruption while keeping false refusals on clean audio
rare. Finally, these consistency gains transfer to unseen perturbations,
such as choice permutations and reverberation.1
Index Terms— audio-language models, soft prompts, adversarial
robustness, hallucination, self-distillation.
1. INTRODUCTION
Large audio-language models (LALMs) combine an audio encoder
with a large language model (LLM) to perform understanding and rea-
soning across speech, environmental sound, and music [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6,
7]. Benchmarks such as AIR-Bench [8] and Dynamic-SUPERB [9]
evaluate general audio tasks. Other benchmarks like MMAU [10],
MMAR [11], and SAKURA [12] test expert knowledge, multi-step
reasoning, and audio-grounded logic. However, these benchmarks
primarily rely on clean audio recordings and static prompt templates.
Consequently, they do not systematically evaluate model behavior
under real-world conditions, where inputs are frequently noisy, in-
complete, or subject to adversarial manipulation.
LALM robustness is essential in safety-critical domains like
healthcare and emergency services [13]. In practice, inputs face two
main types of perturbations. First, natural perturbations like back-
ground noise, missing segments, or reordered choices occur without
malicious intent. Second, adversarial perturbations like injected text
or audio are designed to mislead the model. Recent studies show
that current LALMs are vulnerable to both types. Performance drops
significantly when noise or masking increases, even when the answer
remains audible [14]. LALMs are also sensitive to choice permuta-
tions [15] and audio compression [16]. Furthermore, models often

Text-only LLMs show similar sensitivity to typos, paraphrases,
and choice ordering [25, 26]. These issues can be mitigated via
prompt optimization [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. For LALMs, however,
robustness also requires handling distortions in the audio modal-
ity. Noise, masking, or injected speech alter the audio embeddings
supplied to the decoder. Standard hand-written instructions operate
purely in text, so they cannot re-align these corrupted audio represen-
tations. In practice, hand-written instructions alone fail to stabilize
model behavior across diverse audio and text perturbations.
To address this, we propose AnchorPrompt, an efficient soft-
prompting framework. It inserts a small set of trainable soft-prompt
vectors [33] between audio and question embeddings at the input of a
frozen LALM decoder. We train these vectors via self-distillation in a
teacher-student setup. The teacher is the frozen LALM operating on
clean audio. The student is the same model processing perturbed in-
puts with the soft prompt. The student learns to replicate the teacher’s
clean prediction. When the audio lacks sufficient evidence to an-
swer, the student learns to output a refusal target, preferring to refuse
rather than hallucinate. Unlike prior methods that fine-tune model
parameters [34], train task-specific adapters [35], or modify decoding
mechanics [36, 37], AnchorPrompt requires zero model parameter
updates and relies on self-distillation instead of ground-truth labels.

We evaluate AnchorPrompt across three LALMs and three bench-
marks covering diverse natural and adversarial perturbations. We
compare our approach against hand-written instruction baselines,
assessing clean accuracy, distortion robustness, and hallucination mit-
igation. We also analyze zero-shot transfer to perturbations not seen
during training, such as choice permutations and room reverberation.
2. METHOD
Fig. 1 gives an overview of AnchorPrompt. We add a soft prompt
in the form of a small set of learnable vectors to the decoder input
of a frozen LALM. The rest of the model parameters remain frozen,
and we only train the newly added vectors. Training follows a self-
distillation setup, in which a teacher and a student share the same
frozen LALM. The teacher sees a clean input and provides the target
answer. The student sees a perturbed version of the same input
with the soft prompt and learns to stay consistent with the teacher’s
answer, or to decline under the two refusal-target conditions defined
in Sec. 2.2. At inference, we use only the student. Sec. 2.1 describes
the soft prompt, Sec. 2.2 the training inputs and their targets, and
Sec. 2.3 the training objective.
2.1. Soft prompt
An LALM receives a recording, a question, and its answer options.
The audio encoder turns the recording into a sequence of audio em-
beddings, and the language model decoder reads these embeddings
jointly with the embedded question and options. We keep all model
weights frozen and add a soft prompt P ∈RL×D, which consists
of L = 8 learnable vectors with the same dimension D as the to-
ken embeddings of the language model. We insert the soft prompt
into the decoder’s input embedding sequence, between the audio
embeddings and the embedded question, and leave the rest of the
input unchanged. We initialize each vector with the embedding of
a randomly chosen vocabulary token. For a given LALM, a single
soft prompt is shared across all audio perturbations. When evaluating
multiple LALMs, each model is trained and evaluated with its own
dedicated soft prompt.
2.2. Training inputs and targets
For each training item x, the teacher answers the clean input once
with greedy decoding. Our goal is consistency: under a perturbation,
the model should give the same answer as on the clean input. We
therefore use the teacher’s answer as the target, even when it differs
from the ground-truth answer. We then create perturbed versions ˜x
of the item with the perturbations of Sec. 3.2 and assign each input a
target yt. The inputs fall into three groups:
• Clean input, where yt is the teacher’s answer.
Training
on clean inputs encourages the soft prompt to preserve the
teacher’s clean-input answers.
• Perturbations with clean-answer targets, where yt is the
teacher’s answer. This group contains noise and masking
below the extreme levels, adversarial audio injection, and ad-
versarial text injection. The student is trained to stay consistent
with the teacher under these perturbations.
• Refusal-target conditions, where the target yt is the phrase
CANNOT DETERMINE. This group contains 100% masking
and noise at −20 dB signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The student
is trained to decline under these two conditions.
2.3. Training objective
Let S be the set of all training pairs (˜x, yt), where ˜x is a clean or
perturbed input and yt is the clean-answer or refusal target assigned
in Sec. 2.2. The student is the frozen LALM with the soft prompt
P. As in standard soft-prompt tuning [33], we minimize the cross-
entropy of the student on the target tokens, so that the student learns
the assigned target. Only the soft prompt is updated, and the teacher’s
answers are computed once before training. Ground-truth answers are
excluded from the loss calculation and used only for evaluation and
for constructing adversarial injections using the dataset from [14].
3. EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
3.1. Data and partitioning
We use SAKURA [12], MMAU test-mini [10], and MMAR [11].
SAKURA contains 4,000 multiple-choice questions over 2,000
recordings in four tracks (animal, emotion, gender, and language),
and MMAU and MMAR contain about 1,000 questions each over
speech, sound, and music. We split the recordings of each track into
20% training, 16% validation, and 64% test. The test sets contain
2,560 SAKURA, 640 MMAU, and 637 MMAR questions, and
the training set contains 1,200 questions. These counts are before
applying perturbations.
3.2. Perturbations
We adopt perturbations such as noise, masking, and adversarial audio
injections from Mousavi et al. [14]. Noise is white Gaussian noise at
20, 10, 0, −10, or −20 dB SNR, and masking sets randomly chosen
100 ms chunks of the recording to zero until 20, 40, 60, 80, or 100%
of the recording is silent. We designate −20 dB SNR noise and 100%
masking as refusal-target conditions. While full masking completely
eliminates the original audio evidence, −20 dB SNR noise heavily
degrades it, making a correct answer highly unlikely, though not
necessarily impossible, depending on the question. We report the 10
and 0 dB SNR and 40% and 60% masking results in Table 1, and
the two refusal-target conditions in Table 2. For adversarial audio
injection, we reuse the recordings of [14], in which a synthesized
voice speaks a wrong option inside the recording at a power at or

3.3. Models, baselines, and training
We use Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Qwen2.5) [3], Qwen3-Omni-30B-A3B
(Qwen3) [6], and Audio Flamingo 3 (AF3) [5]. We evaluate each
LALM with a fixed multiple-choice prompt, and our clean accuracy
on MMAU and MMAR is close to the accuracy reported by the model
authors.
Baselines. Base is the frozen LALM with its original prompt.
Manual adds the following hand-written instruction at the position
of the soft prompt: “Base your answer on the audible evidence in
the recording. Answer when that evidence supports an option, even
if the recording is noisy or incomplete. If the recording provides in-
sufficient evidence to distinguish the options, respond with CANNOT
DETERMINE. Otherwise, output only the selected option’s letter.”
Training. We choose the prompt length and the training-set
size on the Qwen2.5-Omni validation split (Fig. 2). Consistency and
refusal level off at L = 8 and keep improving with more training data,
so we use L = 8 and the full training set for all models. We train
the soft prompt for three epochs with AdamW and a learning rate of
10−3, and we select the checkpoint with the highest agreement with
the teacher on held-out validation recordings. The soft prompt has
28,672 trainable parameters for Qwen2.5-Omni and AF3 and 16,384
for Qwen3-Omni, which is less than 0.001% of the parameters of
each model. Training takes 3.3 h for Qwen2.5-Omni and 2.7 h for
AF3 on a single NVIDIA L40S (48 GB), and 8.8 h for Qwen3-Omni
on a single NVIDIA A100 (80 GB).

We compare three settings: Base, Manual, and AnchorPrompt. All
models decode greedily. We report the following metrics.

question xi and the option ypert,i selected for the perturbed in-

two selections match, treating a refusal as an additional option:

4. RESULTS
4.1. Robustness to perturbations
Table 1 shows that the largest gains occur under adversarial injections,
with more variable changes under noise and masking. Clean accuracy
improves in six of nine model–benchmark pairs and decreases by only
0.1–1.2 percentage points in the remaining three. For Qwen2.5-Omni,
clean-accuracy gains are accompanied by lower clean-audio refusal
rates (Table 2). Meanwhile, AF3’s clean accuracy changes by −1.2
to +1.4 percentage points relative to Base.
Under adversarial text and audio injections, AnchorPrompt in-
creases both accuracy and clean-answer consistency across every
evaluated model–benchmark pair. Under noise and masking, An-
chorPrompt improves consistency in most conditions for the Qwen
models while increasing accuracy in most cases, with the largest
gains observed on Qwen3-Omni. In contrast, AF3 is already highly
consistent under these natural perturbations without prompt tuning,
and its results change marginally.
The tested Manual instruction fails to match AnchorPrompt’s
gains under adversarial injections. On Qwen2.5-Omni, it lowers
accuracy across every condition because the model declines to answer
more frequently; on Qwen3-Omni and AF3, it has a smaller impact.
While both prompts share the same decoder position after audio
encoding, only the soft prompt is explicitly optimized over clean and
perturbed inputs.
4.2. Answering under full masking and severe noise
Table 2 shows that AnchorPrompt reduces answer rates under both
refusal-target conditions across all three LALMs. Under 100% mask-
ing, answer rates drop to 0%–6.7%, while under −20 dB SNR noise,
they fall to 3.3%–13.4%. Crucially, clean-audio refusal rates remain
near zero (≤0.3%).

The reduction under full masking confirms that the model avoids
selecting options when the audio provides no supporting evidence.
Under −20 dB SNR noise, the lower answer rate demonstrates that
the model refrains from making unjustified guesses when severe
corruption makes the audio unanswerable. While the Manual in-
struction also reduces answering for Qwen2.5-Omni, it increases
over-refusal on clean audio (up to 33.4%). Because both corruption
conditions are included in training, these results measure adherence
to the trained refusal targets rather than zero-shot detection of missing
evidence.
4.3. Zero-shot transfer to unseen perturbations
We evaluate zero-shot transfer on two unseen perturbations. The first
is choice permutation, a text shift to which models are sensitive [26,
15]. Options are reordered so that no choice keeps its original position.
The second is 1-second room reverberation. Unlike noise or masking,
reverberation spreads the audio signal over time without adding noise
or removing content.
Table 3 shows higher clean-answer consistency in eight of nine
model–benchmark pairs under choice permutation and seven of nine
under reverberation. These gains are not uniform: under reverbera-
tion, consistency decreases by 4.2 percentage points for Qwen2.5-
Omni on MMAU and by 1.6 points for AF3 on MMAR. Accuracy
changes relative to clean performance are also mixed; for instance,
AnchorPrompt reduces the reverberation-induced accuracy drop by
3.3 points for Qwen2.5-Omni on MMAR and by 4.2 points for AF3
on SAKURA. Overall, clean-answer consistency gains transfer to
most OOD conditions, though the benefit depends on the model,
benchmark, and perturbation.
5. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
We propose AnchorPrompt, a parameter-efficient soft-prompting
framework that adapts frozen LALMs via self-distillation and refusal
targeting. Across multiple benchmarks and models, AnchorPrompt
improves robustness against audio and text perturbations while pre-
serving clean-audio performance. Furthermore, by learning to decline
under severe corruption, the method successfully mitigates halluci-
nations without triggering false refusals on clean inputs. Finally,
the learned prompt vectors demonstrate zero-shot transferability to
unseen perturbations, such as choice reordering and acoustic rever-
beration. Overall, AnchorPrompt provides a practical and efficient
approach for stabilizing multimodal language models against real-
world input distortions.
