Abstract
Unified language models are increasingly expected to combine heterogeneous
capabilities, such as mathematics, code, instruction following, and controllable
thinking behavior, within a single set of parameters. A common solution is se-
quential post-training on multiple objectives, but this entangles all objectives along
one optimization trajectory and makes the final model highly sensitive to training
order, data ratios, schedules, and stopping criteria. Weight-space merging offers
a modular alternative, but naive merging of single-objective experts often fails:
domain capabilities degrade sharply, or think/non-think modes collapse into
one dominant behavior. We attribute both failures to incompatible weight-space
geometry: experts trained on single objectives drift to distant regions of parameter
space, placing their interpolations outside any shared low-loss basin. We propose
Mixture-Trained Merging (MTM), which trains each branch on an objective-
biased data mixture rather than a single objective, exposing it to cross-objective
interactions and making branches compatible at merge time. MTM uses merged-
model evaluations as a low-cost signal for selecting branch mixtures, avoiding
expensive data-mixture ablations. The procedure is iterative: each round promotes
the base model using globally selected merge coefficients and refines each branch
mixture using domain-preferred coefficients under constraints that preserve other
objectives. To scale beyond simplex grid search, MTM uses qNEHVI-based multi-
objective Bayesian optimization. Across code, mathematics, instruction following,
and think/non-think control, MTM outperforms naive merging and preserves
behavioral separation where single-objective merging collapses, suggesting that
effective unified models require branches trained to be mergeable.

Introduction
Recent large language models (LLMs) increasingly aim to unify diverse capabilities within a single
model, including complex reasoning, direct response generation, coding, and instruction following [1,
2, 3, 4]. Beyond performance, such unified models are practically appealing because they reduce
the need for separate model stacks and offer a simpler deployment interface across heterogeneous
workloads.
A common practical approach to building such unified models is single-trajectory training, in which a
model is post-trained across multiple objectives within a shared set of parameters [2, 4, 3]. While
conceptually simple, this strategy introduces substantial engineering overhead. The final performance
is often highly sensitive to training order, data mixing ratios, and scheduling choices, because these

factors strongly affect objective interference and catastrophic forgetting [5, 6, 7]. As a result, finding
a robust recipe typically requires extensive hyperparameter tuning over near-full-budget runs, making
unified model construction costly and difficult to iterate on.
Weight-space merging, which trains specialized branches separately and then combines them, offers
an appealing alternative to optimizing all objectives along a single training trajectory [8, 9, 10]. This
makes unified model construction more modular and avoids much of the optimization complexity of
single-trajectory training. Merging aims to combine specialized branches into a single model that
performs well on all target objectives. However, we empirically find that merging single-objective
experts does not always lead to such a model. For example, when constructing a hybrid model that
should support both non-think and think modes, merging a non-think expert with a think expert
collapses the two modes toward the branch with the larger merge coefficient, producing uniformly
verbose or concise outputs regardless of the requested format (Figure 3). Similarly, when constructing
a multi-domain model that should handle both code and math, merging a code expert with a math
expert leads to broad downstream degradation across both domain accuracy, as shown in Figure 2.
We hypothesize that these failures arise because independently trained branches drift into different
regions of weight space. Consistent with this view, we observe substantial accuracy drops along the
linear interpolation path between high-performing branches, indicating that they do not lie on an
accuracy-preserving path [11, 12]. This suggests that mergeability must be encouraged during training
rather than imposed only through post-hoc merging. We therefore train each branch on a controlled
mixture of objectives. By exposing branches to non-primary objectives, mixture training reduces
inter-branch divergence while preserving specialization. Unlike single-trajectory multi-objective
training, which searches for a single global data ratio, our setting requires branch-specific mixtures
that yield specialized yet mutually mergeable branches.
To identify these mixtures, we use the connection between weight-space merging and data-mixture
training established by [13]: merge coefficients can approximate data-mixture ratios under first-order
training dynamics. We use merged performance as a proxy signal and optimize per-branch mixtures
with Multi-Objective Bayesian Optimization (MOBO) [14, 15]. Rather than running mixture search
as a separate stage, we integrate it into an iterative branch specialization and merging procedure.
Each round selects mixtures, continues branch training, and merges branches back into a common
reference model to control divergence. Thus, mixture selection, specialization, and realignment are
coupled within a single training loop.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We show that merging hard single-objective experts induces task interference, manifesting
as mode collapse in think/non-think merges and broad degradation in domain-level merges.
• We connect this failure to weight-space incompatibility, showing that independently trained
branches exhibit accuracy barriers along linear interpolation paths.
• We introduce iterative branch specialization and merging, which optimizes branch-specific
objective mixtures to preserve both specialization and mergeability.
• We demonstrate consistent gains on Qwen3-4B and OLMo-7B across diverse math, code,
and instruction-following benchmarks.

Related Works
2.1
Building Unified Models via Data Mixtures
Modern LLMs are expected to handle multiple objectives such as math, code, and instruction
following within a single model [2, 16], which makes the choice of the SFT data mixture a central
design decision. A growing line of work therefore aims to determine mixture ratios in a principled
way. DoReMi [17] reweights domains via a small proxy model trained with group distributionally
robust optimization. RegMix [18] fits a regression model from many small-scale runs to predict
performance of unseen mixtures. Data mixing laws [19] formulate scaling laws for the mapping
from mixture proportions to validation loss. MergeMix [13] bypasses surrogate training entirely by
approximating mixture-trained models with linear weight-space merges of objective-specific fine-
tunes. Even with these advances, identifying a single global mixture that simultaneously balances all
objectives still relies on heuristic choices and remains difficult to tune as the set of objectives grows.

2.2
Weight-Space Model Merging
Model merging offers a parameter-space alternative to multi-objective training, combining multiple
specific objective models into a single model without increasing inference cost [20, 10, 9, 21,
22]. Model Soups [20] showed that averaging weights from multiple fine-tuned checkpoints can
outperform the best individual checkpoint, while task arithmetic [10] generalized this idea by
representing fine-tuned models as task vectors and combining them through linear operations in
weight space. For LLMs, Branch-Train-Merge [8] trains domain experts from a shared branching
point to keep them close enough in parameter space to remain mergeable.
A central challenge in merging is parameter interference between objectives. Several methods address
this interference post hoc: TIES Merging [9] trims low-magnitude updates and resolves sign conflicts,
DARE [21] sparsifies and rescales SFT deltas as a plug-in for existing mergers, and SCE [22] sets fine-
grained fusion coefficients from update magnitudes. More recently, application-specific mergers such
as RAIN-Merging [23] target the fusion of reasoning and instruction-tuned models by constraining
thinking-format-aware subspaces. In contrast to these post-hoc correction methods, MTM does not
take independently trained experts as fixed. Instead, it trains each branch on a domain-biased mixture,
shaping task vectors to be mutually compatible before merging.

A standard approach to building such a multi-objective model is to train a single model on an

math, code, and instruction following saturate at different training steps and at different metric values.
A single-trajectory recipe must therefore compress this heterogeneity into one training schedule.

undertrained while others have already saturated, despite their intrinsically mismatched saturation

A natural alternative is to train objective-specific branches in parallel and combine them in weight
space. For each k, we fine-tune a hard expert Ek = SFT(M0, Dk) and merge with weights
α ∈∆K−1 as MMERGE(α) = MERGE(E1:K; α). This decouples branches and reduces unified-
model construction to many cheap, independent fine-tunes plus a simple mixing step. Most existing
weight-space merging methods [10, 8] operate in this regime: take hard experts as given, then
post-hoc combine them.
3.1
Failure Modes of Hard-Expert Merging
In this section we examine two failure modes that arise across our experimental settings. First,
hard-expert merging exhibits a sharp trade-off between target domains: gains on one domain come at
the cost of another, so no merge ratio recovers all per-domain experts at once. Second, when merging
a non-think expert with a think expert to obtain a hybrid model, the resulting model collapses into a
single mode rather than retaining both behaviors as intended. Both failure modes are alleviated when
the branches are trained on objective mixtures rather than as hard experts, motivating the approach
we develop in Section 4.
Specialization at the cost of other domains.
Hard experts specialize in their target domain, but
underperform the base model in other domains. As a result, their linear merge recovers expert-level
performance only near the simplex endpoints, as shown in the left panel of Figure 2. As the code-
expert ratio increases, MBPP+ accuracy improves, but MATH-500 accuracy drops sharply from about
85 to below 50. Thus, no interior merge ratio reaches expert-level performance on both objectives. In
contrast, the right panel shows that mixture-trained branches maintain strong performance on both
tasks across a wide range of merge ratios, indicating that mixture-trained components are easier to
combine.
Mode collapse between qualitatively different branches.
The problem becomes more pronounced
when branches encode different generation behaviors. A non-thinking expert is trained to give short
direct answers, whereas a thinking expert produces longer Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. A
useful hybrid model should preserve both behaviors and switch between them according to an explicit
mode signal, but linear interpolation of hard experts fails to do so. In the left panels of Figure 3,
generation lengths under no-think and think prompts move together as the think-expert weight
changes, so the mode gap largely disappears across the interior of the simplex; Qwen3-4B-Base
additionally shows a length blow-up at one merge ratio. In contrast, the right panels show that
mixture-trained branches preserve the separation across merge ratios, maintaining short no-think
outputs and long think outputs simultaneously.
4
MTM: Mixture-Trained Merging
We now present Mixture-Trained Merging (MTM), which addresses the failures of hard-expert
merging identified in Section 3 by training each branch on a mixture of objectives. MTM has two
variants. The single-round variant (Section 4.1), MTM-Single, trains mixture-biased branches once
and selects merge weights on top of them using the MergeMix approximation. The iterative variant
4


(Section 4.2), MTM-Multi, spreads the same budget across several shorter rounds and reuses each
round’s merged model as the starting point of the next, so that mixture search and model construction
share a single training trajectory. The candidate merge weights evaluated in either variant are produced
by multi-objective Bayesian optimization over the simplex (Section 4.3).
4.1
Merge-Based Mixture Search
Section 3 showed that merging hard experts fails because the experts drift into mutually incompatible
regions of weight space. MTM addresses this by replacing each hard expert with a soft-expert: a
branch trained on a domain-biased mixture of objectives. Each soft-expert remains specialized toward
its target domain keeping it close enough to the other branches in weight space. The unified model
is then constructed by merging these K soft-experts. We initialize the reference model as the base
model, M0 = M(θ0), and associate each branch k ∈[K] with its own mixture pk ∈∆K−1 that is
biased toward domain k. Each soft-expert is trained on its mixture,

single global mixture and trains one model on it, MTM uses it to pick K branch-specific mixtures
and deploys their merge. We refer to the resulting single-round procedure as MTM-Single.
4.2
Iterative Merging with Branch Reuse
MTM-Single decouples mixture search from training, but at a cost: the branches trained for search
and the model deployed at the end are different objects, so producing a final unified model still
requires a second, full training pass on the chosen mixture. The total budget is roughly twice that of a
single-trajectory baseline, and the final model never benefits from the merge evaluations carried out
during search.

Main Experiments
We evaluate MTM in two settings that reflect the failure modes identified in Section 3: a multi-
domain setting that merges math, code, and instruction-following branches (Section 5.2), and a
hybrid-mode setting that merges non-think and think branches (Section 5.3).
5.1
Experimental Setup
Training.
We use two open base models that differ in scale and pretraining recipe: Qwen3-4B-
Base [2] and OLMo-3-1025-7B (referred to as OLMo-7B) [16]. For the multi-domain setting, we
build math, code, and instruction-following data from Dolci-Instruct-SFT3 by sampling 10K examples
from each corresponding category. We generate model-specific trajectories using Qwen3-4B-Instruct4
for Qwen3-4B and OLMo-Instruct5 for OLMo-7B. For the hybrid-mode setting, we sample 10K
non-think examples from Dolci-Instruct-SFT and 10K think examples from Dolci-Think-SFT-32B6,
generating think trajectories with Qwen3-4B7 in think mode and the OLMo3-7B-Think for the
respective experiments. Mixture datasets contain 10K examples sampled from the branch-specific
datasets, matching the total training size of each individual branch. See Section A for details.
Benchmarks.
We evaluate along three axes that correspond to the target objectives. For Math,
we report accuracy on GSM8K [25] and MATH-500 [26]. For Code, we report MBPP+ [27] and
LiveCodeBench (LCB) [28]; For IF (Instruction Following), we report IFEval [29]. In the hybrid
setting we additionally report the average response length (in tokens) under both no-think and
think prompts, which measures whether the merged model preserves behavioral separation between
the two modes. See Section A for evaluation prompts and additional details.
Baselines.
We compare MTM against several baselines constructed from the same set of single-
objective experts. (1) Linear Merge [10] linearly combines expert updates, with merge weights
chosen by grid search over the simplex. (2) MergeMix [13] uses the selected merge weights as data-
mixture ratios and trains a single model on the resulting mixture, rather than merging at deployment
time. (3) TIES-Merge [9] resolves interference by sparsifying updates, electing a shared sign, and
averaging sign-consistent parameters. (4) DARE [21] randomly drops and rescales update entries,
and we apply it as a preprocessing step before TIES. (5) SCE [22] assigns matrix-wise coefficients
based on update magnitudes to enable fine-grained fusion without hard sparsification.

5.3
Hybrid Mode
We now turn to the harder hybrid setting in which the two branches encode behaviors rather than
domains: a no-think branch trained to produce short, direct answers, and a think branch trained to
produce long CoT reasoning. As shown in Section 3, naively merging these two branches collapses
the two modes onto a single behavior, motivating mixture training and ultimately MTM as remedies.
We probe this on OLMo-3-7B by comparing Linear Merge, Mixture Training, and MTM. Each
method is evaluated on the same unified model under both no-think and think prompts, yielding
two (token, accuracy) points per method (Figure 4). The horizontal extent between the two points
measures how strongly the modes diverge in generation length, while their vertical positions reflect
accuracy: a short segment means the two modes have collapsed into near-identical behavior, whereas
a longer segment positioned further to the upper-left means modes remain distinct while overall using
fewer tokens at comparable accuracy.
MTM preserves mode separation and shifts the frontier.
Reading Figure 4 under this convention,
Linear Merge (gray) produces a near-degenerate segment, indicating that the two modes have
collapsed into a single behavior. Mixture Training (blue) yields a clearly extended segment, recovering
the separation between short no-think and long think generations. MTM (orange) preserves this
separation and additionally shifts the segment toward the upper-left, using fewer tokens at no-think
while matching or exceeding Mixture Training accuracy at think. Together, these results confirm
that mode separation must be built in through mixture training rather than merging hard experts,
and that MTM further exploits branch-level mixture control to push the hybrid model onto a more
favorable length/accuracy trade-off than mixture training alone can reach.

7
Conclusion
We study multi-objective language models built through weight-space merging.
Merging
single-objective experts often produces sharp cross-domain trade-offs or mode collapse in
think/non-think hybrids. We attribute these failures to weight-space incompatibility: indepen-
dently trained experts drift away from a shared high-performing basin. We propose Mixture-Trained
Merging (MTM), which trains each branch on an objective-biased mixture so that task vectors remain
compatible. MTM uses merged-model evaluations as a proxy for data-mixture training and iterates
between MOBO based mixture search, branch specialization, and merging. Across Qwen3-4B-Base
and OLMo-7B, MTM-Multi achieves the best average performance, keeps all objectives close to their
single-domain experts, and preserves think/non-think separation. The flatter, higher-valued sim-
plex induced by mixture-trained branches supports our main conclusion: mergeability is determined
by the branches, not only by the merging rule. Our study is limited to SFT-stage merging; extending
MTM to larger objective sets and post-RL training remains an important direction for future work.

A.3
Bayesian Optimization Details
We use Bayesian optimization to search over merge coefficients on the simplex. At each MTM
iteration, the initial design includes the simplex vertices, a central or previously best merge point,
and a small number of space-filling random candidates. Each candidate corresponds to a merged
checkpoint, which is evaluated on all objectives.
Each objective is modeled with an independent Gaussian process surrogate using a Matérn-5/2
kernel with automatic relevance determination and outcome standardization. The surrogates are
fit by maximizing the marginal likelihood and refit after each new observation. We use qNEHVI
as the acquisition function, optimized sequentially with batch size q = 1. The reference point is
defined relative to the single-objective expert scores, encouraging hypervolume improvement only
for candidates that approach the expert frontier while maintaining balanced performance across
objectives.
After exhausting the BO budget, we select a global merge coefficient from the observed candidates
using a balanced multi-objective score that prioritizes the weakest objective and avoids near-vertex
solutions. We also select specialist merge coefficients for the next MTM iteration by requiring
the target objective to remain close to its expert score while constraining degradation on the other
objectives. These coefficients are then used for base promotion and mixture propagation in the next
iteration.
