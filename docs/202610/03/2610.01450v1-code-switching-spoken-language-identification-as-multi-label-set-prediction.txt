Abstract —Code-switched (CS) speech leaks through the mono-lingual language identification (LID) filters used to curate massive speech corpora, calling for CS-aware LID (CS-LID). We formu-late utterance-level CS-LID as multi-label language-set prediction and propose a set generator that directly outputs the languages in an utterance, comparing it against atomic-pair and score-based classification baselines. Oracle Top-k is the strongest baseline, but thresholding fails because no single threshold separates CS from monolingual speech. Our set generator predicts the correct language count on unseen pairs without assuming the number of languages, but underperforms oracle Top-k in exact set accuracy. Our analysis identifies the key obstacles to robust CS-LID: oracle cardinality, threshold instability, language bias in CS training data, and the synthetic-to-real gap. 

Index Terms —Code-Switching Spoken Language Identifica-tion, Code-Switching Speech, Spoken Language Identification. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

In recent years, massive multilingual speech datasets com-piled from web-crawled, in-the-wild audio [1]–[3] have sig-nificantly advanced speech foundation models [4]–[6]. In the preprocessing pipelines that filter and refine language labels of such uncurated audio, utterance-level spoken language identification (LID) [7], [8] plays a pivotal role. However, even the heavily curated YODAS [1] subset within OWSM v4 [6] still contains code-switched (CS) speech that leaked through monolingual LID filtering [9]. The problem is not the presence of CS speech itself, but the mismatch between monolingual labels and multilingual content, which can bias language-balanced sampling, contaminate language-specific evaluation, and leak unintended languages into language-conditioned ASR training. This motivates CS-aware LID (CS-LID) that identi-fies the set of languages present in each utterance. Research on code-switched speech has historically focused on a few specific language pairs (e.g., Mandarin–English or Hindi–English) [10]–[12]. Recently, massively multilingual CS corpora such as CS-YODAS [9] and CS-FLEURS [13] have been introduced in order to explore research questions concerning how models for code-switching scale both in terms of linguistic diversity and volume of data. This scaling changes the nature of the CS-LID task: as the language inventory grows, the number of possible language pairs grows quadratically, and collecting CS training data for every pair is unrealistic. Amidst this expansion of language coverage, existing utterance-level CS-LID approaches mainly follow one of two output strategies. One treats each code-switched language pair as an atomic class, such as jpn-eng , alongside monolingual classes [9], [14]. This is compatible with standard multi-class LID, but it ignores the compositional nature of code-switched speech and cannot naturally handle language pairs that were not observed during training. Another approach keeps the output inventory as individual languages and derives a language set from LID scores. Patra et al. evaluate Top-k language predictions, using k = 2 for two-language CS utterances, and also fine-tune LID models with soft language-distribution targets by KL divergence [15]. This avoids introducing atomic pair classes and can in principle represent unseen combinations of known languages. However, Top-k decoding assumes that the number of languages in the utterance is known in advance. Threshold-based decoding removes this explicit cardinality assumption, but its perfor-mance depends on the operating point, which can vary across languages, domains, and code-switching densities. To overcome these limitations, we formulate utterance-level CS-LID as multi-label language-set prediction. Instead of mapping an utterance to an atomic pair label such as 

jpn-eng , our model predicts the set of individual languages present in the utterance, such as {jpn , eng }. This makes unseen language pairs representable as new combinations of known languages, while avoiding both the oracle language count required by Top-k inference and the operating point required by thresholding. In this paper, we provide a controlled comparison of four representative strategies for utterance-level CS-LID: (1) hard-target multi-class classification with atomic pair labels, (2) softmax classification over soft targets, (3) sigmoid classi-fication over multi-hot targets, both decoded with Top-k or thresholding, and (4) multi-label language-set generation. We evaluate them on FLEURS, CS-FLEURS, and real-world CS speech datasets. Atomic-pair classification performs well on seen pairs but is structurally unable to predict unseen ones. Score-based classification, (2) and (3), with Top-k is a strong oracle-cardinality baseline, while thresholding does not match Top-k accuracy. Set generation avoids atomic pair labels, oracle cardinality, and hand-designed thresholds, but does not yet outperform the Top-k baseline. Robust language-set prediction for unseen, non-English, and real-world CS speech arXiv:2610.01450v1 [eess.AS] 1 Oct 2026 thus remains an open challenge. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/espnet/espnet/tree/master/egs2/cs-fleurs. II. R ELATED WORK 

A. Frame/Token level Code-Switching Language Identification 

Fine-grained approaches model switching boundaries within an utterance [11], exploit token- or frame-level language information for CS-ASR [10], [16], [17], or perform lan-guage diarization [18]–[20]. While useful for switch-point localization and CS-ASR, such granularity is unnecessary for corpus filtering, and local language labels are often ambiguous due to borrowings, cognates, and accent-dependent pronunci-ations [21], [22]. We therefore focus on utterance-level LID. 

B. Utterance level LID at Code-Switching Speech 

Utterance-level CS-LID has mainly followed two formula-tions. The first treats each code-switched language pair as an atomic class. For example, Shastri et al. classify Hindi, En-glish, and Hindi–English as three separate classes [14], and the CS-YODAS baseline distinguishes jpn from jpn-eng [9]. This formulation is compatible with standard multi-class LID, but it ties each CS label to a pair observed during training. The second formulation keeps the output inventory as indi-vidual languages and derives a language set from LID scores. Patra et al. rank individual languages by their predicted LID probabilities and analyze the ranks of the languages contained in CS utterances [15]. They also fine-tune LID models with soft language-distribution targets. However, deriving a set from scores either assumes the number of languages in the utterance (Top-k) or depends on an operating point that varies across languages and domains (thresholding). We address these limitations with multi-label language-set generation, as formulated in Section III. III. M ETHOD

CS-YODAS [9] consists of naturally occurring CS speech mined from YouTube. Following the paper, we select lan-guage pairs combining English with Arabic, Mandarin, French, Hindi, Japanese, and Russian. We use it to test whether in-the-wild supervision improves robustness beyond controlled settings. 

The Bangor Miami Corpus [24] is a conversational Spanish–English bilingual corpus. We leverage this dataset to analyze how model performance shifts as the proportion of English within a CS utterance varies. 

Multilingual Soap Opera Corpus [25] features South African broadcast speech with naturally occurring CS among English, isiXhosa, and isiZulu. This corpus serves to test whether CS-LID models can scale to three-language CS sce-narios. 

B. Models 

All systems use the same 965M-parameter MMS-1B fron-tend, which is also updated during training: its multilayer representations are combined via a learnable weighted sum and normalized at the utterance level. Excluding MMS-1B, the trainable parameters are 9.33M, 9.32M, 9.32M, and 23.55M for the hard-target, soft-target, sigmoid, and set-generator models, so the largest gap accounts for less than 2% of the parameters updated during training. The three classifiers are based on the ESPnet LID recipe and share the same architecture and optimization, differing only in the output head and the objective defined in Section III. The frontend features are passed through an ECAPA-TDNN encoder, channel-attentive statistics pooling, and a RawNet3-style projector to obtain an utterance-level embedding, on which a subcenter cosine head with K = 3 operates, with an additive angular margin of m = 0 .5 and scale s = 30 

applied to the positive languages. Each model is trained for 3 epochs with an effective batch size of 32; the learning rate is linearly warmed up from 3 × 10 −6 to 5 × 10 −6 over the first 10% of the total steps, held constant for the next 20%, and linearly decayed to 5 × 10 −7.

a) Hard-target multi-class classifier: Trained without CS data, the model has no CS class, so we take the top-2 individual languages for CS utterances; trained with CS data, the predicted atomic pair is converted back to an unordered language set. 

b) Soft-target classifier: CS utterances are trained with soft targets assigning 0.5 probability mass to each constituent language. 

c) Sigmoid-based multi-label classifier: The BCE objec-tive uses a positive-class weight of 50. At inference, both score-based classifiers take the top-k languages for oracle evaluation, where k is the reference language count; we additionally sweep the score threshold. 

d) Multi-label set generator: The set generator is based on the ESPnet ASR recipe. The frontend features are passed to a 24-layer Transformer encoder, and a 4-layer Transformer decoder with 4 attention heads and 256 hidden dimensions generates the language tokens. It is trained with decoder cross-entropy only, without CTC loss; since the task is unordered, we compute the loss for both token orders of each CS utterance and take the smaller one. Unlike the three classifiers, the set generator uses two-stage training with the same data and objective in both stages. The first stage stabilizes early Transformer training: MMS-1B is frozen and only the randomly initialized encoder-decoder is updated, about 1/40 of the parameters trained in the second stage. We then initialize from the best validation-loss checkpoint and unfreeze MMS-1B. Each stage runs for 3 epochs, with a peak learning rate of 10 −3 and 5 × 10 −6

respectively; in both, the learning rate is linearly warmed up from near zero over the first 10% of the total steps and then decayed by an inverse square-root schedule. C. Experimental Configurations 

We train nine configurations, summarized in Table II. They differ in model family and in whether CS-FLEURS and CS-YODAS are included in training. We report exact set accuracy: a prediction is correct only if 

ˆY (x) = Y (x), regardless of language order. For CS-FLEURS, seen and unseen pairs are distinguished by whether the pair appears in CS training data. Unseen pairs consist of languages individually observed in training, but not in that combination. V. R ESULTS 

This section presents the evaluation results of the nine configurations from Section IV-C on FLEURS, CS-FLEURS, the Bangor Miami Corpus, and the Multilingual Soap Opera Corpus. Notably, our Top-k decoding evaluation relies on prior knowledge of oracle cardinality ( k = 1 for monolingual FLEURS and k = 2 for CS-FLEURS). Because the multi-label set generator solves a strictly harder problem by pre-dicting a variable-length language sequence without such prior knowledge, direct performance comparisons do not necessarily reflect architectural superiority. 

a) Overall results: Table III summarizes the results on FLEURS and CS-FLEURS. For CS-FLEURS, we separately report results on seen and unseen language pairs. On FLEURS, the hard-target classifiers maintain high monolingual LID accuracy. H1-Top1, H2, and H3 achieve 96.7%, 97.3%, and 97.4%, respectively. The multi-label set generator G1 also remains competitive, achieving 95.6%. In contrast, the soft-target classifier S1-Top1 achieves a lower monolingual accuracy of 91.4%. This suggests a conflict when training a single softmax model with both one-hot and soft targets: monolingual examples encourage probabilities to concentrate on one language, whereas CS examples distribute them across two. The sigmoid classifier M1-Top1 avoids this conflict and reaches 96.5%, since its per-language scores are not normalized to sum to one. On CS-FLEURS, the hard-target multi-class classifier H2 achieves high accuracy on seen pairs but obtains 0.0% on unseen pairs. This confirms the structural limitation of the atomic-pair formulation: an unseen language pair has no corresponding output class. The FLEURS-only baseline H1-Top2 can theoretically predict unseen combinations, but its accuracy remains low. The score-based classifiers with Top-2 decoding are the strongest baselines for CS utterances, led by the sigmoid classifiers M1-Top2 and M2-Top2 at 59.7% and 58.7% overall. This shows that classifying over individual languages, with targets that mark all constituent languages of an utterance, is a strong alternative to atomic-pair classification when oracle cardinality is available. With the same training data, G1 improves unseen accu-racy to 11.1%, compared to 0.0% for H2. This indicates that language-token set generation alleviates the structural limitation of atomic-pair classification. b) CS-FLEURS subset analysis: Table IV shows that performance strongly depends on the CS-FLEURS subset. On seen synthetic pairs, most models trained with CS-FLEURS perform well. For example, H2, H3, S1-Top2, M1-Top2, M2-Top2, and G1 all reach around 99.6–99.8% on XTTS1 seen pairs and MMS seen pairs. The main differences appear on unseen pairs and human-read speech. On MMS unseen pairs, G1 achieves 28.5%, whereas H2 obtains 0.0%. This indicates that language-token set generation can represent unseen combinations of known individual languages in some synthetic conditions. However, M1-Top2 achieves 87.3% on the same subset, showing that the sigmoid classifier is a very strong oracle Top-k baseline. In contrast, the near-zero accu-racy across all models on XTTS2 unseen pairs demonstrates that generalizing from English-involved training data to non-English CS pairs remains an open challenge. For the CS-trained models, almost all predictions on this subset include English, even though no XTTS2 pair contains it. The domain shift from synthetic training data to human-read speech makes the Read subset challenging. Specifically, H2 and G1 achieve near-zero accuracy, whereas S1-Top2, M1-Top2, and M2-Top2 reach 82.7–88.6% on seen pairs, suggesting that they are more robust to this synthetic-to-real domain gap. Read unseen pairs remain difficult, although M2-Top2 reaches 19.0%. 

c) Effect of adding CS-YODAS as training data: Adding CS-YODAS yields only limited improvement on CS-FLEURS: Read seen accuracy improves from 0.2% to 0.8% for the hard-target classifier (H2 vs. H3) and from 0.0% to 0.2% for the multi-label set generator (G1 vs. G2). However, these gains are small and do not solve the Read condition. Moreover, G2 loses the MMS unseen improvement observed in G1, and the soft-target classifier degrades markedly (S1 vs. S2, 52.0% to 33.0% overall). The one substantial gain is for the sigmoid classifier on Read unseen pairs, from 1.2% to 19.0% (M1 vs. M2). All comparisons in this paragraph are significant ( p < 0.01 ) under a two-sided exact McNemar test over paired utterances, with Holm correction across all 44 model-pair-by-subset tests we ran. This limited benefit is likely due to the mismatch between CS-YODAS and CS-FLEURS. CS-YODAS consists of long in-the-wild utterances of around 60 seconds, whereas CS-FLEURS utterances are much shorter, around 10 seconds. CS-YODAS is also noisy, since it is mined from YouTube without studio conditions, whereas CS-FLEURS is read or synthesized. This length, noise, and domain mismatch may make naive mixing of CS-YODAS with FLEURS and CS-FLEURS ineffective under the current training setup. 

d) Prediction cardinality: Table V compares the four models that directly decide whether a CS-FLEURS utterance is predicted as one-language or two-language. For H2, this is determined by whether the predicted class is an individual language or an atomic pair. For G1, this is determined by the number of unique language tokens generated by the decoder. Both H2 and G1 almost always predict one language on the Read subset, which explains their near-zero exact set accuracy there. In contrast, both models mostly predict two languages on synthetic seen subsets such as XTTS1 and MMS seen. On MMS unseen pairs, G1 predicts two languages for 96.8% of utterances, whereas H2 does so for only 38.5%. This partly explains why G1 improves over H2 on MMS unseen pairs. However, G1’s exact set accuracy is still 28.5%, showing that correct cardinality alone is not sufficient; the model must also identify the correct constituent languages.

This is likely because English appears only as the embedded language in CS-FLEURS, so the training data consists entirely of CS utterances with a low proportion of English. These results highlight the difficulty of generalizing to CS utterances dominated by English. 

g) Three-language code-switching: We also evaluate the models trained on FLEURS and CS-FLEURS (H2, S1, M1, and G1) on a subset of the Multilingual Soap Opera Corpus that features code-switching among three languages: English, Xhosa, and Zulu. All four models obtain 0.0% exact set accu-racy. Extending utterance-level CS-LID beyond two-language mixtures thus remains an open challenge. VI. D ISCUSSION 

Most CS-trained models are near-perfect on seen synthetic CS, but each fails once its assumption breaks: atomic-pair classes cannot represent unseen pairs, thresholding cannot separate CS from monolingual speech, and transfer from TTS-based training data to human-read speech remains challenging. We formulated utterance-level CS-LID as multi-label language-set prediction and compared our set generator against atomic-pair classification and two score-based classifiers, on a shared MMS-1B frontend. Oracle Top-k is the strongest baseline but requires the language count, and thresholding is not a viable substitute. Our set generator removes both assumptions but does not yet match Top-k accuracy.
