# Balancing Multimodal Learning via Functional Progress 

Zhongjing Gu Fengqiang Wan Yiming Cui Yufa Feng Yang Yang 

Abstract 

Multimodal learning often suffers from modality imbalance, where the joint optimization process is dominated by a single modality. Existing methods typically estimate modality imbalance from score disparities derived from prediction uncertainty or optimization statistics. However, due to distinct prediction uncertainty and learning dynamics across modalities, direct comparison of such scores may misinterpret intrinsic modality differences as progress gaps, leading to biased imbalance estimation. In this paper, we propose Function-Space Guided 

Multimodal Optimization (FGMO) , which leverages a function-space progress signal to assess modality-wise optimization progress and coordinate optimization across modalities to alleviate modality imbalance. Specifically, we introduce Functional Progress Estimation (FPE) to measure each modality’s update-induced function-space response and calibrate it against a loss-aligned unimodal reference, producing a comparable progress signal. Based on this signal, Functional Response Control (FRC) redistributes modality-level function-space budgets and realizes the target responses through tensor-wise learning-rate adjustment. Theoretical analysis establishes a one-step target-contraction property of FRC under bounded controller-state mismatch, and extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of FGMO across multiple multimodal benchmarks. 

# 1 Introduction 

Multimodal learning (Baltruˇ saitis et al., 2019) aims to integrate information from multiple modalities, such as vision, language, and audio. Yet, incorporating more modalities does not always lead to better performance (Wang et al., 2020). Previous studies have shown that different modalities often learn and converge at different rates during joint training (Yang et al., 2024). As a result, the easier-to-learn modality may dominate the training process, while the other modalities remain under-optimized (Hua et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025). This phenomenon is commonly referred to as modality imbalance (Yang et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025). To alleviate modality imbalance, existing methods typically estimate modality-wise imbalance scores from prediction uncertainty or optimization statistics and adjust the optimization accord-ingly (Peng et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2026). Prediction-based methods derive such scores from signals such as confidence or prediction variance (Peng et al., 2022; Fan et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025), while optimization-based methods use statistics such as gradient or update magnitudes (Wei and Hu, 2024; Gao et al., 2026). Typically, these scores are compared directly across modalities to determine which modality is under-optimized. However, different modalities naturally exhibit distinct prediction uncertainty and learning dynamics, making cross-modal score gaps poorly calibrated to the actual rebalancing demand. Consequently, the magnitude of a score disparity may not accurately indicate which modality is relatively under-optimized or how strongly it should be adjusted, potentially leading to inappropriate rebalancing. To address this limitation, we assess each modality’s optimization during joint training relative to its own unimodal training, rather than directly comparing absolute scores across modalities. We then normalize each modality’s current functional response by its loss-aligned unimodal reference to obtain a function-space progress signal termed Relative Functional Progress (RFP), which accounts for modality-specific learning dynamics and provides a more comparable signal for modality rebalancing. To evaluate the rebalancing reliability of RFP, we conduct controlled intervention experiments on CREMA-D and compare RFP with the modality-level rebalancing scores derived from RGM (Gao et al., 2026) and SMV (Wei et al., 2024), representing optimization-based and prediction-based signals, respectively. To isolate the quality of these signals, we retain only their modality-level scores and convert all three signals into intervention coefficients using the same mapping rule. Specifically, we select several checkpoints along the same vanilla training trajectory and, from each checkpoint, construct four continuation branches: one vanilla branch that preserves the original optimization process and three rebalancing branches guided by the RGM-derived score, SMV-derived score, and RFP, respectively. We measure the intervention benefit of each signal at the current training stage by comparing the final accuracy of its guided branch with that of the corresponding vanilla continuation. As illustrated in Figure 1, while all three signals show a consistent audio-over-video ordering with generally beneficial interventions in the early stage of training, the RGM- and SMV-derived scores retain clear audio–video gaps in the later stage even as their interventions become harmful. This suggests that these cross-modal scores can be biased by modality-specific differences and consequently misguide rebalancing. By contrast, the RFP gap gradually narrows and eventually reverses its ordering, while RFP-guided interventions remain beneficial at most checkpoints, suggesting that RFP provides a more comparable measure of optimization progress across modalities. To this end, we propose Function-Space Guided Multimodal Optimization (FGMO) , which coordinates modality optimization using RFP, a function-space progress signal. Specifically, we introduce Functional Progress Estimation (FPE) to recover unit-learning-rate updates, measure their tensor-wise responses in function space, and derive RFP. Using the resulting RFP, Functional Response Control (FRC) further allocates modality-level response budgets toward slower-progress modalities under KL regularization. In turn, the assigned budgets are converted into tensor-wise learning rates according to the reference profile and current functional responses, which modulate the optimizer updates. Together, these components form a feedback loop that continuously reassesses 2and regulates modality progress throughout training. Our contributions are as follows: • We rethink modality imbalance in function space and introduce Relative Functional Progress (RFP) , which provides a more comparable signal for modality rebalancing. • We propose Functional Progress Estimation (FPE) and Functional Response Control (FRC) to form a closed-loop optimization framework, which dynamically coordinates modality optimization to alleviate modality imbalance. • We provide a theoretical analysis of the proposed closed-loop controller and establish its one-step target-contraction property for reducing RFP discrepancies. Extensive experiments on six multimodal benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. 

# 2 Related Work 

Imbalanced Multimodal Learning. Compared to unimodal learning, multimodal learning (Bal-truˇ saitis et al., 2019) can integrate multi-source information to improve performance. However, traditional fusion methods (Baltruˇ saitis et al., 2019; Nie et al., 2021) often suffer from modality imbalance, where the strong modality rapidly dominates the training process, leaving the weak modality under-optimized (Wang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022). To alleviate this issue, one line of work estimates modality status from prediction-related signals. OGM (Peng et al., 2022) estimates modality imbalance from prediction contributions, while methods such as PMR (Fan et al., 2023) and AGM (Li et al., 2023) introduce modality-specific classifiers or predictions to characterize the learning state of each modality. More recently, ARL (Wei et al., 2025) and AUG (Jiang et al., 2025) calibrate modality optimization based on prediction variance and bias and modality classification ability. Another line of work characterizes modality status from optimization-related signals. G-Blend (Wang et al., 2020) and Greedy (Wu et al., 2022) adjust modality optimization according to generalization dynamics. Recent methods such as AMSS+ (Yang et al., 2025) and RGM (Gao et al., 2026) exploit fine-grained parameter information or cumulative gradient energy to regulate modality optimization. Despite their different forms, both categories estimate modality imbalance by directly comparing modality-wise scores. However, different modali-ties naturally exhibit distinct prediction uncertainty and learning dynamics, making these scores not inherently comparable across modalities. Consequently, score disparities may reflect intrinsic modality differences rather than actual differences in optimization progress. 

Function-Space Methods. To more faithfully characterize how neural networks change during learning, early studies began to examine optimization from a function-space perspective. HCGD compared parameter- and function-space distances during optimization (Benjamin et al., 2019). Subsequent works such as S-FSVI regularized model outputs in function space to preserve previously learned knowledge (Rudner et al., 2022). More recently, SFR introduced a function-space parameter-ization for sequential learning (Scannell et al., 2024), while FLeRM proposed an efficient first-order estimator to quantify function-space changes induced by parameter updates (Milsom et al., 2025). Despite these advances, existing function-space methods mainly focus on single-network or unimodal learning scenarios. How to leverage function space to measure and balance modality competition in joint multimodal training remains under-explored.

# 4 Experiments 

4.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset: We conduct experiments on six widely used multimodal datasets, i.e., CREMA-D (Cao et al., 2014), KSounds (Arandjelovi´ c and Zisserman, 2017), VGGSound (Chen et al., 2020), Twit-ter (Yu and Jiang, 2019), Sarcasm (Cai et al., 2019), and NVGesture (Molchanov et al., 2016), covering audio-video, image-text, and trimodal settings. CREMA-D is an audio-visual dataset for emotion recognition, containing 7,442 video clips from 91 actors. KSounds is an audio-visual dataset for human action recognition with 31 action classes. VGGSound is a large-scale audio-visual dataset with nearly 200K video clips. Twitter is an image-text dataset for emotion recognition with three classes. Sarcasm is designed for sarcasm detection and contains 24,635 image-text pairs. NVGesture is a trimodal dataset for gesture recognition, containing 1,532 samples from RGB, optical flow (OF), and depth modalities. 

Baselines: We compare FGMO with traditional multimodal fusion methods, including Con-cat (Baltruˇ saitis et al., 2019), Affine (Perez et al., 2018), and ML-LSTM (Nie et al., 2021), and modality rebalancing methods, including G-Blend (Wang et al., 2020), MSLR (Yao and Mihalcea, 2022), OGM (Peng et al., 2022), PMR (Fan et al., 2023), AGM (Li et al., 2023), MMPareto (Wei and Hu, 2024), SMV (Wei et al., 2024), MLA (Zhang et al., 2024), DI-MML (Fan et al., 2024), ReconBoost (Hua et al., 2024), LFM (Yang et al., 2024), AMSS+ (Yang et al., 2025), InfoReg (Huang et al., 2025), ARL (Wei et al., 2025), AUG (Jiang et al., 2025), DecAlign (Qian et al., 2026) and RGM (Gao et al., 2026). 

Evaluation Protocols: Following established evaluation protocols in prior multimodal stud-ies (Yu and Jiang, 2019; Cai et al., 2019; Peng et al., 2022), we adopt accuracy, mean average precision (mAP), and Macro-F1 as evaluation metrics. Accuracy measures the proportion of cor-rectly classified samples. mAP reports the mean average precision over all classes, while Macro-F1 computes the average F1 score across classes. 

Implementation Details: Following OGM (Peng et al., 2022), we employ ResNet-18 (He et al., 2016) as the backbone for both audio and visual encoders on CREMA-D, KSounds, and VGGSound. All backbone parameters are randomly initialized. For NVGesture, we employ I3D (Carreira and Zisserman, 2017) as the encoder for RGB, OF, and depth modalities and initialize it with pretrained weights. We use SGD with a momentum of 0 .9 and a weight decay of 1 × 10 −4 for these datasets. The initial learning rates are 7 .5 × 10 −3 for CREMA-D, 1 × 10 −2 for KSounds and VGGSound, and 8 × 10 −3 for NVGesture. For Twitter and Sarcasm, we adopt BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) as the text encoder and ResNet-50 (He et al., 2016) as the image encoder. We use Adam (Kingma and Ba, 2015) with an initial learning rate of 2 × 10 −5. The batch sizes are set to 64, 32, 16, 2, 32, and 64 for CREMA-D, KSounds, VGGSound, NVGesture, Twitter, and Sarcasm, respectively. For fair comparison, all baselines use the same backbone and initialization strategy. All models are implemented with PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2019) and experiments are conducted on NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPUs.

4.4 Hyperparameter Analysis 

We investigate the influence of the adjustment interval K and the controller gain λ on the model performance and its internal optimization dynamics on the CREMA-D and Sarcasm datasets. 

Analysis of Adjustment Interval K: The interval K controls how frequently FGMO estimates RFP and invokes FRC to update the modality budgets. A small K provides frequent feedback, but makes the controller more responsive to short-term optimization fluctuations and increases the function-space probing overhead. In contrast, a large K reduces the computational cost but introduces delayed feedback, limiting timely correction of evolving modality imbalance. As shown in Figure 3, K = 20 achieves the highest accuracy of 0.8589 with a training time of 2.1796 hours. These results suggest that a moderate update frequency provides a better balance among feedback stability, control responsiveness, and training efficiency. We therefore set K = 20 as the default. 4.5 Further Analysis 

Reliability of Rebalancing Signals. To evaluate signal reliability across training stages, we extend the controlled interventions (Figure 1) to six datasets and 1 ,000 checkpoints. We compare RFP with five baselines using the Beneficial Rate —the percentage of checkpoints where signal-guided interventions improve accuracy over vanilla continuations. As Figure 5 (a) shows, RFP achieves the highest beneficial rate of 83 .7%, outperforming the competitive baselines and demonstrating superior and more reliable guidance for stage-dependent modality rebalancing. 

Computational Cost. FGMO introduces additional computation for unimodal reference construction and periodic function-space probing. Figure 5 (b) compares joint-training time, excluding reference construction. The same fixed references are reused across training seeds, allowing their construction cost to be amortized over repeated runs under the same setting. Although reference construction increases the end-to-end training cost, FGMO achieves improved multimodal performance through reference-calibrated rebalancing. 

Interpretable LR Allocation. As Figure 5 (c) shows, FRC exhibits a clear hierarchical pattern in learning-rate allocation rather than a simple depth-wise decay. Stem layers retain multipliers near 1×, suggesting FRC preserves the adaptation capacity of low-level feature extractors. Meanwhile, intermediate and deep blocks are substantially suppressed to stabilize high-level representations, before the multipliers increase again at the prediction heads. Moreover, audio and video show distinct allocation patterns in deeper blocks, confirming that FRC dynamically learns a modality-specific optimization strategy instead of applying a predefined schedule. 

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we propose Function-Space Guided Multimodal Optimization (FGMO) to allevi-ate modality imbalance through comparable progress estimation and coordinated optimization. 10 Functional Progress Estimation (FPE) measures update-induced function-space responses and calibrates them against loss-aligned unimodal references to produce a comparable progress signal termed RFP. Guided by this signal, Functional Response Control (FRC) redistributes modality-level function-space budgets and realizes target responses through tensor-wise learning-rate adjustment. Theoretical analysis establishes a one-step target-contraction property of FRC under bounded controller-state mismatch, and extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of FGMO across multiple multimodal benchmarks.
