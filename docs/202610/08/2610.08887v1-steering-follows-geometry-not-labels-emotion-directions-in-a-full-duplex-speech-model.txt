## Abstract 

Full-duplex voice agents need to modulate emotion and delivery during real-time conversations, when de-escalating a complaint, carrying urgency in dispatch, soft-ening a clinical result. Emotion and delivery control is well studied for TTS and turn based models through prompt-conditioned synthesis, reference-conditioned synthesis and activation steering; PersonaPlex [10] controls identity in a duplex model but not affect. We study emotion steering in Moshi, a fully open sourced full-duplex speech language model, across four emotions, using mean-difference activation steering, which costs only a few vector additions per frame and no retraining. We show that emotion is linearly decodable from Moshi’s residual stream, but activation steering is only partially achievable, and unevenly so; as happy, angry and surprise steer towards a shared direction while sad is distinctly steerable. We also show that the shared component across the three emotions cannot simply be projected away from all the emotions equally. 

## 1 Introduction 

Emotion and delivery control is a well understood problem statement for voice agents with use-cases spanning across various real time voice interactions involving de-escalating a complaint by modulating delivery, carrying urgency in dispatch, or softening clinical results. Different scenarios require varying degrees of emotion control in voice agents which humans are able to gauge and dictate quite effectively. Mechanisms such as prompt-conditioned and reference-audio-conditioned audio synthesis have been studied extensively [1,2], which suits turn based or Text-to-Speech (TTS) synthesizers. Activation steering [3,4] is another effective and efficient methodology to control emotion or delivery, as shown in EmoSteer-TTS [5]. Full-duplex speech models are superseding turn-based voice agents due to improved natural communication with humans. With Full duplex models becoming more powerful and feasible in the modern world, the effect of activation steering becomes more relevant in the context of emotion and delivery control. At the same time prior work understudies its impact on newer full-duplex speech language models such as Moshi [6], which listens and speaks at once, emits audio frame by frame at 12.5 Hz through a neural codec, and carries its own dialogue state. Recent full duplex models, such as PersonaPlex [10], which is an extension to Moshi, focus primarily on identity control via prompt conditioning, instead of dynamic delivery or emotion control. Whether a mean-difference vector direction still controls emotion effectively, under those conditions is untested. In this paper we analyze Moshi’s[6] capability to steer towards four emotions; angry, sad, happy and surprise. Our contributions include:

## 2 Related work 

Activation steering for emotion in full duplex models. Adding a direction to hidden states controls behaviour in text language models [3,4]. EmoSteer-TTS [5] brings this to speech, applying difference vectors to a searched subset of activations in flow-matching synthesisers, training-free. Two recent studies question whether a single direction is the right instrument at all. Du et al. [15] find with sparse autoencoders that emotional variation in an LLM-based TTS model is distributed over several latent features rather than one global shift, and that intervening on a chosen subset outperforms global steering. Wang et al. [16] compare steering sites geometrically and report that a flow-matching module generalises poorly across speakers because speaker and emotion are entangled there, while the speech-LM module offers a cleaner low-dimensional emotion subspace. Braun et al. [17,18] make the same kind of geometric argument in text language models, predicting steering reliability from the cosine structure of the activation differences a vector is built from - measured within one behaviour, where ours is measured between emotions. Our result is the duplex counterpart of both observations. Steering has reached full-duplex models, though not for delivery: Chang et al. [12] add a perception vector to reduce the state inertia that makes a model miss the opening of an interruption, training-free, on Moshi among others. Duplex systems that do control delivery train for it - F-Actor [13] conditions on voice, topic and conversational behaviour, and JoyAI-Talker [14] takes delivery instructions at inference after fine-tuning on expressive speech. We are not aware of prior work controlling emotion in a streaming duplex model by editing activations. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Construction of emotion direction vectors 

We write real ESD [7] recordings into Moshi’s output codebooks, with their transcripts forced through Moshi’s inner monologue, and let it run a forward pass. Moshi’s audio output is a stream of Mimi codebook tokens that can be overridden. For those frames the model is producing that recording. We read the residual stream over the last few frames before the override ends, by which point the model has been carried through the whole utterance rather than its opening. Averaging over frames and clips and subtracting the same quantity on neutral recordings gives one direction per layer, 

vl = mean( hl | e) − mean( hl | neutral) , (1) which we unit-normalise. Whether that state is genuinely emotional is measured at §5.6. 

3.2 Injecting direction vectors to steer emotion 

At the injection stage, we interpolate the output of layers 0, 8 and 24, at every frame (Figure 1) towards the desired emotion, by adding α · w(l) · rl · ˆvl. Here rl is that layer’s median residual norm, so α is relative to the activations it is added to, and w(l) = 1 − l/ 31 weights deeper layers down. The depformer that predicts the audio codebooks, the codec and the other 29 layers are left untouched. We use α = 0 .30 and this three-layer schedule throughout the experiments. Both were chosen in preliminary work on held-out sentences: strength peaks at 0.30 and falls away past it as intelligibility 2degrades faster than the emotion improves, and spreading the push across layers 0, 8 and 24 beat concentrating it at specific layers.

The injection sits upstream of the split into the text head and the audio depformer, so it can shape delivery without touching the codec.

3.3 Probing the residual stream 

To determine whether Moshi is able to distinguish the emotions accurately, we fit one multinomial logistic regression per layer to 60 clips per emotion drawn from the same states. Inputs are standardised and reduced to 30 principal components inside each split, since 4096 features against 240 rows is ill-conditioned. Accuracy is cross-validated over five splits, each holding out two of the ten speakers, so no speaker appears in both training and test. 

## 4 Experimental setup 

Model. All experiments use kyutai/moshika-pytorch-q8 , the int8 release of Moshi [6], with its Mimi codec at 12.5 Hz, on a single 16 GB RTX 4090 laptop GPU, which the int8 checkpoint is chosen to fit. Quantization raises word error rate across all unsteered and steered generated audio, so we report word error as a difference from the unsteered baseline on the same sentence and seed rather than as an absolute measure. 

Vectors. For emotion direction vector construction we use 800 clips per emotion from the English half of ESD [7], which covers angry, happy, sad, surprise and neutral over ten speakers, across all ten speakers. 

Audio Generation. We use two different modes for audio generation in Moshi. 

Forced text mode fixes what is said so that only delivery can vary: all 720 Harvard sentences [11] - the 72 phonetically balanced lists of ten from the IEEE speech-quality standard, whose content is emotionally neutral - are forced through the inner monologue, and the model renders each one. Any change in the emotion score is then attributable to delivery, since the words are identical across conditions. 

Free duplex mode is the setting the model is built for and gives up that control: Moshi hears an emotional ESD utterance on its input stream and answers in its own words, with nothing forced, 50 clips for each of five input emotions. Content is free to vary, so an emotion score there reflects delivery and wording together, and replies are short - the model often says little. Vectors, injection schedule and α are identical in both. 

Metrics and Evaluation. emotion2vec+ large [8] ( iic/emotion2vec_plus_large ) is used for emotion probability labels and emotion embeddings. Emotion is scored only on clips with at least 1.5 s of voiced audio, since emotion2vec reads silence as sad and averaging over near-silent clips invents a sad result that is not there. Every emotion number is reported against the score real ESD speech of that emotion receives from the same classifier, which we call its ceiling : held out from the clips building the similarity centroids, referring to the mean classifier score and not a theoretical bound from human level speech. Similarity to real speech is reported as a mean-centred cosine: raw cosine between two unrelated real recordings is already 0.713, so uncentred values compress into the top quarter of their range. Whisper large-v3 [9] ( openai/whisper-large-v3 ) is used for word error rate (WER), against the imposed sentence under the forced text mode and against the model’s own inner monologue in free 

3duplex mode where no reference sentence exists. The two therefore measure different things - fidelity to a script, and agreement between what the model planned to say and what its codec produced - and are not directly comparable across settings, only across conditions within one.

Removing the shared direction entirely leaves nothing to steer with - angry falls to 0.016, below the unsteered rate - implying it carries the direction that makes angry sound angry. Removing half separates angry from happy for the first time here: P(angry) rises from 0.184 to 0.288, 26% to 40% of ceiling, the happy rival falls from 0.334 to 0.162, and word error falls from 32.2% to 22.6%, marginally under the 24.7% unsteered baseline. The sweep leaves sad almost untouched, at 0.647 to 0.701 across all four settings. That is the control the construction provides: sad is the one direction not aligned with the shared component (| cos | = 0 .245 at layer 24 against 0.89 –0.94 for the others), so the manipulation barely changes it, and the movement in angry cannot be a generic effect of perturbing the vectors. Surprise is not recovered at any setting, and removal makes it worse rather than better: it falls from 0.139 to 0.031 as β goes to zero, and never beats its strongest rival - happy at β ≥ 0.5, sad below that. Partial removal resolves one of the three merged emotions, not all of them.

## 6 Limitations 

We test one model, one corpus, one extraction point and one injection schedule; we do not establish that other duplex speech models behave the same way. The int8 checkpoint puts unsteered word error at 24.3% before any intervention, and we cannot rule out that a bf16 model carries emotion differently. The WER is high for many steered audio samples, hence we offer a diagnosis rather than a comprehensive method of delivery control. Every measurement comes from emotion2vec with no human listening test. The four emotions are ESD’s, and we make no claim about how emotion is organised in general. Modern steering algorithms such as Sparse Auto Encoders (SAEs), LoRA or full retraining/fine-tuning remain unexplored in the paper. 

## 7 Conclusion 

We study whether activation steering controls emotion in a full-duplex speech model, where fast and dynamic controls to delivery control is highly beneficial. We derive steering directions from the residual stream and find that a linear probe separates the emotions in the activations while the mean-difference directions built from them barely do. For the high-arousal emotions, two different directions agree to within a few hundredths of what a single emotion’s direction agrees with itself across a change of speaker. Steering follows that geometry rather than the requested label, and only the emotion whose direction stands apart is controlled as its label implies. The result holds with the text forced as well as in a full duplex dialogue. We propose a generation-free check that explains it from the vectors alone, and show that the shared component is necessary rather than incidental: removing it entirely leaves a direction that steers nothing, while retaining half of it restores one of the merged emotions. Which clips a number covers. Emotion scores are taken over clips with at least 1.5 s of voiced audio, since emotion2vec reads silence as sad. Table 10 reports how many that removes from each condition. 

Two different real-speech references. Table 8’s recording column is the 100 clips per emotion that were actually primed, under the voiced filter. Table 9’s real speech column is a separate set of 64 clips per emotion, held out from those used to build the similarity centroids and scored without the filter. They are therefore not the same measurement, and differ most for sad and surprise, whose clips are quietest: 0.720 against 0.448 for sad, 0.593 against 0.458 for surprise. The second is the ceiling every “% of ceiling” figure in the paper is taken against, so those percentages compare filtered generated speech with unfiltered real speech. 

Prime and release. Each recording yields three clips: the original, the model’s re-rendering of it while its output codebooks are overridden, and its free continuation once the override is lifted. silent 

is the percentage of continuations falling below the voiced threshold, so that column rests on a small and non-random subset and is not a retention rate. 

Cosine panels. In Figure 2 (right) every direction is built from one half of the ten speakers and compared with the other half’s, so no two directions being compared share a voice. The left panel builds both directions from all ten. The off-diagonal falls by roughly a third between the panels, and that difference is speaker identity rather than emotion.
