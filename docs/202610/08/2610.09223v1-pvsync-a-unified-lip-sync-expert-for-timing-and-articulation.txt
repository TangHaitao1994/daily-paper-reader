ABSTRACT 

Lip movements can match the timing of speech without match-ing the spoken sounds. We introduce PVSync, a unified model for audio–visual offset estimation and phoneme-level articulation scoring. PVSync combines window-level contrastive learning for synchronisation with a phoneme-level articulation objective that aligns audio and video embeddings of the same viseme class across clips. Visemes group phonemes with similar visible articula-tion. Viseme labels are derived automatically from forced-aligned transcripts, without manual annotations. On videos from 13 talking-head video generation models, PVSync matches human rankings of lip-sync quality more closely than LSE-C, achieving a Spearman correlation of 0 .83 versus 0 .34. On an automatically constructed benchmark from held-out speech, PVSync distinguishes viseme-matched from mismatched audio–visual pairs with an ROC AUC of 0.91. PVSync also recovers the offset within one frame on 92 .7% of held-out in-the-wild clips, against 87 .4% for MTD-VocaLiST and 80 .0% for SyncNet. Code and benchmark data will be released upon acceptance. 

Index Terms — lip-sync estimation, audio-visual synchronisa-tion, viseme representation, contrastive learning 

1. INTRODUCTION AND RELATED WORK 

Accurate lip-sync requires more than temporal alignment. A gen-erated mouth can move in time with speech yet fail to close for /p/ or form the lip–tooth contact required for /f/. Clip-level scores may obscure these brief errors, motivating evaluation of both timing and local articulation. LSE-C, derived from SyncNet [1], is widely used to evaluate talking-head video generation models including Wav2Lip [2] and LatentSync [3]. Yet high scores do not establish correct phoneme-level articulation. We introduce PVSync , a unified model for temporal offset es-timation and phoneme-level articulation scoring in a shared audio– visual embedding. A contrastive objective aligns same-viseme audio and video spans across clips, alongside window-level synchronisation learning. Labels come from forced-aligned transcripts, without man-ual annotation. A final projection-head fine-tuning stage improves temporal precision while retaining articulation-sensitive representa-tions. On offset-corrected videos from 13 talking-head video genera-tion models, PVSync agrees with the human ranking of the models at Spearman 𝜌 = 0.83, versus 0 .34 for LSE-C (1620 ratings by 180 blind raters, Fig. 1b). PVSync achieves ROC AUC 0 .91 on an automatically constructed benchmark of viseme-matched and mis-matched audio–visual pairs from held-out speech (Table 4). 2. METHOD 2.1. Architecture 

A clip is scored in 12-frame (480ms) windows at 25 fps. The video tower is AV-HuBERT Base [11] video-only, reading 88 ×88 grayscale mouth ROIs; the audio tower is wav2vec2-base [12] read at layer 8 over the matching 7680 samples. The video tower emits one 768-d token per frame and the audio tower one every 20ms, so a window gives 12 video tokens and 23 audio tokens, H𝑣 ∈ R12 ×768 and H𝑎 ∈

R23 ×768 (Fig. 2). The audio count is 23 rather than 24 because the front end has a 400-sample receptive field at a 320-sample stride and no padding, so ⌊( 7680 − 400 )/ 320 ⌋ + 1 tokens fit and the last partial stride is dropped. In each tower, the tokens of the window are mean-pooled over time and the pooled 768-d vector is projected by a two-layer head into a shared 512-dimensional space. The similarity score is the cosine similarity 𝑆 = cos (f𝑣 , f𝑎 ) of the two projections.

3. EXPERIMENTS 3.1. Implementation details Data. We train on VoxCeleb2 [14] (967k clips, 2073 hours), an in-ternal set of talking-head recordings, and six public datasets: Hallo3 [15], CelebV-HQ [16], CelebV-Text [17], VFHQ [18], MEAD [19] and RAVDESS [20]. The internal and public sets total 420k clips (1055 hours) after a SyncNet filter that drops clips with SyncNet confidence below 4 or an offset more than three frames from the dataset mode, and shifts the rest to their detected offset, so the audio of every kept clip is aligned to its video. VoxCeleb2 is not filtered. Stages 2 and 3 fine-tune on the filtered internal and public sets only, and stage 2 also draws 1 .88M forced-aligned phone spans [13] from 77k of the filtered clips. The in-the-wild test clips of Section 3.2 were split off before training; HDTF, LRS2 and the generated clips appear in no training set. 

Training. All stages train on eight H100 GPUs with an effective batch of 𝐵 = 512, a learning rate of 1 × 10 −5 for the projection heads and 5 × 10 −6 for unfrozen encoder blocks, and audio augmented with pitch shift, gain and additive noise. Stage 1 freezes the convolutional front end of each encoder and trains all twelve AV-HuBERT trans-former blocks and wav2vec2 blocks 0–7 (143M trainable parameters) for 300k steps at 𝜏 = 0.1. Stage 2 continues for 20k steps and adds a batch of 𝐵 𝑠 = 64 phone spans per GPU, eight per viseme class, so every anchor has up to seven positives and about 56 negatives after same-clip masking; 𝜆 = 1, 𝜏 𝑣 = 0.1. Each step runs the encoders once over the window batch and once over the span batch and takes one gradient step on Lsync + 𝜆 Lvis . Stage 3 freezes both encoders and trains only the two projection heads (1 .31M parameters, 0 .7% of the model) for 10k steps under Lsync at 𝜏 = 0.2.

Results. At a 12-frame context window PVSync stage 3 reaches a synchronisation accuracy of 92 .7%, against 87 .4% for MTD-VocaLiST and 80 .0% for SyncNet (Table 1). It is also more consistent than every baseline, with a per-window error standard deviation of 1.93 frames against 6 .35 for SyncNet (Table 2). SyncNet trails on a test set aligned by its own offsets because its estimate is reliable once pooled over a clip but not window by window. On the LRS2 test set [21], which no PVSync stage trained on, PVSync lands within 0.4pp of the in-domain state of the art (Table 3). AVST, VocaLiST, MTD-VocaLiST and PVSync all exceed 99% at 𝐾 =15, so LRS2 no longer separates them, which is why we report on in-the-wild clips. 

3.3. Reading visemes Per-phone score. A 12-frame (480ms) window slides over the clip with a stride of 1 frame, taking the same 480ms of video and audio at every position. Within each window, forced alignment (Section 2.2.2) gives each phone a video span and an audio span, the tokens its interval covers in each tower. For every phone whose interval is at least 80% covered by the window, the two spans are embedded and their cosine similarity is recorded. A phone’s score is the mean of these cosines across all windows it appears in, and it is flagged when the score falls below a threshold. 

Benchmark. The per-phone score compares a phone’s video span with its own audio span. To test that it responds to phoneme–viseme agreement, we pair video spans with the audio spans of other phones and ask whether the score separates matching visemes from mis-matched ones. We build the benchmark on HDTF [22], 805 clips of 292 speakers, on which no stage of PVSync and none of the baselines was trained. The footage is clean and frontal, the speech well articu-lated, and each clip’s audio is aligned to its SyncNet-detected offset, so every video span shows the sound of its phone being produced. Phones come from Whisper transcripts force-aligned with MFA and are mapped to the eight viseme classes of Table 5. The bank holds 2163 phone spans longer than 80ms, 240 to 330 per class and at most seven per clip. Retrieval. For each of the eight viseme classes we average the embeddings of its audio spans into one point, the class centroid, leaving out spans from the clip being queried. A video span is classified correctly at 𝑘 when its own viseme class is among the 𝑘 

centroids closest to it by cosine; retrieval@ 𝑘 is this accuracy over all video spans (Table 4, last two columns; chance 0 .125 and 0 .25). Stage 3 scores 0 .74 at 𝑘 =1 and 0 .90 at 𝑘 =2. The audio clusters themselves are well formed, since a nearest-centroid classifier on the audio embeddings alone reaches an accuracy of 0 .94 after stage 3, against 0 .70 after stage 1. The retrieval confusion at 𝑘 =1 (Table 5) shows the lip-explicit classes, labiodental (/f, v/) and bilabial (/p, b, m/), retrieved almost perfectly, with the errors falling between classes that look alike on the lips. Open and spread vowels are exchanged, because a vowel such as /æ/ (“cat”) opens the mouth only slightly more than a spread vowel such as /E/ (“bed”). Rhotic and rounded are exchanged, because /r/ is produced with rounded lips. Velar is the weakest class and scatters over its neighbours, because /k/ and /g/ have no lip gesture of their own and the mouth during them shows the neighbouring vowel. In all, 70% of stage-3 errors fall on one of these adjacent classes.

3.4. Agreement with human judgments on generated video Generation-model level. Thirteen talking-head video generation models produced 324 clips, each shifted to its detected offset so that articulation alone is judged. We asked 180 blind raters to score each clip from 1 to 5 on how well the mouth matches the speech. Amodel’s error rate is the percentage of its phones scored below 0 .28, a threshold fixed on real footage before the study. Ranked against the raters’ mean scores, the error rate agrees at Spearman 0 .83, where LSE-C [2] reaches 0 .34. By mean rating, five models score above 4 (good), six between 3 and 4 (mid) and two below 3 (bad). The error rate ranks all five good models above every mid and bad model and misorders only two of the twelve mid–bad pairs (Fig. 1b). 

Chunk level. We sampled 400 chunks of 12 frames (480ms) from 240 generated talking-head clips held out from all training. Ninety-six blind raters saw each chunk with 13 frames of context on either side and offset-corrected audio, and answered yes or no to whether the mouth matches the speech. Each chunk got ten ratings on average, and their majority is its label, 350 yes and 50 no. Compared with the rest of the panel’s majority, a single rater catches 59% of the chunks the panel flags and wrongly flags 16% of the chunks it accepts. We fix every model’s threshold at this 16% false-flag rate and report the percentage of human-flagged bad chunks it catches, along with its ROC AUC against the majority label (Table 6). We compare our model with the baselines through two scores, the window score of the whole chunk and the worst-phoneme score, the lowest per-phone score (Section 3.3) inside it. The window score reaches an ROC AUC of 0 .788 and catches 56% of the panel’s flags. The worst-phoneme score reaches 0 .836 and catches 70%, above the single rater’s 59%. SyncNet and MTD-VocaLiST trail both of our scores, StableSyncNet ties the window score, and no other model can score a chunk phone by phone. 

4. CONCLUSION 

Clip-level lip-sync scores do not measure articulation. LSE-C ranks thirteen talking-head video generation models at Spearman 0 .34 against human raters, where our per-phoneme error rate reaches 0 .83. PVSync scores lip-sync per phoneme from one shared audio–visual embedding and calculates the offset to within one frame with 92 .7% accuracy, within 0 .4pp of the state of the art on LRS2 without training on it. Its viseme loss teaches the embedding viseme awareness with no human labels, raising the ROC AUC for telling a good phoneme– viseme match from a mismatch from 0 .69 after stage 1 to 0 .96 after stage 2, 0 .91 in the final model. On blind chunk ratings, its worst-phoneme score matches a single rater’s agreement with the panel. 5. ACKNOWLEDGMENTS

6. COMPLIANCE WITH ETHICAL STANDARDS 

The offset-recovery, viseme-mismatch and LRS2 experiments are numerical studies on existing datasets for which no ethical approval was required. The rating studies of Section 3.4 involved paid crowd-workers recruited via Prolific and administered through Mabyduck, who rated the perceptual lip-sync quality of generated videos. Par-ticipants were adults (18+) who took part voluntarily after receiving task instructions, and no ethics committee approval was required for this minimal-risk perceptual evaluation task. Participants were com-pensated at £18.55– £19.67 per hour. The authors received only the ratings, keyed by pseudonymous participant identifiers. Other data comprised publicly available or proprietary research datasets used under their respective licences, and videos generated by the authors using commercial talking-head systems.
