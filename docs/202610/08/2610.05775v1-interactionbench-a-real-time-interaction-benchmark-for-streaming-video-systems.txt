Abstract 

A video assistant must speak when its instruction warrants a response and stay silent otherwise. We introduce a benchmark that evaluates this decision for the complete system of model, memory, and response controller. I NTERACTION BENCH covers query responses, event triggers, and ongoing updates in 1,060 interactions over 812 videos, with 69 negative streams and 53 suites that pair counted events with look-alike near misses. It scores content accuracy, timing accuracy, and silence compliance on the video clock. Timely speech costs silence across systems. Polled Qwen3-VL-8B [1] reaches 77.8 timing accuracy but 10.9 silence compliance. A native real-time interaction system reaches 29.2 silence compliance at 66.8 timing accuracy, yet emits on 89.9% of negative streams. No open-weight system clears a third of the near-miss suites. Fewer replies help only when chosen, as random deletion merely trades timing for silence. Offline scores miss these failures and mispredict online behavior. Adding restraint is costly, as the native system’s controller adds little by itself and agentic systems add it only at about 30 s per poll. “Tell me when the water boils” asks an assistant to make two decisions. It must respond when boiling becomes visible and remain silent while the pot merely simmers. After reporting the event, it should keep watching without repeating the same message. Answering a question about a sampled frame tests only part of this behavior. The complete interaction also depends on when the system speaks, when it waits, and when it resumes monitoring after it has responded. Streaming video models process frames as they arrive and respond during the stream [2–4]. Interaction systems add memory and turn management around a visual backbone [5]. Their observable behavior therefore belongs to the complete system. A capable model can still speak at every poll, repeat an answer, or stop attending after its first response. Conversely, a system can avoid false alarms by missing every event. Useful interaction requires both timely speech and disciplined silence, and an evaluation must score both. Existing benchmarks study proactive responses and streaming interaction [6–9]. SPOT-Bench [10] and LiveProBench [11] penalize premature and repeated replies but include no negative streams or near misses. I NTERACTION BENCH focuses on the paired obligation created by a standing instruction, to respond to sufficient evidence and withhold speech when that evidence is absent. We annotate the earliest answerable moments, score repeated emissions, include streams where the requested event never occurs, and pair real events with visually similar near misses. These tests separate detecting a scene from judging whether its instruction is met. INTERACTION BENCH contains 812 videos and 1,060 interactions across six tasks. Negative streams and matched near misses reveal additional false alarms, even among systems that speak less often. Controlled adapter changes and offline comparisons on identical items show that offline grounding and online decisions rank systems differently, and that denser frame sampling buys little silence. 2.1 The System Decision 

A system receives a video stream v and a question or standing instruction q. At stream time t, it can access only the video prefix v≤t, its instruction, and its own interaction history. It chooses to remain silent or emit text y. The output is a time-ordered sequence ˆ E = (( t1, y1), . . . , (tM, yM)) of 

M non-empty emissions, where emission i carries text yi at stream time ti on the video clock. The evaluated system includes its visual model, memory, sampling policy, and response controller. A polling adapter is one such controller for turn-based models. It asks the model at every stream second whether to speak at that moment, and silence is a valid answer to any poll. Content accuracy Acc grades what the matched response says, timing accuracy TA measures when it arrives, and silence compliance SC penalizes unnecessary emissions. 2.3 Three Response Obligations 

As shown in Figure 1, the six tasks differ in the evidence they need and in what each new response must contain. Look answers a query from the current view, whereas Recall draws on earlier visual evidence for a later query. Time waits for a temporal relation such as an order, duration, or scheduled condition, whereas Alert watches for a specified visual event. Track maintains an accumulated count or state across events, whereas Commentate describes the scene as it unfolds, as in sports play-by-play. Each pair shares one response obligation. Silence is required before evidence arrives, between unchanged states, and after a reported event, and negative streams and near misses test it directly. The display names map to the released labels and sub-labels, see Appendix B. In Figure 1, each task pair shares one video [13–15] and faces identical frames. When the water begins to boil, Alert speaks while Time waits 15 more seconds. Track reports each egg added to the bowl, whereas Commentate stays silent until the next step. See Appendix Q for the provenance and sources of all three videos. 

2.4 Data and Quality Checks 

We collect procedural and monitoring videos where a visible change, completed action, or scheduled step anchors a response. Automatic filtering and human screening exclude unsuitable clips, broken timelines, and answer overlays. Annotators define an observable response condition before writing the instruction, reference answer, and earliest answerable timestamp. Positive items must be solvable from the prefix, and negative items must keep their condition false throughout the video. Screening also rejects ambiguous intent, and a text-only audit revises or removes keyed questions that a language model answers without frames. As shown in Figure 2,the 812 videos span ten domains, led by sports, and average 159 s. Their 1,060 interactions comprise 626 questions and 434 instructions. The 991 positive interactions hold 3,693 reference events, and 69 are negative streams. The 53 matched suites test whether a system answers counted events but stays silent at near misses. Each suite pairs a positive counting item with near-miss moments on the same video, 106 in total, and a near miss resembles the counted event without completing it. # 3 Speech and Silence in Current Systems 

3.1 Evaluation Settings 

We group systems by the component that decides when to speak. JoyAI-VL [5] is a native real-time interaction system whose own controller and memory make that choice, and kernel swaps keep them but replace its model. Native streaming video LLMs use their released mechanisms and input rates, as in MMDuet2 [25], VideoLLM-online [2], VideoChat3 [27], MOSS-Video [26], and LiveCC [24]. Turn-based models answer one prompt at a time, and a polling adapter asks them at every stream second whether to speak. It gives each model a sliding window of 16 frames at 2 fps and a 512-pixel long side, with a fresh prompt at every poll. The turn-based models are Qwen3-VL-8B and Qwen3-VL-4B [1], LLaVA-OV2-8B [32], LLaVA-OV [29], Gemma-3n-E4B [30], MiniCPM-o-4.5 [31], Qwen2.5-Omni-7B [28], Claude Sonnet 5 [34], and Gemini 2.5 Flash [33]. Flash-VStream [3] is a streaming video LLM without a response decision of its own, and the same adapter polls its streaming memory. The agentic interaction system wraps a turn-based model in a tool-using loop with Qwen-MM-Plugins [38] at each poll. One Claude Sonnet 5 run uses Claude Code [39] tools instead, see Appendix R. Offline temporal grounding gives a system the whole video and asks when and what it would respond. The six open backbones, Claude Sonnet 5 [34], and the Claude and GPT-5.6 Sol [36] agents run in this mode. Grounding thus has future access, whereas online systems see only the prefix. Dispider [35] releases only whole-video inference and runs in this mode as well. Two references anchor the comparison. A blind Qwen3-VL-8B [1] run sees only the instruction, and volunteers answer during live playback. An option-aware judge scores their free-text answers. Current runs open each response window one second before its reference time. Keyed answers are matched to options, counting items require the exact final count, and a Qwen3-14B judge [40] grades free-text responses. We calibrated seven candidate judges on 954 question–answer triples. A Gemini judge [33] labeled each triple first, and annotators screened these labels and corrected wrong ones. Greedy Qwen3-14B agrees with 89.7% of the final labels at a Cohen’s kappa [41] of 0.79, on par with Qwen3-32B [40] and gpt-oss-20b [42] at 90.0% and 89.9%. We chose it to balance reliability and efficiency. A five-vote majority adds only 0.1 point, and one decoding pass therefore scores free-text answers and segments, see Appendix I for all seven candidates. Scores use the video clock, and every poll also records its wall-clock time. On B200 hardware, JoyAI-VL [5], Qwen3-VL-8B [1], Qwen3-VL-4B, and LLaVA-OV2 [32] keep pace with a live stream and finish at least 94.8% of polls within the one-second interval. JoyAI-VL [5] leads Qwen3-VL-8B in content accuracy, 71.8 to 24.4, and in silence compliance, 73.0 to 66.2. One aggregate score hides this gap between current and remembered evidence. On T IME , Qwen3-VL-8B reaches 79.6 timing accuracy with 0.0 silence compliance, and on A LERT , 79.1 with 9.8. Reaching an event window does not imply that the system waited for it or stopped afterward. T RACK also demands a correct count. Qwen3-VL-8B pairs 75.3 timing accuracy with 8.6 content accuracy, and JoyAI-VL 58.7 with 3.2. On C OMMENTATE , polled Claude Sonnet 5 [34] reaches 84.1 content accuracy but only 4.0 silence compliance. Free-form Overall uses segment content, and only the silence diagnostic reveals that its commentary rarely pauses.

3.3 Four Failures of Selectivity 

As shown in Figure 5, repetition and premature speech dominate different systems. Polled with a 64-frame window, Qwen3-VL-8B [1] produces 21.4 redundant emissions per reference event. Its fresh prompt has no record of its previous reply, and while the evidence stays visible, each poll can repeat the same answer. MMDuet2 [25] instead produces 7.5 premature emissions per reference event, exceeding its redundant count. A familiar scene alone does not establish the requested condition, and the system must keep waiting until it holds. The same answer content can conceal opposite failures of response control. In a saved Alert item with four green-light events, both systems always choose the correct option. Qwen3-VL-8B produces 104 emissions, including five premature replies, four matched responses, and 95 repetitions. JoyAI-VL [5] emits three replies, one premature, one matched, and one repeated, and misses three of the four events, see Appendix Q for the full traces of both systems. The 69 negative streams contain no valid trigger, yet JoyAI-VL [5] emits on 89.9% of them. Qwen3-VL-8B [1] in offline grounding, with future access, emits on 5.8%. The blind reference raises false alarms on only 1.4% of streams but fails every suite. On these suites, LLaVA-OV2 [32] passes 26.4% and JoyAI-VL 22.6%, while the polled Qwen3-VL models pass 5.7% to 13.2%. No open-weight system clears a third of them. The two checks capture different failures, as Qwen3-VL-8B raises the fewest false alarms of the four systems yet passes fewer suites than a scripted burst policy. Each suite takes its event and near-miss moments from one video, so the scene alone does not tell a system when to answer and when to stay silent. Selective interaction requires waiting for evidence, responding once, and rejecting both absent events and near misses. None of the systems in Figure 5 manages all four at once on Alert and Track streams.

Memory and foresight cost timing. Reply history, agent loops, and grounding all raise silence, and each pays in timing, speed, or stability. Reply history keeps earlier frames and replies in one conversation, whereas a fresh prompt starts anew each poll. It raises silence compliance for all six open turn-based models, which suggests that much repetition comes from forgetting the previous reply. Its effect on Overall depends on the backbone, as three models gain and three lose, and a decoding change confounds the MiniCPM-o [31] gain. Agent loops make restraint deliberate. On the agent items, the Claude Sonnet 5 [34] agents reply about a quarter as often as the polled model and roughly triple its silence compliance at some cost in timing. Yet a logged agent poll takes about half a minute, far beyond the one-second interval, see Appendix L. Swapping the plugins for Claude Code tools alone moves the Claude agent’s Overall by 5.2 points, and under the same plugins GPT-5.6 Sol [36] scores twice as high as Gemini 3.5 Flash [33]. Grounding shows that foresight alone does not buy timing. With the whole video, Qwen3-VL-8B [1] raises its silence compliance more than fivefold but halves its timing accuracy, and Claude Sonnet 5 shows the same shift. Query responses stay nearly immediate, but later events are hard to locate in a finished video without tools that inspect chosen frames. Dispider [35] earns almost no timing credit on them. With such tools, the Claude Sonnet 5 agent in grounding is the only configuration above 70 in both timing and silence. An agentic system is evaluated with its loop and tools, not by its model alone. 

13 4 Conclusion and Discussion 

INTERACTION BENCH evaluates whether a video assistant speaks when its instruction warrants a response and stays silent otherwise. High timing scores coexist with frequent unnecessary speech, restrained output misses events, and both behaviors belong to the complete system. The tested native controller adds little restraint by itself. Reply history and agentic systems add it, and logged agent polls take about half a minute, far slower than the one-second poll interval. Future systems should retain an instruction, recognize new evidence, respond once, and resume monitoring. Progress reports should cover both obligations on matched items, speech when due and silence otherwise, with runtime measured separately. On the Alert and Track streams that carry them, negative streams and near-miss suites separate systems whose aggregate scores look similar. 

AI use statement 

Generative models served as the open judge for free-text items and as the first labeler of the judge-calibration triples before human screening and correction. They labeled content domains for the statistics and served as the blind auditor of the keyed pool. They also assisted with evaluation, analysis, and figure code, with prose editing, and with checking bibliographic metadata against arXiv and publisher records. Dataset annotation remains a human responsibility. This draft uses AI-assisted editing and figure generation, and the authors verify the final text, analyses, and submission. 

Ethics statement 

All videos are public and referenced by id, faces are not annotated, and any video is removed from the release at the request of its uploader. Two domains carry risk. A trigger such as “tell me when a person appears” can serve surveillance. The first-aid and driving items can invite over-reliance on a system that this paper shows to be far from reliable. The human reference was collected from volunteers who consented to the task and could withdraw at any time.

# D Curation and Annotation 

Annotators collect procedural and monitoring videos and screen them for observable response conditions. Automatic filters examine duration, resolution, views, and upload dates. Human screening excludes unsuitable clips, compilations that reset the timeline, and overlays that reveal the answer. For each positive interaction, the annotation specifies its instruction or question, query time, task label, response format, and reference events. Each event has content and an earliest answerable timestamp. An online solvability check asks whether the prefix contains enough evidence at that timestamp and whether earlier prefixes do not. A held-out annotator checks the decisive visual evidence without the target timestamp. Appendix H tests timestamp noise under Timing tolerance. The 69 negative interactions include 39 Alert and 30 Track items. Their instruction predicates remain false throughout the video. A text-only audit screens keyed questions for language-only solutions. Premature-state distractors distinguish the requested event from an earlier scene. 23 E Task Examples 

Look and Recall answer a query. Look asks which lane a swimmer occupies when the starting list is visible, and Recall asks about a distance demonstrated earlier in a driving lesson. Both require an answer at the query, drawing on current or historical visual evidence, and neither licenses repeated copies of it. Time and Alert wait for evidence. Time includes deciding which of two windows opens first, and the response becomes due when the required order can be established. Alert asks the system to report when a traffic light turns green, and a standing instruction can require several such reports, separated by intervals of silence. Track and Commentate update as needed. Track maintains a running count of appearances or revises a state when new evidence arrives, and Commentate reports successive on-screen headings or procedural steps. The requested update frequency follows the reference events or segments rather than an instruction to speak at every frame. 

# F Release Format 

The full collection is the evaluation set, and there is no training split. Annotations reside in

# H Scoring Specification 

Table H.1 lists how each response format contributes to Overall. For each item, the evaluator removes empty emissions and sorts the rest by timestamp. Negative items receive 100 only if the list is empty. For event-based positive items, each window starts one second before its reference timestamp and ends where the next window starts. The last window extends to the end of the video. The first emission within a window is matched regardless of content, with nonnegative delay measured from the reference timestamp. Earlier speech is premature and subsequent speech in an occupied window is redundant. Matching ignores content. Matching by position keeps timing and silence independent of the judge, and content accuracy then grades the matched reply. A content-aware match would reward emitting candidate replies until one fits. Missing event responses contribute zero timing credit. Content accuracy uses the first post-query response for query-answer items and the mean over matched responses for triggers. Keyed answers first match an option letter in the first 40 characters. Without a usable letter, the scorer compares token F1 with every option, requiring a unique best match of at least 0.5. Unique full-option containment is the final fallback. The keyed pool has 688 items, including 637 six-option questions. Free-text answers use the open judge. Lexical fallback takes the maximum of exact match, token F1, and a length-guarded numeric match. Human free-text responses are judged against the keyed answer text. Counting accuracy compares the last number in the emitted sequence with the final reference count. This check uses the final count. A per-event variant also requires each matched reply to state the running count of its event. It stays at or below 38.5 for every system, against 40.0 for the human reference, and exceeds final-count accuracy for 12 of the 20 configurations scored on all 160 counting items, for example 26.2 against 8.8 for polled Qwen3-VL-8B [1]. The final count is therefore the stricter criterion for most configurations. The violation budget equals the number of reference events. A fully silent positive item has zero accuracy and timing. Its silence component is undefined and its Overall is zero. Free-form items use reference timestamps to partition the video. # I Judge and Segment Scoring 

Seven candidate judges are calibrated against 954 question–answer triples. A Gemini judge [33] first labeled each triple as correct or incorrect, and annotators screened these labels and corrected 27 wrong ones. As shown in Table I.1, greedy Qwen3-14B [40] agrees with 89.7% of the final labels, with Cohen’s kappa [41] of 0.79, on par with Qwen3-32B and gpt-oss-20b [42] at 90.0% and 89.9%. Its five sampled votes are unanimous on 98.3% of the triples, against 92.1% and 92.3% for those two, and a five-vote majority raises its agreement only to 89.8%. The final scorer uses greedy Qwen3-14B to balance reliability and efficiency. Rescoring all 30 current configurations with greedy Qwen3-32B keeps the Overall ranking at a Kendall rank correlation [43] of 0.972 against Qwen3-14B, and no Overall moves by more than 1.3 points. The 108 free-form items comprise 107 Commentate items and one revision item. The segment scorer follows VDCS CORE [12]. Each reference is decomposed into at most five question–answer pairs, averaging 2.0 across 303 cached reference texts. Answers are extracted from the segment’s emitted text and judged against those reference answers. Caching fixes the decomposition across systems. 

# J Evaluation Prompts 

Two prompt shapes drive the adapted models. Single-turn modes, cumulative or with a fresh prompt, restate the full task at every poll. The reply-history mode runs one interleaved conversa-tion, states the task once in the system message, and sends a compact nudge per poll. The system prompt reads verbatim as follows. 

You are a real-time visual assistant watching a LIVE video stream. You receive frames as they arrive; the LAST image is the current moment. You must decide, right now, whether to speak or to keep watching silently. Only speak when this exact moment calls for it --- do not speak early, and do not repeat what you already reported. If nothing needs saying, wait silently. 

Each single-turn poll then renders the stream time, the standing request, one hint chosen by task type, and a fixed output format. The hint depends only on the task type and never on whether an item contains an event, and positive and negative items of the same task therefore receive identical prompts. The hints read verbatim as follows. 

Task Hint Look Answer the question as soon as you are asked, from what you can see. Recall Answer from what you saw earlier in the stream. Answer immediately. Recall, causal Answer the question as soon as it becomes answerable from what you saw. Time Answer at the earliest moment the question becomes decidable from what you have seen; before that, wait. Alert Speak only at the exact moment the requested event happens. If it never happens, stay silent for the whole stream. Track Track the requested state across frames. Each time it changes (e.g. the count increases), speak the update (for counts: just the new number). Otherwise wait. Track, revision Track the requested state; speak whenever the correct answer changes, correcting your earlier answer if new evidence contradicts it. Commentate Follow the stream and speak one short line for each new step or scene as it happens, like a live commentator or guide.

# K System Configuration and Provenance 

The polling default is two frames per second, a 16-frame sliding window, a 512-pixel long side, greedy decoding, and at most 96 generated tokens. The MiniCPM-o [31] fresh-prompt run uses beam search instead. Polls occur once per video second. Query-answer items permit a ten-second post-query response period. Reply-history variants retain earlier frames and replies in one conversation. Fresh-prompt variants start anew at each poll. Native streaming systems keep their released interfaces and recommended input rates. JoyAI-VL [5] runs its own server-side memory and silence decision at 4 fps, and each tick pushes only new frames. # L Measured Latency 

Every prediction file stores the wall-clock duration of each poll, and Table L.1 summarizes these durations. A poll spans one second of stream time, and a system keeps pace with a live stream only when most polls finish within that second. Scores ignore these durations because the runners advance the video clock only after a poll returns. MOSS-Video [26] is the exception, since it consumes frames at real-time pace. A live replay estimates what these durations cost. Eight polled runs are replayed, the six open backbones and polled Claude Sonnet 5 [34] and Gemini 2.5 Flash [33]. Flash-VStream [3] logs irregular polls and is left out. In the replay, a poll starts only after the previous one returns, uses the latest recorded decision it could see, and replies after its measured latency. # R Agentic System Details 

Agents use the Qwen-MM-Plugins media tools [38] to extract and inspect frames, crop images, and read timestamps. The Claude [34] shell-tool run instead allows the built-in shell and file tools of Claude Code [39] in a per-item working directory that holds the trimmed prefix and no answer keys. Before each poll, ffmpeg [56] trims the source video to the current stream time. The agent receives only this prefix, its instruction, and earlier utterances. The GPT-5.6 Sol loop [36] allows at most twelve tool turns and 1,500 completion tokens per session. Claude and Gemini [33] use their command-line agent loops. Each polling session has a 240-second timeout. Timeout is not a latency measurement. # S Additional Diagnostics 

Oracle restraint. Table S.1 applies a reference-based filter to existing Qwen3-VL-8B [1] and JoyAI-VL [5] traces on the items shared with the agent runs. It removes all negative-stream replies and retains only the first response attributed to each reference window. The filter deletes 3,418 Qwen replies and 1,977 JoyAI replies without rewriting retained content or timestamps. Timing is unchanged, while silence compliance reaches 100.0 by construction. Overall rises from 33.9 to 66.2 for Qwen and from 38.4 to 63.9 for JoyAI. Applied to all 1,060 items of the four randomly thinned traces, the same filter leaves timing unchanged and lifts silence compliance to 100.0 for each. Content falls slightly because deleting replies changes segment-level text and counting answers. This privileged post-processing diagnostic measures the effect of suppressing violations with privileged reference annotations. 

Native response threshold. VideoLLM-online [2] speaks when the probability of its frame-interval token falls below a threshold, released at 0.725. Table S.2 reruns it at 0.5 and 0.9 on the items shared with the agent runs. Raising the threshold from 0.5 to 0.9 raises timing accuracy from 30.2 to 51.7 and lowers silence compliance from 56.4 to 18.4, while Overall falls from 17.2 to 7.4. The native trigger therefore moves along the same timing–silence trade-off as random thinning and does not escape it. 

# T Limitations and Future Directions 

Coverage. Videos mainly last a few minutes and do not test hour-long monitoring. All items are visual, and audio channels such as speech and sound events remain outside the benchmark. Negative streams and counting-derived near misses cover only part of the space of unnecessary 47 responses. 

Scope of the scores. Stream-time evaluation isolates response behavior from hardware speed. It does not certify live deployment, and the latency measurements come from heterogeneous hardware. 

Scope of the comparisons. Aggregate correlations across a few systems cannot identify causal bottlenecks. Existing comparisons of agentic systems change multiple components and lack a complete factorial design. Future work should pair identical items and model versions, test long negative intervals, and time every system on shared hardware. 48
