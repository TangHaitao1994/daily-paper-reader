## ABSTRACT 

Model intelligence and fast response jointly shape the quality of interaction with speech language models, yet remain difficult to achieve together. Explicit chain-of-thought (CoT) improves reasoning and audio understanding, but generating in-termediate reasoning tokens delays responses. Describing fine-grained acoustic cues further lengthens CoT and increases latency. Latent reasoning can reduce this overhead, yet existing methods often trail CoT and remain limited by single-path supervision and reasoning budgets that do not adapt to problem difficulty. We introduce AURAL , which models a distribution over multiple plausible rea-soning continuations in latent space and jointly predicts chunks of future states to reduce sequential forward passes and reasoning latency. To provide initial supervi-sion for latent reasoning, we construct AuralReason-683K: 683K bilingual speech utterances (about 1,000 hours) with concise CoT for emotion recognition, empa-thetic dialogue, and general reasoning. AURAL-RL then explores beyond these traces, rewarding concise reasoning that yields high-quality answers and adapting reasoning effort to each problem. Across two backbones, AURAL-RL achieves performance comparable to CoT-RL, with larger gains over the respective super-vised checkpoints on most metrics. Analysis further shows that harder questions elicit more latent reasoning steps. On Qwen2.5-Omni, it reduces time to the first answer token by 11.8×, from 1.22 to 0.10 s, versus 0.05 s for direct answering. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Interaction is one of the defining capabilities of a speech language model (SLM). Users will not keep talking to a model that lacks the intelligence to understand their needs and reason well enough to help. Nor will they wait through a long pause for every thoughtful answer. The ideal resembles the voice assistant imagined in Her 1, responsive enough to feel present and perceptive enough to understand what the user means. Although current SLMs remain far from this vision, improving both intelligence and response speed is essential to closing the gap. Explicit chain of thought (CoT) is a natural way to improve intelligence in LLMs, and in SLMs its benefits extend further to audio understanding (Tian et al., 2025a). Yet CoT remains rare in interactive models because every intermediate reasoning token is generated before the answer and is therefore paid for in response latency. For speech, CoT introduces a second cost. Acoustic cues such as timbre, prosody, emotion, speaker traits, and background sounds vary continuously. Changes in these cues can alter how an utterance is understood, but text often reduces them to simple labels. The label “upset” can describe the tone of “I am fine”, but it does not capture the strength and mixture of hesitation, tension, and sadness in the voice. Describing these details in CoT requires more words, which add tokens and further increase latency. Recent work finds that even when CoT is used, SLMs may recognize paralinguistic cues without using them in their responses (Wang et al., 2026d;e). CoT

Continuous hidden states can retain acoustic detail without spelling it out token by token. Latent reasoning performs intermediate computation in these states, reducing the number of serial reason-ing steps before an answer. Each state can also represent information that does not map cleanly to a single vocabulary token, giving the model a broader reasoning space than explicit CoT. How-ever, latent reasoning remains rare in SLMs. FLAIR fills the silence slots that occur while the user is speaking with latent thoughts, so speech length determines the number of reasoning steps (Wu et al., 2026). CoAT and LatentOmni instead use a latent budget fixed in advance (Han et al., 2026; Dai et al., 2026). Although these designs limit response latency, they often lag behind explicit CoT in reasoning quality. Their reasoning budgets do not adapt to problem difficulty, and supervision typically provides a single target for each latent step, encouraging one continuation rather than pre-serving plausible alternatives. In addition, explicit CoT remains useful as initial supervision for latent reasoning, yet large speech corpora with reliable traces remain scarce. We therefore propose AURAL (Adaptive Unspoken Reasoning over Alternative Latents), a latent reasoning framework for SLMs that retains the benefits of CoT at much lower response latency (Fig-ure 1). AURAL models multiple plausible reasoning continuations in latent space with a Gaussian mixture model and jointly predicts chunks of future states to reduce sequential forward passes. To provide initial supervision, we construct AuralReason-683K, a carefully filtered bilingual collection of 683K utterances (about 1,000 hours) with concise CoT traces for speech emotion recognition, empathetic dialogue, and general reasoning. AURAL-SFT pools consecutive CoT embeddings into latent targets. AURAL-RL then explores beyond these traces, rewarding concise reasoning that yields high-quality answers and learning a problem-dependent stopping policy. Across two SLM backbones, AURAL-RL achieves performance comparable to CoT-RL, with larger gains over the respective supervised checkpoints on most metrics. Analysis on GPQA and VoiceBench BBH fur-ther shows that harder questions elicit more latent reasoning steps. With the default setting on Qwen2.5-Omni, it reduces time to the first answer token from 1.22 s for CoT-RL to 0.10 s (an 11 .8×

speedup), compared with 0.05 s for direct answering. Our contributions are: • We introduce AURAL, which models a distribution over plausible reasoning continuations in la-tent space and jointly predicts future-state chunks to reduce forward passes and reasoning latency. • We construct AuralReason-683K, a bilingual corpus of 683K speech utterances (about 1,000 hours) with concise CoT for emotion recognition, empathetic dialogue, and general reasoning. • We develop AURAL-RL to explore beyond supervised traces and adapt reasoning effort to each problem. It achieves performance comparable to CoT-RL with an 11 .8× speedup in time to the first answer token on Qwen2.5-Omni. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Latent reasoning moves intermediate computation from verbalized CoT into continuous states. Co-conut feeds predicted hidden states back into the model (Hao et al., 2025), while CODI adds endpoint self-distillation (Shen et al., 2025). Because neither method provides targets for intermediate latent states, the latent chain cannot be trained efficiently with teacher forcing and must instead be unrolled serially. Their preset latent length also prevents depth from adapting to problem difficulty. CCoT supplies teacher-forced targets, which improves supervision but still generates them autoregressively (Cheng & Van Durme, 2024). PCCoT replaces slow serial update with parallel Jacobi refinement at the cost of several refinement rounds (Wu et al., 2025a). Latent-SFT obtains fine-grained supervi-sion targets from a separately trained encoder (Deng et al., 2025), while CoLaR (Tan et al., 2026) and C-MTP (Yerram et al., 2026) greatly simplify target construction by pooling grouped CoT em-beddings but providing coarser supervision. Beyond supervised target design, Latent-GRPO shows that latent policy optimization can leave the valid manifold or average incompatible modes (Deng et al., 2026). Latent Thought Flow instead learns stochastic trajectories whose length depends on answer quality and computation cost (Zou et al., 2026). These findings motivate latent reasoning that preserves multiple valid continuations and uses more computation only when a problem requires it. Latent reasoning in SLMs remains sparse. FLAIR is the closest full-duplex precedent (Wu et al., 2026). It replaces idle listening slots with recursively fed soft vocabulary embeddings learned from a global expert, enabling causal thought while the user speaks. CoAT inserts a latent workspace supervised by hand-selected audio experts (Han et al., 2026). Because both the workspace and its supervisory dimensions are prescribed, it learns an engineered acoustic represen-tation rather than discovering an autonomous reasoning trajectory. ## 3 METHOD 

Overview. Explicit CoT provides direct supervision, but inference must verbalize every interme-diate step as a discrete token, making it slow and restricting reasoning to token-expressible paths. AURAL retains CoT only as a training signal. After CoT finetuning on AuralReason-683K (Sec-tion 4), AURAL-SFT pools consecutive CoT embeddings into continuous targets and uses a Gaus-sian mixture model (GMM) to represent multiple chunks of future states; at inference, each sampled chunk is fed back in one backbone pass. AURAL-RL then optimizes complete rollouts, adapting reasoning depth to the problem while improving answer quality. Joint chunk prediction adds structure and speed. The GMM restores multiple possible tran-sitions, but applying it independently at each position still leaves the structure among neighboring latent states implicit. Under teacher forcing, each prediction receives the gold prefix, so independent one-state losses do not directly teach several future states to evolve together. Inference is also serial because each predicted state must pass through the backbone before the next state can be generated. Generating a latent trace of length L therefore requires L sequential forward passes through the backbone. We address both limitations by predicting a chunk of m future states jointly, reducing this cost to ⌈L/m ⌉ sequential forward passes. Our latent head uses m learned slot embeddings, one for each future state. A shared MLP maps hi into the slot feature space. From alternative paths to adaptive depth. AURAL-SFT imitates one recorded CoT and its length, so an alternative latent path receives no credit even if it produces the correct answer. AURAL-RL instead scores complete rollouts by their final outcomes, allowing the model to discover valid latent paths and allocate different amounts of depth to different problems. The GMM lets us evaluate the same sampled chunk under the updated policy and the policy that generated it. Because chunk log density scales with the number of accepted latent coordinates, we divide each log density by that number before exponentiating their difference. This normalization keeps latent-action updates com-parable across chunk sizes and provides the multiplier for our GRPO-style clipped objective (Shao et al., 2024); Appendix A gives the equations. For each prompt, we sample 8 rollouts, compute group-relative advantages, and update all parameters without a reference policy. 

Reward design. For questions with a verifiable answer, we compute quality q ∈ [0 , 1] using exact match or the task-specific checker. Open-ended responses are judged by DeepSeek-V4-Flash, while tasks whose correctness depends on the source audio are judged by Gemini-3.5-Flash. Let IEOL 

equal 1 when a rollout never emits EOL, and let Iend equal 1 when its response lacks the end token. Both are 0 otherwise. For latent length L and rollout cap Lmax , we define the normalized length cost and reward as 

ℓ(L) = L − 1

The value ℓ(L) increases from 0 for the shortest trace to 1 at this limit. Thus the conciseness bonus is largest for a short correct trace and decreases linearly with length. Multiplication by q prevents an incorrect answer from earning a shortcut reward by stopping early. This quality-gated bonus is what makes AURAL-RL adaptive: additional latent depth is rewarded only when it contributes to a better outcome. We use λ = 0 .5, α = 0 .1, and β = 0 .02 , with the last two coefficients penalizing invalid termination. 

## 4 DATA CONSTRUCTION 

Latent reasoners learn their initial behavior from explicit CoT, but existing public speech corpora do not provide reasoning that uses the voice rather than only the transcript. We therefore build AuralReason-683K, a bilingual corpus of 683K utterances and about 1,000 hours of speech for emotion recognition, empathetic dialogue, and general reasoning. Appendix F gives the full pipeline, per-source counts, and teacher prompts. To teach the model what the voice can change, we begin with two complementary sources. LIME-440K offers mostly synthesized bilingual affective dialogue, while EmotionCoT-35K supplies En-glish human recordings for emotion classification (Zhao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026b). We retain only their audio and write new CoTs and responses. Controlled speech makes affect easy to isolate, but it covers only a narrow slice of real interaction. We therefore build HumanSpeech-1M from about 20K hours of public videos, podcasts, films, and speech corpora. Scale alone is not sufficient. Useful supervision requires utterances whose vocal delivery materially changes the appropriate as-sistant response. After removing synthetic or unreliable audio, we screen the remaining clips for this criterion, retaining 75K of 1.69M candidates. These three branches teach voice-sensitive in-teraction, but they do not teach broad problem solving. GeneralSpeech fills that gap with bilingual 5tasks in mathematics, knowledge, comprehension, and instruction following. We rewrite public text problems as natural spoken questions before synthesizing them with Qwen3-TTS (Hu et al., 2026). Once the audio is selected, the next question is how much reasoning to write. AURAL derives its latent targets from explicit CoT, so a verbose teacher trace is not harmless. Repeated explanations can become extra latent computation. We therefore seek the shortest trace that still supports the response, retaining every necessary inference while removing restatement and reasoning after the conclusion. Prior work likewise finds that shorter valid CoTs can preserve or improve accuracy (Hassid et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025b; Xu et al., 2025c). For dialogue, the trace must explain how the voice changes the reply rather than merely name an emotion. Gemini-3.5-Flash listens to each clip and writes states with distinct roles. Cue records what is audible, Need interprets what the speaker needs, optional Risk handles ambiguity or safety, and Plan determines the reply. A shaky voice with long pauses, for example, motivates reassurance before advice. Emotion traces instead use audible cues to rule out the closest competing emotion before describing the affective state. Across all tasks, the CoT stops before the final answer so that it teaches a reasoning path rather than a shortcut to the target (Shen et al., 2025). GeneralSpeech begins with a CoT and response from Qwen3-Omni-Thinking (Xu et al., 2025b). Gemini-3.5-Flash then creates two versions because training and analysis require different guar-antees. For the controlled comparison in Section 5.5, Gemini shortens only the CoT and keeps the answer unchanged, even when wrong. Any performance difference therefore comes from the rewrit-ten CoT rather than answer correction. For the main training set, it verifies the answer and repairs the CoT and response when needed. Only verified outputs enter the corpus. After filtering and rewriting, LIME-440K, EmotionCoT-35K, HumanSpeech-1M, and GeneralSpeech contain 320K, 18K, 75K, and 270K examples. Their 377K Chinese and 306K English utterances form AuralReason-683K. 

## 5 EXPERIMENTS 

For latent reasoning to be practically useful, its efficiency gains must not come at the cost of broad task competence or architectural generality. Our experiments examine whether AURAL preserves answer quality across diverse speech and text tasks, transfers across backbone architectures, and reduces the computation required before producing an answer. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

We assemble an evaluation suite that covers speech-specific capabilities and broader reasoning. EchoMind (Zhou et al., 2026) evaluates vocal understanding, integrated reasoning, and empathetic response generation. We report multiple-choice accuracy and the mean judge score across its four official dimensions for empathetic responses. IEMOCAP (Busso et al., 2008) and MELD (Poria et al., 2019) evaluate speech emotion recognition on their standard test sets. MMSU (Wang et al., 2026a) covers fine-grained spoken language perception and reasoning. MMAU-Pro (Kumar et al., 2026) measures broad audio intelligence, while GPQA (Rein et al., 2024) measures expert-level scientific reasoning. VoiceBench (Chen et al., 2026) spans multiple dimensions of voice assistant capability. We select four subsets with objective metrics that assess knowledge, reasoning, and instruction following. These subsets are OpenBookQA, MMSU, BBH, and IFEval. Qwen2.5-Omni-7B (Xu et al., 2025a) is the primary backbone. We compare direct answering, ex-plicit CoT, existing latent-reasoning baselines, and AURAL, together with RL-trained variants where applicable. The external latent baselines are Coconut (Hao et al., 2025), CODI (Shen et al., 2025), and CoLaR (Tan et al., 2026). All RL variants use the same prompts, schedule, and reward, with length counted in latent states or CoT tokens. Kimi-Audio (KimiTeam et al., 2025) provides a second architecture for testing transfer. Unless stated otherwise, AURAL uses compression factor

CODI perform worse than the other latent methods on most benchmarks. They lack direct supervi-sion for intermediate states and use a fixed latent length, which may make latent trajectories harder to learn and prevent depth from adapting to question difficulty. AURAL-RL leads both EchoMind metrics and both MMSU evaluations, which rely heavily on spoken and paralinguistic information. The margins are small but consistent with latent states retaining cues that are hard to verbalize. A closer comparison of the latent variants shows the empirical effects of each AURAL design choice. Compared with Latent MSE , Latent GMM improves ten metrics and ties one, suggesting that mod-eling multiple plausible next-state continuations is more effective than predicting a single state. AURAL-SFT then improves ten of eleven metrics over Latent GMM , consistent with an additional benefit from jointly modeling relations among adjacent latent states. This flexibility becomes espe-cially important in AURAL-RL. Its SFT-to-RL gain exceeds CoT’s on most metrics under our setup. These larger gains likely arise because outcome rewards allow the distributional latent policy to ex-plore multiple continuous trajectories and reinforce those that yield stronger final answers, rather than remaining tied to the single CoT path used for supervision. Separately, the MSE model gains less from RL than the CoLaR and GMM variants, consistent with distributional policies offering broader exploration than point prediction. The results on Kimi-Audio align with the overall pattern on Qwen2.5-Omni. AURAL-SFT again lies between Base and CoT on most measures, and AURAL-RL improves all eleven of its scores. AURAL-RL remains competitive with CoT-RL, while its larger benefit from RL recurs across most evaluation metrics. Shorter CoTs provide cleaner supervision. Section 4 creates paired GeneralSpeech examples with the same question and answer but either the original or shortened CoT. We fine-tune one Qwen2.5-Omni model on each set with the same setup and evaluate both on GPQA. The answers remain unchanged even when wrong, making the CoT the only difference in supervision. Shortening reduces the mean trace length from 317.5 to 65.3 tokens and raises GPQA accuracy from 29.85% to 35.35%. This result supports concise CoTs as a cleaner training signal. 

Do GMM components represent different valid paths? We copy the same intermediate reason-ing state four times. The copies share the question and latent prefix, but a different GMM component produces the next latent chunk in each one. AURAL then continues from each chunk to a final an-swer. This isolates the effect of choosing a component. We sample one component four times to test whether randomness alone creates different paths. We also replace the components with one Gaus-sian covering the same overall region, asking whether one wide distribution works without separate components. If the original GMM still yields more varied correct paths, its components encode useful alternatives. Different paths are useful only if their difference exceeds random variation and both answer cor-rectly. Table 3 reports how often a question meets both conditions. Nearly four in ten questions meet this criterion when the four components are used. This is about three times as frequent as sampling one component and more than twice as frequent as merging the components. Individual accuracy changes little, so the difference cannot be explained by extra noise. Voting provides a second test. It selects the most frequent final answer among the four paths and is used only as a diagnostic. If the components are redundant, their vote should be no better than voting over four samples from one component. Instead, it performs better on all three benchmarks, showing that the component paths make complementary errors. Together with the K = 1 ablation in Table 2, these results suggest that the GMM keeps plausible next steps separate and gives RL more than one route to a correct answer. Appendix E gives the exact protocol. 

Harder questions elicit deeper latent reasoning. AURAL-RL’s adaptive stopping rule is useful only if it reserves depth for questions that need it. To separate task difficulty from speech prop-erties, we analyze GPQA and VoiceBench BBH, whose solutions rely little on paralinguistic cues. DeepSeek-V4-Flash rates the difficulty of each question on a scale from 1 to 5 without seeing the benchmark identity, latent trajectory, model answer, or reference. Mean latent depth rises from 13.27 to 15.07 across GPQA difficulty levels 3 to 5 and from 10.68 to 13.47 across BBH levels 1 to 93. Levels with too few examples are omitted. The same progression on both benchmarks indicates that AURAL-RL allocates more depth to harder questions rather than using a fixed budget. 

## 6 CONCLUSION 

We introduced AURAL, a framework for SLMs that replaces token-by-token CoT at inference with continuous latent chunks, models multiple plausible continuations with a GMM, and uses RL to adapt reasoning depth. We also constructed AuralReason-683K, a bilingual corpus of 683K speech utterances, and showed across two SLM backbones that AURAL-RL remains competitive with CoT while achieving an 11 .8× speedup in mean time to first token. Overall, AURAL brings deep rea-soning closer to the immediacy required for spoken interaction.

## ETHICS STATEMENT 

This work constructs a speech reasoning corpus from public videos, podcasts, films, and existing speech datasets, and trains models to reason about paralinguistic cues. Inferring affect from voice can shade into profiling, so CoT supervision is limited to acoustic impressions that bear on the response. Perceived age, gender, or accent may appear only in this form, for example when a child’s voice calls for simpler wording. Traces describe how the voice sounds rather than who the speaker is, and inferences about stable personality, illness, and social status are excluded. Crisis, medical, and self harm content receives dedicated safety handling. The audio carries the licenses of its sources, several of which permit research use only, so redistribution follows the terms of each source and uses permitted subsets with source identifiers where waveform redistribution is restricted. No new human subjects were recruited, and because the recorded speakers did not consent to this specific use, we treat that as a reason to limit release rather than broaden it. Reasoning traces are generated by teacher models and may carry their biases, including biased impressions of age, gender, or accent.

Latent scheduled sampling. LSS follows the two-pass procedure in Section 3.1. A teacher-forced pass first computes hidden states at every gold latent position. The joint head is queried only at the stride-m anchors used by decoding, so its sampled chunks tile the latent inputs; the last chunk uses only its valid slots. A second backbone pass replaces the gold latent inputs by these samples and applies the same CSA, EOL, and response objectives. The latent head is still queried at gold anchors, so LSS trains the backbone and language head on sampled inputs, while drift across successive chunks is left to AURAL-RL rollouts. The sampled component index κi is hard and treated with stop-gradient. The reparameterized con-tinuous sample is not detached, so the second-pass language loss updates the selected means, scales, loadings, global directions B, and the backbone through feedback; the supervised mixture NLL supplies the direct signal for the categorical mixture and all components.

## D RESPONSE LATENCY 

Faster reasoning matters in a speech interface when it lets the assistant begin answering sooner. We therefore measure both time to first token (TTFT), ending at the first visible answer token, and full response latency, ending at the last visible token. A stage decomposition connects these interaction measures to the reasoning savings in Section 5.3. D.1 MEASUREMENT PROTOCOL 

Timing boundaries. Each request arrives with its complete waveform, whose mean duration is 

15 .37 seconds in the measured set. We record five stages of response computation. • Preprocessing decodes the waveform, extracts features, and tokenizes the prompt on the CPU. It is identical across systems and excluded from all reported times. • Prefill runs the audio tower, merges audio and text embeddings, and processes the prompt. • Reasoning generates the CoT through its complete closing </think> tag, or the latent chunks through EOL. • Answer onset completes the output scaffolding and reaches the first visible answer token. This includes the <think> and <answer> tags for CoT-RL and the single forced close anchor for AURAL. • Answer body generates the remaining visible response through the end-of-sequence token or the answer budget. TTFT includes prefill, reasoning, and answer onset. Full response latency adds the answer body. Both measure model computation after CPU preprocessing, with the time spent uttering the input excluded. Ending TTFT at the first visible answer token captures when text becomes available for speech synthesis. 

Paired comparison. The five systems process the same 256 examples from Appendix B. Each runs sequentially on the same physical NVIDIA H800, with one process owning the GPU and one request in flight. All use direct Transformers inference, greedy visible decoding, bfloat16, FlashAt-tention, batch size 1, and the same per-example seeds. A shared runtime supports both explicit and latent reasoning. Generation budgets are the one setting that could shorten a measured time regardless of how a system reasons. Every system receives the same per-task budget for its visible answer, and CoT-RL receives a further completion budget of at least 1,024 tokens because its output must also cover the think span. On CoT-RL and both AURAL-RL settings, every measured example finished before reaching either budget, so the compared times reflect where each system chose to stop. 

Instrumentation and aggregation. CUDA is synchronized at each stage boundary, so each recorded time reflects completed computation rather than kernel submission. Every token receives a wall-clock timestamp immediately after the host reads its sampled identifier, which already forces a device synchronization, so the timestamps add no measurement overhead. Marker positions are recovered from stored identifiers after generation rather than during it, which keeps string handling out of the timed path. All speedups are ratios of mean latencies over paired examples. Each CoT preserves the steps needed to support its response. A step introduces evidence, derives a consequence, rules out an alternative, repairs a contradiction, or verifies a conclusion.
