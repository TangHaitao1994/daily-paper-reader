ABSTRACT 

End-to-end (E2E) full-duplex spoken language models (FD-SLMs) enable simu-tanous listening and speaking continously, requiring models to take appropriate interaction actions as conversations unfold. However, existing benchmarks lack a structured action-level formulation, often evaluate action occurrence rather than success, and provide limited coverage of diverse real-world conversations. We introduce DUPLEX ACT , a benchmark for evaluating interaction action taking in E2E FD-SLMs. DUPLEX ACT defines a three-level action taxonomy spanning eight interaction actions and 24 corresponding interaction conditions. We fur-ther characterize successful action-taking through immediate execution and sub-sequent follow-through , and develop action-specific evaluation metrics for both. DUPLEX ACT contains 4,691 test instances spanning casual conversation, meet-ings, customer service, and interviews, including 2,531 instances derived from real-world conversational recordings. Together, D UPLEX ACT provides a struc-tured and real-world-grounded framework for systematically assessing how E2E FD-SLMs participate in ongoing spoken interaction. Code and data are available at https://github.com/JiahengDong/DuplexAct .

1 INTRODUCTION 

End-to-end (E2E) full-duplex spoken language models (FD-SLMs) are advancing spoken human– AI interaction beyond rigid command–response exchanges toward more natural and collaborative communication (Wang et al., 2025a; D´ efossez et al., 2024; Yao et al., 2025). By continuously listen-ing while speaking, these models allow interaction to unfold without fixed boundaries between user and model turns, accommodating conversational patterns such as interruptions and backchannels. Navigating such interaction requires the model to continually determine whether, when, and how to participate in response to the evolving conversational context. Current E2E FD-SLMs typically learn interaction control implicitly from conversational sequence modelling, capturing the temporal and contextual patterns that govern turn-taking from dialogue data. Some models further introduce dedicated control tokens to represent specific decisions, such as starting or stopping speech, main-taining silence, or producing a backchannel (Kim et al., 2026; Cui et al., 2026; Fang et al., 2026), embedding interaction control directly within the generative process. However, current E2E FD-SLMs lack an explicitly defined and structured interaction-action space ,leading to fragmented action-level supervision and evaluation. Existing benchmarks (Lin et al., 2025; 2026c; Peng et al., 2025; He et al., 2026) further reflect this absence: they typically evaluate only a subset of actions, such as turn-taking, backchannels, and interruptions, or organize evaluation around specific phenomena such as overlapping speech, producing results that are neither compara-ble nor comprehensive at action level. In contrast, human Interactional Competence (IC) research offers a more principled model: it structures conversational participation around clearly defined actions: turn management (starting, maintaining, and ending turns), interactive listening (backchan-nels and continuers), and breakdown repair (Galaczi & Taylor, 2018). The absence of an structured action space further leaves the success criteria for actions under-specified. Existing benchmarks rely on metrics such as takeover rate, response latency, and backchannel frequency (Lin et al., 2025). These measures capture whether a behavior occurs, but not whether it succeeds. Both sequential decision-making and IC research make this point: an ac-tion is conditioned on the current conversational state and defined by its relation to both preceding context and subsequent response (Deppermann & Gubina, 2026; May et al., 2020). A backchannel, for instance, achieves successful interactive listening only if the listener’s next turn develops the partner’s ideas or asks a relevant follow-up (May et al., 2020). Evaluating interaction control there-fore requires success criteria that capture two distinct dimensions: immediate execution , whether the action is performed appropriately at the right moment, and subsequent follow-through , whether the model’s behavior remains consistent with that action as the interaction unfolds. Existing benchmarks miss the latter altogether and address the first only partially. 

Datasets with complete action coverage and action-specific success annotations remain lacking. 

Existing benchmarks fall short on three fronts: they cover only a subset of interaction actions, an-notate occurrence rather than success, and rely heavily on synthetic or scripted interactions. These gaps compound: without a complete action space, annotations cannot capture whether each action is executed appropriately and followed through, only whether a surface behavior is present. Com-prehensive evaluation therefore requires a dataset spanning the full action space with fine-grained success annotations across both execution and follow-through dimensions, grounded in real-world interactions and complemented by targeted synthesis where natural data are scarce. To address these limitations, we propose DUPLEX ACT , benchmarking interactive capabilities of E2E FD-SLMs. Grounded in human Interactional Competence, D UPLEX ACT provides a structured formulation of interaction actions in Figure 1, with action-specific definitions and evaluation criteria, diverse conversational scenarios, and real-world spoken interactions. Our contributions are: • A structured interaction-action taxonomy. We propose a structured 3-level hierarchical taxon-omy spanning eight interaction actions across 24 conditions that provide a principled framework for evaluating how full-duplex SLMs participate in spoken interaction. • Action-specific success criteria. We introduce a two-part structure characterising successful action-taking through immediate execution and subsequent follow-through , with corresponding metrics for each action. • A large-scale, real-world-grounded evaluation dataset. 2 RELATED WORK 

End-to-End Full-Duplex SLMs. Recent E2E FD-SLMs internalize interaction control within the generative model, learning turn-taking and other conversational dynamics from dialogue data (D´ efossez et al., 2024; Fang et al., 2026). These decisions are often represented through model-specific control tokens or signals: BayLing-Duplex uses [SILENCE] /[ASSISTANT] 

for dialogue-state control (Fang et al., 2026), while Raon-SpeechChat further introduces an 

audio output backchannel token to distinguish backchanneling from general speech initi-ation (Kim et al., 2026). Lychee-FD similarly uses a dedicated control head with Start /Stop 

signals (Liu et al., 2026). However, these controls are defined at different granularities and for individual model requirements, lacking a structured and common interaction-action space for sys-tematically representing and evaluating conversational actions. 

Full-Duplex SLM Benchmarks Existing full-duplex benchmarks, compared in Table 1, lack a structured action space and evaluate only arbitrary subsets of conversational actions. Moreover, they mix model actions and the conversational conditions under which those actions are expected to occur, at a single flat level. FDB v1 places model actions such as turn-taking and backchannel-ing alongside conversation conditions such as user pauses and user interruptions (Lin et al., 2025). FDB v1.5 and FD-Bench organize evaluation around overlapping-speech conditions such as user interruption and background speech (Lin et al., 2026c; Peng et al., 2025), while HumDial-FDBench evaluates rejection across conditions including third-party speech (Wang et al., 2026). Mixing what action the model should perform with the conditions under which it applies , which operate at dif-ferent levels of granularity, limits systematic action-level characterization of model capabilities. A structured, multi-level action space is therefore required. 

Evaluation Metrics. Existing metrics measure whether a behavior occurs and how promptly such as takeover rates, response latency, and backchannel occurrence (Lin et al., 2025; 2026c; Peng et al., 2025), but not whether the action is successfully carried through as the interaction unfolds. A model that backchannels disruptively and one that backchannels appropriately score identically on occurrence, but only one is listening. Evaluations should therefore assess not just whether an action occurs, but whether it succeeds, e.g., performed appropriately and followed through (Table 1).

Figure 2: Overview of the D UPLEX ACT benchmark framework. 3.1 INTERACTION ACTION SPACE AND CONDITION CONSTRUCTION 

We formulate full-duplex interaction as a three-level taxonomy separating participation state (L0), interaction actions (L1), and the conditions (L2) under which actions are required (Figure 2). L0 rep-resents the model’s observable participation state: speak or silence . While L0 is directly observable, it alone is insufficient for evaluation. 

L1 defines eight interaction actions that form the core of our evaluation framework. We consolidate and adapt these from three categories in human interaction research: turn management, interactive listening, and breakdown repair (Galaczi & Taylor, 2018; May et al., 2020). Turn management cov-ers starting, maintaining, ending, and interrupting a turn, adapted as Take Turn , Hold Floor , Stop ,and Interrupt . Interactive listening, concerned with how a listener displays recipiency while an-other participant speaks, is realized as Backchannel for active listenership, and Wait and Ignore for basic and selective listening in continuous human–model interaction. Breakdown repair, which en-compasses responses to interactional disruption, is abstracted as Recover , capturing how the model should behave after being interrupted. 

L2 specifies the conversational conditions under which each L1 action is required, yielding 24 action–condition pairs. This separation enables evaluation of not only whether a model can per-form an action, but whether it can recognize the condition and adapt its action accordingly: Take Turn after a completed but complex or simple turn, and an explicit invitation each require different turn-entry behaviour from the model. We systematically consolidate conditions from prior bench-marks and model evaluations (Lin et al., 2025; 2026c; Peng et al., 2025; Ge et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026; Chiang et al., 2026) and extend them to underrepresented settings such as safety-related In-terrupt , for instance, spans both home and driving domains. Organizing these conditions within a unified taxonomy makes action–condition coverage explicit and comparable across models for the first time. Detailed mappings are provided in Appendix A. 3.2 BENCHMARK DATA CURATION 

We construct dataset to cover the 24 action–condition pairs defined in our taxonomy, drawing pri-marily from real-world conversational corpora and resorting to controlled synthesis only when nat-ural data are insufficient for a particular condition. Instances span four conversational scenarios: casual conversation, meetings, customer service, and interviews, representing distinct interaction norms and expectations (Schegloff, 2007; Femø Nielsen, 2026), as shown in Figure 2B. 

Real-world interaction extraction. We collect naturally occurring conversations from CAN-DOR (Reece et al., 2023) for casual conversation, AMI (McCowan et al., 2005) for meetings, Loqui (Passonneau & Sachar, 2014) for customer service, and CORAAL (Kendall & Farrington, 2023) for interviews. For each L2 condition, candidate segments expressing the target action are first identified through action-specific signals: speaker transitions for turn-taking actions, overlap-ping speech for interruption and backchannel, and user speech without model response for listening actions. Each candidate is then verified by GPT 5.4, which confirms the intended action is occurring and assigns the correct L2 condition label. Only segments passing both stages are retained. 

Controlled interaction synthesis. When existing conversational corpora do not provide sufficient samples for an L2 condition, we generate controlled conversational transcripts using GPT-5.4, fol-4Preprint lowing the transcript-to-speech synthesis pipeline adopted in τ -Voice (Ray et al., 2026). As illus-trated in Figure 2, generation is controlled by four prompts specifying the scenario , action , con-dition , and setting , where the setting prompt varies conversation topics and physical environment to increase diversity within each scenario. The generated transcripts are then rendered into speech using ElevenLabs v3 with diverse realistic personas following the configuration in τ -Voice. 3.3.1 ACTION SUCCESS DEFINITION AND METRICS 

Successful action-taking is evaluated at two stages as shown in Figure 2D. Immediate execution measures whether the expected action occurs at the target interaction point and with appropriate timing. We further propose Subse-quent follow-through , measuring whether the ensuing interaction remains consistent with that action as the conversation develops. This two-stage formulation reflects the sequential organization of hu-man interaction, where the appropriateness of an action is validated not by its occurrence but by the conduct that follows (Galaczi & Taylor, 2018; May et al., 2020; Deppermann & Gubina, 2026). We evaluate each test instance against action-specific success criteria at two stages, as summarized in Table 3. Immediate execution is determined automatically by rule-based criteria, checking whether the model produces the expected action at the correct interaction point. For example, whether it takes the turn within the expected response window or falls silent upon a stop signal. Subsequent follow-through is assessed by GPT-5.4 against an action-specific checklist, checking whether the model’s behavior after the action remains consistent with it. For example, whether a model that recovers after an interruption adapts its resumed response accordingly, or whether one that backchannels uncertainty asks a relevant follow-up question. We report two success rates accordingly. Immediate execution success rate (I) is the proportion of instances satisfiy the immediate criterion, while subsequent follow-through success rate (S) is the proportion of immediate-success instances that also satisfy the corresponding subsequent criterion. Both rates are micro-averaged across the L2 conditions associated with each action, with condition-specific constraints applied where required. A model may achieve high I but low S when it performs the expected action at the interaction point but fails to sustain the behaviour that should follow, suggesting the model may learn the surface pattern of an action without reasoning how that action should embed in and shape the conversational flow that follows. This is precisely the gap that metrics based only on immediate occurrence cannot reveal. Detailed criteria and metrics are in Appendix B 3.3.2 INTERACTION PIPELINE 

We use two interaction pipelines depending on how each action is triggered: Direct Replay, where the model responds to a fixed audio prompt, and Simulator Replay, where a user simulator drives live bidirectional interaction. Both preserve natural interaction dynamics while maintaining a controlled and reproducible evaluation setting. 

Direct replay. As shown in Figure 2C, for wait , ignore , take turn , and backchannel , the evaluation condition is fully determined by the input audio and does not rely on the model’s ongoing response. We therefore use direct replay, where each instance is streamed to the model at its required input frame rate while model output is recorded concurrently. 

Simulator-based interaction. For Stop , Hold Floor , Recover , and Interrupt , the user-side inter-action timing must adapt to the model’s ongoing response and therefore cannot be predetermined by a fixed input timeline. 5 RESULTS 

5.1 IMMEDIATE EXECUTION PERFORMANCE 

Table 4 summarizes action-taking performance across the evaluated models. We first examine imme-diate execution to characterize shared action-level difficulties and model-specific capability profiles. 

Model capability varies across actions. Model interactive capability varies substantially across interaction actions, even when the actions require similar immediate execution. Wait and Ignore both require the model to remain silent, but the median immediate success rates over all models differ substantially at 94.0% and 75.4%. The same separation appears between Take Turn at the proper time and Recover , which both require the model to enter the floor. The four close-source models achieve 77.7–97.0% on Recover , but only 27.5–41.3% on Take Turn within the required response interval. The pattern is clear: capability on one action does not imply capability on another, even when their immediate execution is similar. Comprehensive evaluation of model interactive capability therefore requires a structured action space that evaluates these capabilities separately. 

Action-specific supervision is associated with selective strengths. Models with targeted super-vision for specific actions show clear strengths precisely where that supervision applies. Raon-SpeechChat, trained with a dedicated BC token and a backchannel loss component, achieves the highest open-source Backchannel score (31.0%) (Kim et al., 2026). BayLing-Duplex, explicitly trained on user interruptions, achieves 98.8% on Stop and 49.0% on Recover (Fang et al., 2026), where both actions evaluate the cases when a user interrupts an ongoing response. The pattern is consistent: targeted supervision only improves the actions it covers. No model performs consistently well across the full action set, revealing that current training remains concentrated on a narrow sub-set of interaction behaviours. Action-level capability extends beyond a single interaction pattern. Condition-level analysis reveals variation in model capability that action-level scores alone cannot capture. As shown in Fig-ure 3a, Take Turn exhibits substantial condition-level variation: success reaches 60.9% for delayed entry, but only 8.0% for immediate entry and 20.9% for slight overlap. In contrast, Recover , which similarly requires the model to re-enter the conversation floor, remains relatively stable across its six conditions (49.3–55.1%). This contrast suggests that the main difficulty is not simply deciding to take the floor, but adapting when to do so across different conditions. Models appear to follow a learned turn-entry pattern rather than adjusting action timing to different conversational context. Therefore, action-level capability requires robust performance across conditions, revealing whether models can adapt action-taking to different interaction requirements rather than executing in a fixed way. 5.2 SUBSEQUENT FOLLOW -THROUGH BEYOND IMMEDIATE EXECUTION 

Action-taking performance is further examined through subsequent follow-through. This is evalu-ated only on cases in which the model successes in immediate execution. 

Immediate execution does not guarantee successful action taking. Figure 4 shows that subse-quent follow-through failure remains widespread even after successful immediate execution. For 

Interrupt , Moshi achieves 67.5% immediate success in Table 4, but reaches to almost 100% subse-quent failure rate, which indicates none of these successful interruptions subsequently carrie out the required intervention. The same separation appears even in simpler actions such as Wait , where sev-eral models successfully remain silent but subsequently fail to incorporate the ongoing information that motivated waiting for some specific intervals. The distinction becomes clearer at the condition level. For Hold Floor , average immediate success remain high in Figure 3a when the model should continue, shorten, or simplify its response (90.5%, 85.4%, and 88.9%), but subsequent success rate in Figure 3b remains 73.3% for continuing and falls to only 4.5% and 5.7% for shortening and simplifying. Models can therefore recognize to maintain the floor when the user provides a backchannel, but often fail to understand why the user inter-vened and how their ongoing response should adapt accordingly. Immediate execution alone can consequently overestimate action-taking capability: in many cases, models learn when to perform an action without understanding its consequence. 

Base-model reasoning capability does not automatically transfer to action taking. Successful follow-through requires more than strong reasoning capability in the base model before full-duplex post-training: the model must apply this capability to infer why an action is required based on both the current conversational context and the intended consequences of executing it. This contrast suggests that reasoning transfers more readily to generating a new answer than to managing the purpose and consequences of actions during concurrent listening and speak-ing. Recent work addresses the loss of base-model capabilities during full-duplex post-training (Liu et al., 2026). Our results highlight a complementary challenge: full-duplex post-training should not merely preserve or strengthen those capabilities, but explicitly connect them to the conversational circumstances that motivate an action and the subsequent behaviour that fulfils its purpose. 5.3 PERFORMANCE ACROSS INTERACTION SCENARIOS 

Figure 5 compares action-taking performance across conversational scenarios to reveal how perfor-mance varies across different interactional norms. Actions with high success rates, including Wait ,

Ignore , Stop , remain strong regardless of setting, suggesting their interactional norms are largely shared across contexts and capabilities learned in one setting could transfer to others. Beyond the shared norms, scenario-specific characteristics still affect immediate execution. For instance, Wait is weaker in meetings, suggesting that models trained predominantly on casual con-versational patterns, where opinion exchanges may be faster and shorter, are less well calibrated to structured multi-party exchanges, where speakers may hold the floor through longer pauses while developing their opinions. Scenario differences become more pronounced when immediate execu-tion is compared with subsequent follow-through. For actions such as Wait and Take Turn , models may reproduce similar action-taking patterns across scenarios from local acoustic or turn-taking cues, while the subsequent response is less transferrable because the action understanding is not sufficiently grounded in the conversational contexts during training. The broad coverage of our benchmark enables the evaluation across conversation settings, and suggests that full-duplex training should cover a broader range of conversational scenarios, exposing models to different interaction norms and conversation contexts. 

6 LIMITATIONS AND CONCLUSION 

Conclusions. We introduce D UPLEX ACT , systematically evaluating action-taking in E2E FD-SLMs. It builds on a structured taxonomy of interaction actions and conditions, and a two-part evaluation framework of immediate execution and subsequent follow-through. Our findings show that current models exhibit highly uneven action-taking capabilities. Targeted supervision produces selective action strengths, but no model performs consistently well across the full action space. Crucially, successful immediate execution also frequently fails to carry through to the subsequent behavior required by an action, highlighting a gap between immediate interaction control and the 9Preprint semantic consequences of that decision. These findings motivate a more structured view of full-duplex interaction in which models must learn not only when an action should occur, but also what that action requires as the interaction continues using a shared action space. More broadly, D U-

> PLEX

ACT establishes a framework through which progress in full-duplex interaction can be tracked at the action level, also guiding future model development accordingly. 

Limitations. DUPLEX ACT incorporates more than 2,000 real-world recordings. As naturally oc-curring interaction phenomena are not equally distributed, conditions with limited natural coverage are complemented by targeted synthesis: a balance that future work can continue to improve with additional conversational corpora and their action annotations. Simulator Replay enables rigorous live bidirectional evaluation with temporally aligned streaming signals, though its current design restricts assessment to models with compatible interfaces. Future work on more flexible simulator designs would broaden coverage to a wider range of full-duplex model interfaces.

Second, a text-based judge determines whether the candidate is a genuine, self-contained backchan-nel that provides listener feedback while leaving the floor with the user. Candidates that form the truncated beginning of a substantive turn, contain no intelligible speech, or do not express a listener-feedback function are rejected. Third, for candidates confirmed as genuine backchannels, an audio-capable judge classifies the feed-back function as affirmative or uncertain . The judge does not receive the target condition or the can-didate transcript. Immediate execution is successful only when a genuine backchannel is confirmed and its predicted function matches the function specified by the interaction condition. 26 Preprint 

Subsequent follow-through. Conditional on successful immediate execution, subsequent follow-through evaluates whether the model’s later substantive speech remains consistent with the under-standing or stance expressed by the backchannel. A change in stance is permitted when it is justified by user speech occurring after the backchannel. If no later model speech is produced, subsequent follow-through is unsuccessful.

C INTERACTION PIPELINE DETAILS 

This section provides additional details of the two interaction pipelines introduced in Section 3.3.2. C.1 DIRECT REPLAY 

Each Direct Replay instance is evaluated in an independent session using a fixed user-audio input. The input is streamed in real time according to the model’s native input configuration, while model output is recorded concurrently on the same time-aligned input–output timeline. The timing origin is defined by the first streamed input sample rather than connection establishment, ensuring that interaction timestamps are comparable across model interfaces. A fixed 15 s observation period is appended after the user content to capture responses following the target interaction point. Model speech segments used for evaluation are derived from the recorded output timeline rather than directly from provider-side turn-detection events. Provider-side voice activity or turn detection may control when a model begins generating a response, but does not define the scored response onset. This allows the action-specific timing criteria in Appendix B to be applied consistently across different model interfaces. 29 Preprint C.2 SIMULATOR -B ASED INTERACTION 

Simulator-based evaluation retains fixed benchmark audio while adapting interaction timing to the model’s ongoing behaviour. The simulator uses GPT-5.4 as a decision model and operates only as an interaction controller; it does not generate the content or speech of the simulated user online. All user cues are prerecorded benchmark audio, allowing their linguistic and acoustic content to remain fixed across evaluated models. 

Cue injection for Stop, Hold Floor, and Recover. These actions use a lead-in followed by a prerecorded interaction cue. The lead-in is first streamed to elicit an ongoing model response. Once the model is speaking, the simulator observes its model-provided streaming transcript together with the reference response and cue information associated with the benchmark instance, and determines an appropriate point at which to inject the fixed cue. If no suitable point is identified within the permitted interaction period, the cue is force-injected to ensure that the benchmark opportunity is presented. A force-injected trial is not automatically treated as invalid; its inclusion in action evaluation depends on whether the cue actually occurs while the model is speaking. For model interfaces whose streaming transcript can advance ahead of audible output, the tran-script exposed to the simulator is aligned with the progression of model playback. This prevents the simulator from making an insertion decision using model content that has not yet reached the corre-sponding interaction point, while avoiding an additional speech-recognition stage over the rendered audio. After cue playback, the interaction continues for a fixed observation period to capture the model’s immediate and subsequent behaviour. 

Barge-in interaction for Interrupt. For Interrupt , the interaction direction is reversed: the sim-ulated user continuously holds the floor while the model is allowed to intervene. The simulator monitors the model’s ongoing output and determines whether detected model speech represents an attempt to take the turn rather than a brief backchannel or filler. When a takeover is detected, the remaining unsent user audio is stopped so that the interaction can proceed from the model’s inter-vention; otherwise, the user utterance continues to completion. The simulator decision in this pipeline controls only the progression of the interaction and is not itself used as the Interrupt evaluation outcome. Immediate execution is independently computed from the recorded model-speech timeline using the timing criterion defined in Appendix B.6.
