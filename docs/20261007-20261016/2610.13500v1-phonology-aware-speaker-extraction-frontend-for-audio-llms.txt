ABSTRACT 

Target speaker extraction (TSE) is a key frontend for enabling audio large language models (LLMs) to recog-nize and understand a designated speaker in overlapping speech. However, multilingual mixtures exhibit het-erogeneous extraction demands, causing fixed-depth systems to either under-process challenging inputs or waste computation on inputs that have already con-verged. Existing TSE frontends generally apply uniform computation, while Audio-LLM adaptation methods neither explicitly model language-conditioned extrac-tion demands nor preserve plug-and-play compatibility with frozen downstream models. We therefore propose LATTE, a lightweight 5.2M-parameter frontend that combines an acoustic Difficulty Estimator, a task-adaptive phonology-informed prior learned from multi-depth TSE gains, and an Adaptive Hourglass Extractor to route each mixture without requiring language identity at inference. On PolyExtract, LATTE achieves a macro SI-SNRi of 15.07 dB at 0.30 GFLOPs, retaining performance within 0.09 dB of a 0.53-GFLOP fixed-depth model while reducing computation by 43.4%, and lowers the average WER from 16.8% to 14.6% relative to TF-GridNet. By jointly modeling sample-level extraction demands and phonological structure, LATTE provides a practical framework for quality-aware, compute-efficient multi-lingual TSE and offers a scalable path toward robust Audio-LLM deployment. 

Index Terms — target speaker extraction, audio large language models, multilingual speech, adaptive computa-tion, phonological modeling 

1. INTRODUCTION 

Target speaker extraction (TSE) recovers a designated speaker from an overlapping mixture using a short enroll-ment utterance. Existing systems typically apply the same network depth to every input [1, 2, 3]. Our study begins with an overlooked empirical observation: under matched mixture conditions, speech from different languages can require substantially different amounts of computation to reach a common extraction-quality target. Controlled multi-depth experiments reveal, however, that multilin-gual conditions can require markedly different amounts of computation to approach their best extraction qual-ity. A shallow budget under-processes demanding inputs, whereas a deep budget repeatedly refines inputs that have already converged. This motivates our central question: 

can a TSE system estimate the demand of each input and execute only the computation needed for reliable extrac-tion? 

Why can extraction demand vary across lan-guages? Cross-linguistic variation in tone, phonation, rhythm, and segmental structure changes the acoustic organization of linguistic content and speaker cues [4, 5]. Because these factors interact with mixture acoustics and target identity, we treat language structure as a training prior rather than a deterministic or intrinsic measure of language difficulty. Specifically, we derive a task-adaptive 

Phonological Difficulty Index (PDI) from the minimum processing depth needed to approach deepest-exit quality. URIEL+ and PHOIBLE [6, 7] provide a phonological similarity graph that smooths this task-observed evidence across related training languages. We propose LATTE , a phonology-aware adaptive TSE model evaluated on PolyExtract , a controlled bench-mark covering eight languages and 64 directed target– interferer conditions. LATTE combines a sample-level Difficulty Estimator with a shared multi-exit Hourglass Extractor. The estimator predicts an easy, medium, or hard computation budget from mixture features and the target-speaker embedding, routing each input through two, four, or six shared blocks. PDI supplies weak supervision only during training; inference requires neither language iden-tity nor access to an external linguistic database. LATTE also serves as a lightweight waveform frontend for frozen audio-language backends, avoiding backend-specific adaptation such as LoRA [8]. On PolyExtract, it achieves 15.07 dB macro SI-SNRi at 0.30 GFLOPs, remaining within 0.09 dB of the deep-est fixed path while reducing computation by 43.4%. arXiv:2610.13500v1 [cs.SD] 9 Oct 2026 Fig. 1 . 3. EXPERIMENTS 3.1. Experimental setup and PolyExtract 

LATTE has 5.2M parameters and is trained with Adam ( 10 −3 learning rate, 10 −5 weight decay), mixed precision, and gradient clipping. Training uses online 4-s mixtures at [−5, 5] dB; validation and test mixtures are fixed. All signals are resampled to 16 kHz, and the STFT uses a 512-point FFT with a 128-sample hop. PolyExtract contains eight languages from five families, drawn from LibriSpeech, AISHELL-1, NCHLT, VIVOS, and Common Voice [13, 14, 15, 16, 17]. We evaluate all 64 directed target–interferer pairs with equal sample counts and matched mixture controls.

LATTE leads on Cantonese, Vietnamese, Polish, Zulu, and Kinyarwanda, where adaptive extraction better handles interference than a uniformly processed frontend.

4. CONCLUSION 

LATTE is a lightweight adaptive TSE frontend for frozen Audio LLMs. PDI captures model-specific language-level compute demand from multi-depth ev-idence, while routing remains language-label free at inference. It is task-adaptive and improves routing over task-PDI-only ( 15 .07 vs. 14 .88 dB at 0.30 GFLOPs). Overall, task-observed multi-exit evidence and sample acoustics enable near-oracle, language-label-free routing through one shared extractor, while its waveform interface Fig. 3 . Evaluation of difficulty estimation and adaptive computation. (a) Calibration of the predicted compute demand against the task-observed oracle target. Large markers denote demand-level means, and the dashed line denotes perfect calibration. (b) Quality–computation comparison among fixed-depth and adaptive routing strategies. The upper-left region represents higher extraction quality with lower average computation. remains compatible with frozen downstream backends. The shared exits turn the estimated demand into an explicit computational budget: easier mixtures stop after a short processing prefix, whereas harder mixtures receive additional refinement without maintaining separate language-specific experts. The component study further shows that WavLM context and fine-grained spectral detail provide complementary evidence for extraction and reconstruction. Together with waveform-level output, this separation allows one adaptive frontend to support different frozen downstream systems while leaving their parameters unchanged. PolyExtract covers eight languages and controlled two-speaker mixtures, so generalization to broader language, accent, and real-world overlap conditions remains unverified. Future work should evaluate unseen languages, accents, and real-world overlap, together with deployment latency and energy.
