# RUBATO: Decoupled Clocks Allow Full-Duplex Models to Think as Fast as They Can 

Yi-Jen Shih 1,2,∗, Puyuan Peng 2, Haohe Liu 2, David Harwath 1, Abdelrahman Mohamed 21The University of Texas at Austin, 2FAIR, Meta Superintelligence Labs 

> ∗

Work done at Meta 

Recent Speech Full-Duplex (FDX) models are capable of engaging in naturalistic conversation by listening and speaking at the same time. Building on this foundation, recent architectures introduce internal thinking streams to enhance their intelligence. To guarantee these streams process successfully in real time, existing methods enforce a fixed decoding rate, which assumes a hardware profile capable of a specific latency. We argue this static approach is severely suboptimal. Because internal thinking is intrinsically asynchronous, an ideal FDX model should sustain real-time conversational flow while allowing each stream to decode at its own hardware-adaptive rate. Thinking streams must process as fast as the hardware allows to deliver accurate answers faster. To solve this, we propose Rubato ,a novel multistream positional encoding that assigns independent clocks to entirely decouple the streams. This structural isolation empowers the model to zero-shot interpolate across any inference decoding speed. It drastically simplifies dataset construction by requiring training on only two extreme decoding speeds while smoothly adapting to any intermediate rate during deployment. Rubato 

demonstrates consistent robustness across five spoken reasoning tasks. On Spoken GSM8K, a baseline model collapses to 1.7% accuracy at interpolated speeds. Conversely, Rubato -Full sustains 48.7% to 51.1% accuracy across all rates, leveraging faster hardware to achieve a 6.5 × shorter time-to-answer with a constant time-to-first-response. This advantage holds on BIG-Bench Audio and amplifies significantly during complex full-duplex interruptions. Specifically, when a user interjects while the model is actively speaking and reasoning, Rubato -Full achieves 40.0% to 45.1% accuracy, whereas the baseline fails completely.

# 1 Introduction 

Voice agents are increasingly popular as speech provides a highly intuitive medium for human-computer interaction. To unlock fluid communication, recent research focuses on Speech Full-Duplex (FDX) models (Chen and Yu, 2025; Lu et al., 2026), which concurrently process incoming and outgoing audio streams in real-time to manage dynamic conversational behaviors like backchanneling and interruptions. To capture the full richness of human interaction, recent works are expanding these architectures by integrating additional streams across two primary directions. First, because natural communication relies heavily on non-verbal signals, researchers have added multimodal streams to process incoming visual cues (Jiang et al., 2026; Lim et al., 2026; Huang et al., 2026) and generate outgoing physical actions (Wang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Second, human conversation requires deep cognitive processing rather than just superficial spoken fluency. Because basic Speech FDX models generally lag behind their text-only counterparts in this core intelligence (Xiang et al., 2025), researchers have also introduced internal thinking streams. This bridges the cognitive gap, empowering the model to perform Chain-of-Thought reasoning (Shih et al., 2026b; Chiang et al., 2026b,a; Wu et al., 2026; Shih et al., 2026a) and leverage external capabilities (Chien et al., 2026; Arora et al., 2026) during live conversations. Modeling multiple streams within a Speech FDX architecture introduces a profound temporal challenge: the model must simultaneously orchestrate continuous real-time streams (e.g., audio and visual signals) alongside an internal thinking stream. pacing. Real-time streams operate at strict periodic intervals and require immediate generation to maintain conversational latency. In contrast, the thinking stream is triggered intermittently and is entirely decoupled from physical time, rendering it inherently asynchronous. To ensure the model processes all streams without violating real-time constraints, prior works (Shih et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026) typically rely on a fixed synchronization strategy. By targeting a specific hardware profile, they enforce a rigid decoding pattern that allocates a fixed frame rate to the thinking stream relative to real-time inputs, guaranteeing the model won’t fail during deployment. We argue this static design is fundamentally limiting. An ideal Speech FDX model must allow each stream to maintain its own independent decoding pace and dynamically adapt to available compute while preserving real-time responsiveness (Fig 1). We motivate this decoupled approach for two key reasons. 1) Inelastic compute scaling: Static decode rates for all streams prevent the model from fully leveraging fast hardware to think faster. 2) Hardware variability: Decoding speeds fluctuate across different GPUs, software frameworks, and dynamic server loads. This widespread variance makes it impractical to retrain the model to match every possible target decoding speed. Ultimately, we seek a model architecture that allows real-time interaction to remain stable while thinking streams can process as rapidly as available compute permits. A concurrent line of research attempts to decouple heavy reasoning from real-time interaction by splitting the architecture into a fast interaction model and a slower background reasoning model (Thinking Machines Lab, 2026; Huang et al., 2026). However, this dual-model paradigm introduces substantial engineering overhead for deployment and severely limits the preservation of paralinguistic audio cues during text-based communication between the two systems. More importantly, this split does not bypass the core temporal challenge. The frontend interaction model must still orchestrate real-time audio alongside the discrete tokens required to communicate with the background model. Consequently, these systems face similar bottlenecks when handling asynchronous streams while adapting to different generation speeds. Therefore, any solution that successfully eliminates these rigid temporal bottlenecks would not only enable single-model reasoning but also remain highly synergistic with dual-model architectures. To this end, we introduce Rubato , a multistream positional encoding that allows multiple streams to maintain independent and adaptive decoding rates (Fig. 1). We apply this to an end-to-end Speech FDX model for spoken reasoning, enabling the system to simultaneously listen, speak, and reason in real time. Prior works (Shih et al., 2026b; Chiang et al., 2026b; Zhang et al., 2026) rely on a single shared position counter, a mechanism that functions properly under their fixed synchronization strategies. Naively porting this shared counter to a system with adaptive stream pacing causes the positional distance between consecutive real-time audio tokens to be arbitrarily distorted by the dynamic volume of intermediate thinking tokens. Rubato 

resolves this temporal distortion by assigning independent clocks to the real-time and thinking streams so that each clock advances exclusively on its designated tokens. By doing so, Rubato enforces a steady temporal 2progression for real-time audio while allowing the thinking stream to decode as rapidly as hardware permits 1.Beyond resolving this architectural bottleneck, our decoupled design significantly simplifies dataset construction and training. Simulating diverse reasoning rates in the training data severely complicates temporal alignment, as the model must gather sufficient audio context before initiating thought and conclude its reasoning before the system speaks the resulting answer. Calculating these precise alignments across varying decoding speeds imposes substantial overhead during corpus generation. Rubato greatly eases this data preparation process by removing the strict dependence on deployment budgets. Instead of generating data for every possible speed, we train the model exclusively on two easily generated temporal boundaries. At the baseline rate, the thinking duration aligns deterministically with the real-time audio frames. At the instantaneous rate, the entire reasoning trace resolves within a single audio frame, simulating infinite compute. Everything between these extremes acts as a zero-shot interpolation. Because Rubato structurally teaches the model that these timelines advance independently, the network seamlessly generalizes to any dynamic reasoning speed during deployment. We evaluate Rubato on five single-turn spoken reasoning tasks from Spoken GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and BIG-Bench Audio (Srivastava et al., 2023; Artificial Analysis, 2024a), alongside complex overlapping scenarios where the model must simultaneously think, speak, and listen. Across all settings, Rubato sustains robust reasoning accuracy and a constant time-to-first-response. Crucially, it allows the system to utilize faster hardware to reduce the final time-to-answer. In summary, our key contributions are: • The Asynchronous-Realtime Coupling Challenge: We identify a fundamental limitation in prior works where asynchronous thinking and real-time audio are tied together on the same timeline. This rigid coupling fails to accommodate dynamic generation speeds. • Novel Decoupled Clock Mechanism: Rubato decouples real-time and thinking streams onto independent timelines, running efficiently on stock fused attention kernels without custom modifications. • Efficient Training via Zero-Shot Interpolation: By structurally separating the stream timelines, Rubato 

drastically simplifies dataset construction. This design allows the model to train exclusively on two extreme temporal boundaries and generalize zero-shot to any dynamic inference speed during deployment. • Robust Empirical Performance: We demonstrate that our framework sustains high reasoning accuracy across dynamic inference speeds on complex spoken reasoning tasks. Crucially, it directly translates faster hardware into significantly reduced user-facing latency.

32.2 MultiStream Full-Duplex Models 

To enable full-duplex interaction, we adopt the four-stream interleaving strategy proposed in SCoT (Arora et al., 2025). This architecture consists of User Audio ( ua ), System Audio ( sa ), User Text ( ut ), and System Text ( st ). By providing time-aligned ASR and TTS text for the user and system, respectively, they improve semantic coherence. During preprocessing, audio tokenization compresses the waveforms into discrete tokens, where the temporal span of a single token defines one frame (e.g., 80 ms). Prior architectures model these streams via concatenation or interleaving. Concatenation (Défossez et al., 2024; Roy et al., 2026) forces a synchronized processing rate, while interleaving (Wang et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025) flattens streams into a single 1D sequence for greater flexibility. Both paradigms face strict real-time constraints, meaning the decoding computation must finish faster than the physical duration of the processed audio frames. Because modern GPUs decode rapidly, the residual compute time within each interleaved chunk can be leveraged to decode internal thinking streams like reasoning or RAG. We adopt the interleaved paradigm due to its flexibility. Because hardware decoding speeds dictate how many reasoning tokens can be generated between consecutive real-time audio frames, we formally define this dynamic capacity as the thinking budget B. Let ua f , sa f , ut f , st f , and tt f,k represent the tokens for their respective streams at frame f . We capture frame-level hardware fluctuations by assigning a dynamic budget 

Bf to frame f , defining the interleaved chunk Cf as: 

Cf = ua f , sa f , ut f , st f , tt f, 1, . . . , tt f, Bf

The model consumes these chunks sequentially as a single flat sequence x = [ C1, . . . , C F ],and we use t to denote the position of a token in the flattened sequence. Since B is a per-frame limit, any reasoning process exceeding the current budget simply overflows into the next frame. Unlike prior works that fix B during training and inference, our framework allows B to depend on inference time capacities. In practice, B depends on model size, codec frame rate, serving techniques, and hardware. To simplify, we evaluate varying B directly and defer detailed estimates to Appendix E.  

> The Deficit of Single-Clock.

Existing methods apply RoPE to this flattened sequence using a single monotonically increasing index. While effective for a fixed B, this design creates a positional distribution shift when inference budgets differ from those observed during training. Dynamically expanding or shrinking the intermediate thinking tokens arbitrarily distorts the positional distance between consecutive real-time tokens like ua f and 

ua f +1 . This single-clock constraint thereby prevents the model from robustly learning the true temporal alignment of the streams.

3.1.2 Rubato-Gated 

A primary drawback of Rubato -Split is that it indiscriminately applies all clock rotations regardless of the query and key token types, potentially injecting noise into the attention mechanism. For example, the attention between two ua tokens should strictly depend on their distance along the real-time clock. Under 

Rubato -Split, a varying number of intermediate thinking tokens causes irrelevant rotations within the thinking clock subspace. To eliminate this noise, we propose Rubato -Gated to apply subspace rotations conditionally. To determine the exact rules, we analyze two query-key scenarios: 1) Same Clock : We naturally apply their shared clock’s rotation. 2) Different Clocks : We apply the key token clock’s rotation, allowing the query to differentiate 5this specific key from other keys sharing that same clock. Consolidating these cases reveals that the target rotation always matches the key token. We can thus simplify the mechanism into a single unified rule: rotate solely based on the key token’s clock. > 3.1.4 Rubato Implementation Training.

For Rubato -Split, the implementation is simple. We apply different clocks to separate rotary subspaces before calling FlashAttention. For Rubato -Gated and Rubato -Full, the rotation depends on the key token’s clock. To utilize FlashAttention without writing custom Triton kernels, we expand the hidden dimension to fold this dependence into the tensors. The query pre-computes all potential clock rotations side-by-side. The key retains only the rotation for its specific clock and zeroes out the rest. Consequently, the dot product natively isolates the matching clock pair.

> Inference.

Because decoding scores a single query without a backward pass, we drop the dimension expansion training trick to maximize speed. Preserving decoding efficiency is crucial because any latency overhead directly degrades the thinking budget B. Conceptually, we maintain C independent KV caches. Each key is rotated by its designated clock and stored exclusively in that cache. During generation, we create C

rotated copies of the incoming query, score each against its corresponding cache, and merge the C partial softmax results using the log-sum-exp values returned by the kernel (Ye et al., 2025). This guarantees exact mathematical equivalence to the training path while preserving strict parity with Single-Clock decoding speeds. Details are available in Appendix C. 

3.2 Training on the two extreme budgets 

The structural design of Rubato inherently allows us to interpolate B, which simplifies the effort of compiling training data. Specifically, we propose training the model on just two boundary conditions: B = 1 and B = ∞.Let T = ( l, s ) represent a thinking event comprising l tokens that begins at frame s. For B = 1 , the event strictly requires l frames to execute. This deterministic duration allows us to properly align the reasoning process with the real-time conversation or insert verbal audio fillers to preserve system responsiveness, similar to how people hold the floor when processing complex questions. For B = ∞, the model interleaves the entire 

l-token thinking trace instantly between frame s and frame s + 1 . This effectively decouples the reasoning process from the wall-clock time and largely reduces the alignment complexities between the audio streams. 64 Experiments 

4.1 Training and Evaluation Data 

We evaluate our approach on GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021) and BIG-Bench Audio (Srivastava et al., 2023; Artificial Analysis, 2024b) for two primary reasons. First, they are commonly used benchmarks. Second, they comprise spoken mathematical and logical tasks where intermediate reasoning is critical for accuracy. Solving these tasks successfully thus provides strong evidence that the model’s real-time and thinking streams are coordinating effectively. Because no suitable full-duplex spoken reasoning corpora exist for training, we synthesize three new datasets via Qwen3 TTS (Hu et al., 2026). The first two, Spoken GSM8K and Spoken BIG-Bench Hard , are single-turn datasets derived from the GSM8K-Aug-NL (Wu et al., 2025) and BIG-Bench Hard (Suzgun et al., 2023) text corpora to include mainstream math and logical reasoning. We rigorously make sure there is no test set leakage. For these single-turn datasets, we generate balanced training examples mapped to the two thinking budget extremes. For B = 1 , the system responds to the user’s question with a spoken filler designed to hold the floor. This filler’s duration exactly matches the frame count required to complete the underlying thinking trace before speaking the final answer. For B = ∞, the system skips the filler and answers immediately since all reasoning computes within a single frame. Our third dataset introduces a highly challenging overlapped scenario by randomly merging two Spoken GSM8K QA pairs into one conversation. This setup demands that the model actively reason and speak its answer to the first question while simultaneously listening to the user interject with a second question. Further dataset details are provided in Appendix A.  

> Metrics.

Spoken answers are transcribed and then judged by Deepseek V4 Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026), which reads the reference answer and decides whether the model committed to the correct value. For the overlapped benchmark, the judge scores each question separately. In addition to accuracy, we report two latencies, both measured from the moment the user stops speaking. Time to first response (TTFR) is the onset of any system speech, and time to answer (TTA) is the onset of the spoken final answer. The two come apart exactly when the model speaks a filler: TTFR is when it starts saying something, and TTA is when it commits.  

> Inference Thinking Budget Sweep.

To assess interpolation robustness, we evaluate multiple fixed and random thinking budgets between the 1 and ∞ training extremes (marked by bold ticks in all figures). We test six fixed budgets B ∈ { 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 }, the B = ∞ upper bound, and three random configurations. In these random settings, the per-frame budget Bf is sampled uniformly from the intervals [1 , 2] , [2 , 3] , or [3 , 4] and plotted at the expected mean. Results for highly erratic random budgets like [1 , 16] are deferred to Appendix D as they rarely occur in real-world deployments. 

4.2 Model Implementation 

All models start from the Gemma-4 E4B-IT (Gemma Team, 2026) text backbone and are trained as one autoregressive model over the flat sequence of Eq. 3. Audio is tokenised with NanoCodec at 12.5 Hz (Casanova et al., 2025), giving 80 ms frames, into 4 codebooks of 4032 entries. Frames with no thinking content predict the <empty-think> token. On inference, after finishing decoding a frame’s real-time tokens, we start decoding the thinking tokens until either Bf is reached or the model samples <empty-think> token. Other training and inference details are in Appendix B.

# 5 Conclusion 

In this paper, we address the fundamental challenge of training end-to-end Speech FDX models that can freely reason and maintain responsiveness while dynamically adapting to variable inference hardware speeds. We propose Rubato , a novel positional encoding framework that explicitly models the temporal relationships within and across multiple data streams. This architectural decoupling fundamentally simplifies the training development pipeline. A model only needs to be trained once using two extreme boundaries, yet it effortlessly deploys across diverse generation speed by enabling zero-shot interpolation to any dynamic real-time decoding rate. Our empirical results on complex spoken reasoning benchmarks demonstrate that our Rubato -Full architecture effectively leverages available compute to accelerate responses without sacrificing accuracy. 9Ultimately, the impact of Rubato extends far beyond Speech FDX. The core mechanism of independent positional clocks provides a potential solution for any architectural scenario requiring the orchestration of multiple asynchronous token streams or dynamic generation rates, paving the way for more resilient and flexible multistream models.

# A Corpus construction and statistics 

Overview of Training Datasets. Table 1 details the statistics of our two primary constructed training datasets: 

Spoken GSM8K and Spoken BIG-Bench Hard . These are derived from the GSM8K-Aug-NL (Wu et al., 2025) and BIG-Bench Hard (Srivastava et al., 2023) corpora. The former includes natural language math questions that require multiple steps of arithmetic reasoning. The latter comprises logical reasoning tasks spanning four categories: formal_fallacies , navigate , object_counting , and web_of_lies . We show examples of each task in Table 2. In addition to these single-turn datasets, we construct Overlapped Spoken GSM8K , consisting of 161,198 conversations (2,184 hours). We generate this dataset by randomly drawing and overlapping two QA pairs from the original Spoken GSM8K corpus. 

Construction Details for Single-Turn Datasets. We first employ DeepSeek-V4-Pro (DeepSeek-AI, 2026) to perform text normalization on the questions and answers from the source datasets. Rather than using the originally provided reasoning traces, we prompt Gemma-4 E4B-IT (Gemma Team, 2026) with the same questions and record its generated outputs. We adopt this approach because training on Gemma’s native reasoning traces preserves the model’s reasoning capabilities significantly better than relying on out-of-domain traces from the source text. We validate these generated traces to ensure logical correctness. Next, we synthesize the user questions and system answers into audio using Qwen3 TTS (Hu et al., 2026). To guarantee the accuracy of this synthesis, we run Parakeet-TDT-0.6b-v3 (Sekoyan et al., 2025) on the generated speech. Finally, when concatenating the audio segments, we insert a natural conversational gap between the end of the user’s question and the moment the system takes the floor. 

Construction Details for the Overlapped Corpus. We build Overlapped Spoken GSM8K using examples from the previously constructed Spoken GSM8K . Each training instance consists of two randomly selected QA pairs, denoted as ( Q1, R 1, A 1) and ( Q2, R 2, A 2) corresponding to the questions, reasoning traces, and final answers, alongside any optional spoken fillers ( F1, F 2). To rigorously test the model’s capacity for simultaneous speaking, listening, and reasoning, we restrict the first QA pair to the B = 1 configuration. This ensures the system actively outputs the filler F1 while processing the reasoning trace R1. We then intentionally overlap the audio of Q2 so that the user’s second question begins during this exact filler segment. This design forces the model to comprehend incoming audio ( Q2) while concurrently speaking ( F1) and thinking ( R1). # C Multi-clock KV caching at decode 

In this section, we benchmark the decoding speed of our multi-KV cache implementation for Rubato -Full and Rubato -Gated. We evaluate only Rubato -Full because both variants share an identical attention shape. We report the GPU wall-clock time across various KV cache sizes. As shown in Table 3, the naive wide cache implementation begins to deviate from Single-Clock performance as the KV cache length grows. More importantly, this wide cache approach forces the head dimension to multiply by C as the number of clocks increases, severely degrading efficiency. Conversely, our optimized multi-KV implementation maintains speeds strictly on par with the Single-Clock baseline. These results demonstrate that the multi-KV approach successfully eliminates the decoding speed penalty, allowing us to deploy Rubato -Full and Rubato -Gated without sacrificing hardware efficiency.

# E Decoding speed of common GPUs 

In Section 2.2, we define the thinking budget B as the number of thinking tokens the model can decode within the duration of a single codec frame. In practice, this value depends on several system parameters: the model backbone size, the codec frame rate, the inference framework, and the underlying hardware. In the main text, to focus purely on model design and empirical evaluation, we abstract these factors into a single budget parameter B and evaluate our models directly across assigned values. In this section, we provide estimated values of B across common GPUs to give readers a concrete sense of what realistic deployment budgets look like. Because hardware setups vary widely in real-world deployments, we report estimated values of B derived from hardware datasheets (Table 5). For each GPU, we calculate a theoretical upper-bound estimate alongside a practical estimate assuming 75% memory bandwidth utilization. We also include numbers for smaller models (e.g., LLaMA 3.2 1B) to contextualize how B varies with model scale. As shown in the table, sweeping B from 1 to 32 in our experimental setup covers the decoding throughput of most current GPU configurations.

# F The mixing ratio of the two training layouts 

As described in Sec. 3.2, we build every conversation using either a B = 1 or B = ∞ budget in roughly equal proportions. This ratio is an adjustable parameter with asymmetric computational costs. Preparing B = ∞

rows is nearly cost-free, while B = 1 rows require synthesizing audio fillers. Consequently, the most economical corpus minimizes B = 1 data. Fig. 5 evaluates how aggressively we can reduce this data without degrading interpolation performance.  

> Rubato-Full remains robust across altered training proportions compared to Rubato-Gated.

Across all five dataset configurations, Rubato -Full accuracy at B = 1 spans a tight 41.6% to 53.7%, while Rubato -Gated fluctuates massively between 20.5% and 52.7%. This exact trend persists across all interpolated rates. The 

Rubato -Gated model trained at the 0.4% ratio highlights this instability perfectly. Its time-to-answer flatlines at 1.6 s across all budgets, proving it abandons the reasoning trace prematurely to force an answer.  

> Accuracy does not decay monotonically as the

B = 1 data ratio shrinks. For Rubato -Full, training with a 4.4% share yields performance comparable to a 43.9% share (53.3% to 56.4% versus 52.5% to 57.0%). Paradoxically, the intermediate 21.9% and 8.8% ratios perform worse and rapidly decay above B = 4 . Overall, these results indicate that a roughly equal mixture is the safest empirical choice.

# H Limitations 

Synthetic audio in experimental setups. Following the precedent of prior spoken reasoning literature, our experiments utilize synthetic audio. This choice is necessitated by the absence of publicly available full-duplex human conversation datasets featuring time-aligned reasoning traces. While synthetic audio differs from real-world acoustics, the core contribution of Rubato centers strictly on the temporal alignment mechanism between independent streams. Because this decoupled clock architecture operates entirely independently of the underlying acoustic features, the structural benefits demonstrated in our evaluation should generalize to real audio recordings. 

Scope of reasoning tasks and stream count. The Rubato framework is theoretically designed to orchestrate 19 an arbitrary number of interacting modalities and thinking processes. In this work, we scope our empirical validation specifically to a spoken reasoning setup comprising four real-time streams alongside a single chain-of-thought thinking stream. While this configuration shows the efficacy of our temporal decoupling, expanding this validation to include tool use tokens or massively multi-stream environments remains an area for future work pending the availability of suitable datasets.  

> Architectural and codec uniformity.

All empirical results rely on a Gemma-4 E4B-IT backbone paired with NanoCodec operating at 12.5 Hz using a two-clock configuration. We intentionally fix these architectural variables across all evaluations to maintain a strictly controlled environment. This uniformity successfully isolates the impact of the temporal framework, providing an unambiguous comparison between the statically synchronized baselines and our proposed Rubato variants. 20
