## MultiPartyTurnBench : Benchmarking Floor Control in Multi-Party Spoken Dialogue 

Puneet Mathur, Dinesh Manocha 

> University of Maryland College Park, USA

Project Page: https://multiparty-turnbench.github.io/ 

Abstract 

Full-duplex speech systems must decide when to enter, hold, or relinquish the conversational floor, even as multiple speakers overlap or backchannel without taking it. Existing eval-uations study dyadic turn-taking or end-to-end agent behavior, but lack a unified, con-trolled evaluation of genuine floor transitions against confusable non-events in multi-party settings. We introduce MultiPartyTurnBench 

(MPTB), a benchmark of 240 synthetically gen-erated, channel-separated conversations span-ning two to five speakers and six interaction types. MPTB evaluates two complementary tasks: End-of-Turn (EOT) detection distin-guishes genuine turn completion from tem-porary holds, while Interruption (INT) detec-tion distinguishes successful floor takeovers from non-floor-taking speech. Exact event-conditioned annotations enable causal eval-uation using false-positive-constrained recall and signed latency. Across acoustic, seman-tic, and turn-projection baselines, we find a stark asymmetry: learned systems achieve 82.5–92.6% EOT recall at 4.3–11.8% false-positive rates, while INT detection suffers from missed takeovers or excessive false activations. This asymmetry extends to five full-duplex agents which achieve 86.1–95.8% balanced ac-curacy on turn entry, yet perform near chance (46.9–54.7%) on selective interruption han-dling. Gemini-3.8-Live yields to 97.7% of backchannels, whereas MiniCPM-o-4.5 yields to only 1.4% of genuine interruptions. These findings establish selective floor control as a distinct, unresolved challenge for reliable multi-party voice interaction. 

1 Introduction 

Full-duplex speech systems must continuously de-cide when to enter, hold, or relinquish the conversa-tional floor while processing ongoing speech (Ek-stedt and Skantze, 2022; Peng et al., 2025). These decisions require more than detecting speech ac-tivity: a speaker may pause without yielding the floor, while a listener may backchannel without attempting to take it (Sacks et al., 1974; Yngve, 1970). Misinterpreting either can cause an agent to respond prematurely or unnecessarily abandon its own response, even when its language understand-ing is otherwise correct (Xia et al., 2026). This distinction becomes particularly important in multi-party conversation, where several listeners may backchannel, overlap, or interject while the current speaker retains the floor. Existing evalua-tions address complementary aspects of this prob-lem: dyadic benchmarks distinguish turn endings and interruptions in 2-party settings (Jiang et al., 2026; Xia et al., 2026), natural multi-party cor-pora study next-speaker prediction (Triadic-VAP, 2025; Qi and Skantze, 2026), and full-duplex agent benchmarks assess speaking and stopping behavior (Lin et al., 2025, 2026c; Ma et al., 2026). However, the joint evaluation of genuine floor relinquishment and successful floor acquisition under controlled multi-party conditions remains underexplored. We introduce MultiPartyTurnBench (Fig. 1), a benchmark for evaluating floor control in multi-party full-duplex speech. MPTB evaluates two complementary decisions: End-of-Turn (EOT) de-tection distinguishes genuine turn completion from temporary holds, while Interruption (INT) detec-tion distinguishes successful floor takeovers from backchannels and other non-floor-taking speech. The benchmark comprises 240 synthetically gen-erated, channel-separated conversations balanced across N ∈ { 2, 3, 4, 5} speakers and six interac-tion types. A discrete-event orchestrator establishes floor ownership and event timing before speech synthesis, enabling controlled variation in speaker count, interaction dynamics, and acoustic condi-tions. Main Results. Our evaluation reveals a pro-nounced asymmetry between turn completion and successful floor acquisition. At qualified operating points, systems recover up to 92.6% of turn end-ings but only 56.2% of successful interruptions; acoustic detectors achieve higher recall only at excessive false-positive rates. Increasing speaker count produces jagged degradation, while conver-sational style exposes substantial model-specific interruption failures. Crucially, this asymmetry extends to observable voice agent behavior: four of five full-duplex agents achieve 86.1–95.8% bal-anced accuracy on turn entry, yet all five remain near chance (46.9–54.7%) on selective yielding. Gemini-3.8-Live (Google, 2026) yields almost in-discriminately, whereas MiniCPM-o-4.5 (Cui et al., 2026) rarely yields even to genuine interruptions, revealing opposite failures concealed by aggregate accuracy. Complementary real-speech evaluation further shows that interruption detection remains challenging in natural multi-party meetings. These findings establish selective interruption handling as a persistent limitation of multi-party full-duplex interaction. Our main contributions are: 

• MultiPartyTurnBench , a controlled benchmark of 240 multi-party conversations spanning two to five speakers and six interaction types, with ex-act event-state annotations for end-of-turn (EOT) and interruption (INT) detection. • A systematic evaluation of floor-transition detection across acoustic, semantic, and turn-projection systems, revealing an EOT–INT ca-pability gap and model-specific sensitivities to speaker count and conversational style. • End-to-end validation across five full-duplex voice agents , exposing near-chance selective yielding despite strong turn-entry performance, with opposing tendencies to yield indiscrimi-nately or ignore genuine interruptions.

3 MultiPartyTurnBench 

MultiPartyTurnBench evaluates whether systems can causally infer changes in conversational floor ownership from ongoing multi-speaker audio. It de-fines two complementary, event-conditioned tasks: 

(i) End-of-Turn (EOT) asks whether the current speaker has relinquished the floor and aims to de-tect genuine floor relinquishment by the current speaker, anchored at speech offset against hard neg-atives such as mid-turn pauses where the speaker retains the floor. (ii) Interruption (INT) asks whether another speaker has successfully acquired the floor and detects successful floor acquisition by another speaker, anchored at the interrupting speech onset, against hard negatives like backchan-nels and non-content vocalizations that leave floor ownership unchanged. Unsuccessful interruption attempts are excluded because their outcomes may only become apparent through subsequent interac-tion, making them ambiguous at onset for a causal detector (Jiang et al., 2026). Together, the two tracks evaluate floor relinquishment and acquisi-tion against behaviorally confusable non-events, rather than speech activity alone (Table 2). 

3.1 Benchmark Construction 

MPTB is designed to isolate floor-control decisions under independently controlled interaction condi-tions with exact event boundaries. In natural multi-party recordings, speaker count, overlap, listener activity, and acoustics vary jointly, complicating controlled analysis. Controlled Interaction Axes. MPTB crosses speaker count ( N ∈ { 2, 3, 4, 5}) with six inter-action types derived from TurnBench (Jiang et al., 2026): Casual, Task-Oriented, Instructional, Col-laborative, Argumentative, and Narrative. Gen-eration additionally controls backchannel and in-terruption occurrences, while acoustic rendering varies ambient noise and nuisance events. Conversation Generation Pipeline Figure 2 il-lustrates the five-stage pipeline for multi-speaker acoustic realization annotated with conversational floor dynamics. The first two stages establish the canonical event log and interaction timeline before speech synthesis, ensuring that ground-truth labels derive from the underlying floor state rather than retrospective waveform analysis. 

1. Event-conditioned dialogue generation. Off-the-shelf LLM like GPT-4.1 generates multi-party scripts conditioned on speaker count, in-teraction type, topic, narrative plot, and target turn-taking statistics. A restricted event vocab-ulary ( TURN , CLAIM_FLOOR , VOCALIZE_NO_CLAIM )specifies conversational actions, with backchan-nel and interruption frequencies guided by Turn-Bench (Jiang et al., 2026). The LLM determines linguistic content and proposes listener actions but does not assign final timestamps or resolve floor ownership. Scripts are schema-validated, with mal-formed outputs regenerated or rejected. 

2. Floor-state orchestration. A discrete-event orchestrator maintains the current floor holder and resolves symbolic actions into real-ized transitions: TURN becomes a hold, yield, or interruption-forced termination; CLAIM_FLOOR 

resolves either to a successful I NTERRUPTION 

or a N ON -F LOOR -T AKING INTERRUPTION ; and 

VOCALIZE_NO_CLAIM produces a backchannel or related non-floor-changing event. The orchestrator assigns turn durations, inter-speaker gaps, pauses, and interruption overlap based on real-world timing statistics from AMI corpus (Carletta et al., 2006). Explicit mid-turn pauses provide EOT hard nega-tives. The resulting placement schedule and canon-ical event log define exact EOT and INT anchors. 

3. Speaker rendering. Utterances are indepen-dently synthesized using Kokoro-82M(hexgrad, 2024), with distinct voices sampled from a gender-balanced pool of 24 voices, preventing voice reuse within a conversation. Development and test splits use disjoint voice inventories (8 and 16 voices, re-spectively). Word-level timing is retained for align-ment and quality control. 

4. Acoustic scene rendering. Synthesized speech is placed on synchronized speaker channels accord-ing to the orchestrated timeline, preserving over-lap determined by floor-state transitions. Success-ful interruptions end with a short fade applied at the truncation boundary, and channels are peak-normalized independently. A shared voice-free ambient bed (filtered pink noise and humming) is mixed at a per-conversation SNR. MUSAN (Sny-der et al., 2015) sound effects and intermittent dis-tant third-party speech introduces nuisance activity to stress false-positive detection; neither creates benchmark-positive floor events. 

5. Channel assembly and release artifacts. Each conversation is released with its noisy evaluation channels, clean reference channels, tagged script, canonical event log, render metadata, and genera-tion seed. Clean channels are retained for quality control, while benchmark systems operate on the noisy rendition. All non-generative stages are de-terministic with recorded seeds and manifests, en-abling exact reproduction of the interaction sched-ule and acoustic assembly (see Appendix C.3).

4 Evaluation 

Figure 3 illustrates the causal evaluation protocol: systems emit timestamped EOT and INT decisions using only audio observed up to prediction time. We evaluate event recall, false-positive rate, and signed decision latency at fixed operating points. 

Prediction and Event Matching. Per-speaker model scores are converted to discrete events through threshold crossings with a 2 s refractory period. Following TurnBench (Jiang et al., 2026), each gold positive at ti is matched one-to-one to the earliest unmatched prediction within [ti −0.25 , t i +3.0] s. Matched events are true positives (TP), while unmatched gold positives are false negatives (FN). Hard negatives are scored as temporal spans: any detection within a span counts as a false pos-itive (FP), otherwise a true negative (TN). Predic-tions outside scored regions are ignored. 

Operating Points and Metrics. For each model, we select the development threshold maximizing recall subject to FPR dev ≤ 0.10 (Jiang et al., 2026), then freeze it across all test conversations, speaker counts, and interaction types. Systems exceeding FPR test > 0.15 are marked out-of-budget. We report Recall = TP /(TP + FN) and

High recall without selectivity is insufficient for conversational control. Acoustic detectors illus-trate why floor transitions cannot be reduced to speech activity. Silero VAD achieves 97.2% EOT and 85.4% INT recall, but incurs prohibitive FPRs of 93.5% and 89.2%. This exposes the central challenge: distinguishing genuine floor claims from listener activity that leaves floor ownership unchanged. 

Detection quality and latency trade-offs. Even among strong EOT detectors, comparable recall conceals substantial timing differences. VAP de-tects turn endings at 143 ms p50, versus 1,829 ms for Kyutai SVAD, despite nearly identical recall. Thus, a correct floor transition detected too late may still disrupt natural interaction. 

RQ2: Does Speaker Count Make Floor Control Harder? 

More speakers do not produce uniform degrada-tion in EOT detection. Figure 4 shows that EOT detection remains comparatively stable across two to five speakers. VAP maintains 92–93% recall, while MuVAP and DualTurn show similarly small variations. Surprisingly, Kyutai improves from 84.6% to 96.6%, suggesting that increasing speaker count does not inherently make turn completion harder under MPTB’s controlled conditions. RQ3: Which Interaction Types Expose Failures? 

Interruption detection is substantially more sen-sitive to interaction style. Figure 5 (and Ap-pendix F.2) shows that EOT detection remains com-paratively stable across interaction types with lim-ited variation. In contrast, INT recall shows large swings under the same threshold (Kyutai SVAD is 47.0% in collaborative dialogue to 77.5% in in-structional exchanges). This disparity suggests that EOT detection generalizes more consistently across interaction regimes compared to INT detection. 

Conversational difficulty is model-dependent. 

Kyutai struggles most with collaborative ex-changes, while MuVAP performs worst on narra-tive dialogue (25.0% INT recall), DualTurn on task-oriented interactions (32.6%), and VAP and Mimi Endpointer on argumentative exchanges. These contrasting failures suggest that models rely on dif-ferent conversational cues when interpreting floor claims, rather than a shared hierarchy of interaction difficulty. See Appendix I for qualitative examples.

7 Discussion 

Multi-party floor control requires reasoning about speaker roles, not merely speech activ-ity. MPTB exposes a fundamental limitation of conventional endpointing: incoming speech does not necessarily imply a change in floor ownership. Reliable interruption handling in multi-speaker settings requires distinguishing competing floor claims from backchannels and other non-floor-taking activity, conditioned on the current speaker and conversational context. This motivates event-conditioned modeling of floor transitions rather than independent speech-activity decisions. 

Floor-control evaluation must capture the conse-quences of incorrect decisions. High event recall can conceal excessive false activations, while con-servative yielding policies can appear reliable de-spite missing genuine interruptions. MPTB demon-strates the importance of evaluation breakdowns across speaker counts and interaction regimes. Such diagnostics expose failure modes obscured by aggregate scores and provide actionable targets for developing more selective full-duplex systems. 

Synthetic to real evaluation gap. Table 24 illus-trates that this difficulty is not confined to synthetic speech: VAP recovers only 35.9% of interruptions on real four-party AMI meetings at 5.3% FPR, de-spite in-slice threshold tuning. This supports cor-roborating learnings from synthetic evaluations to real-world relevance of the interruption challenge. 

8 Conclusion 

We introduced MultiPartyTurnBench , a con-trolled benchmark for turn taking dynamics for end-of-turn and interruption detection in multi-party spoken dialogues. Existing systems detect turn completion reliably but struggle to recog-nize floor takeovers without excessive false ac-tivations, with failures varying across conversa-tional regimes and speaker counts. This asym-metry extends to full-duplex agents where all sys-tems perform near chance on selective yielding. 

MultiPartyTurnBench establishes selective inter-ruption handling as a critical unresolved challenge for reliable multi-party voice interaction. 9 Limitations 

Synthetic speech and external validity. MPTB’s controlled construction provides exact floor-state annotations and reproducible interaction condi-tions, but synthesized speech cannot fully capture the prosody, disfluencies, timing adaptations, and ambiguous floor negotiations of spontaneous con-versation. The synthetic-to-real comparison (Ta-ble 24) reveals non-uniform difficulty: learned endpointers generally achieve higher EOT recall on MPTB, whereas VAP detects fewer interrup-tions on MPTB (11.7%) than on real four-party AMI (35.9%). Thus, synthetic evaluation does not uniformly overestimate performance, and MPTB scores should be interpreted as controlled diagnos-tics rather than estimates of natural-conversation error rates. 

Scope of real-speech validation. Our multi-party validation uses 15 four-speaker AMI meetings with floor events derived from manual speech seg-ments rather than independently annotated under the MPTB taxonomy. The evaluated corpora also differ in speaker count, conversational domain, acoustics, annotation protocols, and threshold cal-ibration, with real-audio slices using in-slice tun-ing. Cross-corpus differences therefore cannot be attributed causally to speaker count or synthetic speech. AMI provides complementary evidence that interruption detection remains challenging in natural multi-party conversation, but does not estab-lish direct synthetic-to-real performance transfer. 

Linguistic and demographic coverage. MPTB is restricted to English, with accent diversity limited to the voices sampled during synthesis rather than representative real-world speaker populations. We do not systematically evaluate generalization across languages, regional accents, or speaker demograph-ics. Turn-taking conventions, prosodic cues, and in-terruption behaviors may vary across linguistic and sociocultural contexts. Consequently, our findings should not be interpreted as universal patterns of conversational floor control; broader multilingual and demographically diverse real-speech evalua-tion is necessary to establish their generality. 

Ethics Statement 

The released MPTB corpus contains synthetic speech and fictional LLM-generated dialogue rather than newly collected private conversations. The benchmark is designed to diagnose conversa-tional control failures and should not be interpreted as a safety certification for deployment. Real Turn-Bench material used for scorer validation is not redistributed, and AMI is used under its existing research terms. Because full-duplex systems may be used in multi-user or high-stakes settings, bench-mark performance should be considered alongside broader evaluations of recognition quality, safety, fairness, privacy, and task correctness.

A.1 Human Annotation 

Although MPTB derives floor-state labels before speech synthesis, a procedurally valid event may not be perceived as intended in the rendered audio. We therefore conduct a human validation study assessing whether listeners recognize the intended event categories and their temporal realization. The study covers all 240 conversations and 6,116 can-didate events, with three independent annotations per event (18,348 primary categorical judgments, excluding blind repeats). 

Annotators and qualification. Annotators have native or near-native English comprehension and complete written guidelines, worked audio exam-ples, and a held-out calibration set emphasizing turn completion versus continuation, backchannels versus floor claims, and successful versus unsuc-cessful interruptions. Qualification requires at least 75% agreement with expert labels across 10 calibra-tion examples. Annotators are blinded to generator labels, event logs, and model predictions. 

Annotation procedure. Annotators inspect can-didate regions with 10 s of preceding and follow-ing context using synchronized multi-speaker au-dio, independently controllable speaker channels, and a shared timeline. For each region, they as-sign an event category, identify the perceived floor holder before and after the event, and mark onset and offset timestamps. An AMBIGUOUS label is permitted when the floor outcome cannot be deter-mined reliably. Automatic transcripts are available as references, but audio takes precedence. Table 5 summarizes the annotation fields. Annotations are collected independently without discussion or adjudication. Examples are presented in randomized order across interaction conditions, speaker counts, and voices. A subset of 25% of ex-amples is repeated blindly to assess intra-annotator consistency. Since listeners receive subsequent con-versational context, this study validates the percep-tual realization of floor transitions rather than their causal predictability at event onset.

A.3 Human Validation Results Floor-event labels are perceptually distinguish-able, but not equally unambiguous. Table 6 reports agreement across the six generated event categories. Backchannels show the strongest con-sistency, with 93.6% categorical consensus, Fleiss’

Turn endings and mid-turn holds also exhibit lower categorical consensus than backchannels, supporting the distinction between acoustic speech cessation and perceived floor relin-quishment. 

Generator–human agreement supports la-bel plausibility, with important qualifica-tions. Among events receiving human consensus, generator–human agreement ranges from 86.5% for turn endings to 97.8% for backchannels. Suc-cessful interruptions achieve 91.2% generator– human agreement despite having the lowest cat-egorical consensus (81.4%). These measures cap-ture different properties: consensus reflects consis-tency across annotators, whereas generator–human agreement evaluates label correspondence only on retained events. High conditional agreement there-fore supports the plausibility of recognized syn-thetic events but does not establish that every gen-erated event is perceptually unambiguous. 

Human agreement decreases with speaker count. 

Table 7 shows a consistent decline across all three agreement measures as the number of speakers in-creases. Categorical consensus falls from 87.8% at N = 2 to 80.1% at N = 5 , while Fleiss’ κ de-creases from 0.84 to 0.76 and boundary F1 from 0.96 to 0.90. The simultaneous reduction in cate-gorical and temporal agreement suggests that re-solving floor transitions becomes less consistent across annotators in larger groups. This is a de-scriptive association rather than an isolated causal effect of speaker count, since realized event com-position and acoustic conditions may also differ. 

Interpretation and scope. The human study pro-vides complementary evidence that MPTB’s gener-ated floor-state categories have recognizable acous-tic and conversational realizations, while identify-ing residual ambiguity in floor transitions, partic-ularly successful interruptions. Since candidate regions are selected from the generator event log and annotators may inspect future context, these findings validate event-label plausibility rather than independent event discovery or real-time detectabil-ity. The study also does not establish that synthetic speech captures the full variability of natural multi-party interaction.

C.1 Role-relative event taxonomy 

Every event is defined relative to the current floor holder. Table 13 gives the operational meaning of each class, its scoring role, and its realized fre-quency in the released corpus. C.2 Interaction-Type Definitions 

MPTB adopts the six conversation types used in TurnBench (Jiang et al., 2026), which were de-signed to induce distinct patterns of turn-taking behavior. The categories are retained to provide a common interaction-style axis with prior work, while MPTB uses them within a balanced multi-party factorial design over N ∈ { 2, 3, 4, 5}. Ta-ble 14 summarizes the intended interaction charac-teristics used to condition synthetic dialogue gen-eration. These descriptions specify the dominant conversational regime rather than enforcing deter-ministic event patterns; realized backchannel, in-terruption, overlap, and turn-duration statistics are reported separately in Appendix D.1. C.3 Generation pipeline 

Generation proceeds in five stages. (1) An LLM writes a role-tagged dialogue conditioned on party count, interaction type, and target rates for backchannels and interruptions. (2) A deter-ministic state machine resolves floor claims, holds, yields, and overlaps into a timed event graph. (3) Kokoro-82M renders each speaker using a disjoint voice pool. (4) A controlled noise stage adds proce-dural ambient sound, discrete non-content events, and intermittent distant speech. (5) The assembler emits synchronized per-speaker audio and the ex-act event log. Scripts, seeds, audio manifests, and scorer inputs are recorded for reproducibility. We construct 120 interactive scenarios spanning four event conditions: true end-of-turn, mid-turn hold pause, successful in-terruption, and backchannel (30 each). Scenar-ios are balanced across N ∈ { 2, 3, 4} and the six interaction types. Each scenario is evaluated three times on five full-duplex systems: GPT-Live-1 (OpenAI, 2026), Gemini-3.8-Live (Google, 2026), MiniCPM-o-4.5 (Cui et al., 2026), Realtime-Venus (Zhao et al., 2026a), and Step-Audio-3-Realtime (Lin et al., 2026a), yielding 1,800 at-tempted trials. Agents receive streaming speech without access to scenario labels or reference event times.

F.8 Error Anatomy 

To localize errors, we join every scored test anchor with its script utterance and score each attribute slice separately (Table 32). 

End-of-turn scores detect sustained listener speech, not floor claims. Kyutai’s INT score fires on 0.2% of one-word listener vocalizations, 5.7% of two- or three-word ones, and 72.3% of negatives with four or more words, all of which are laughter vocalizations such as “ Haha, yeah, I’ve been waiting all week for this one ”. Its recall on true interruptions also grows with claim length (37.2% for 1–3 words vs. 62.6% for 9+ words). Its INT advantage over VAP (Table 29) therefore comes largely from duration, not from modelling floor ownership. DualTurn and MuVAP show the same direction with flatter slopes (26.2% FPR on 4+-word negatives), and all learned systems false-alarm more on laughter than on backchannels ex-cept ESPnet. 

Hold pauses after complete questions look like yields. G.1 Per-family counts and accuracy 

Table 34 breaks accuracy down by party count and reports run-to-run consistency (the fraction of sce-narios on which all valid repeats agree). The STOP failures are stable properties of each agent, not noise: MiniCPM-o ignores 73 of 74 valid interrup-tions with perfect consistency, and Gemini yields to 86 of 88 backchannels. GPT-Live-1 is the least deterministic agent on STOP (consistency .40 on in-terruptions and .60 on backchannels), and its yield rate grows with N (35% at N =2 to 65% at N =4 )together with its backchannel accuracy (48% to 75%). 

G.2 Latency 

Table 35 reports START latency as the agent’s speech onset relative to the end of the genuine turn, and STOP latency as the time from injection of the interrupting speech to the end of the agent’s ongo-ing utterance, over all valid trials. Gemini stops 230 ms (median) after an interruption begins, in line with provider-side cancellation on detected in-put speech (Table 36). MiniCPM-o (median 8.6 s) and Venus (3.0 s) finish their ongoing answer be-fore going silent, which is why their interruption accuracy is near zero under the 1.5 s stop window.

Table 36 separates provider-driven cancellation from model-driven yielding. Every Gemini stop on a true interruption, and 95.5% of its stops on backchannels, coincide with a provider barge-in event; its STOP behavior is therefore the provider’s voice-activity cancellation policy, which cannot dis-tinguish a backchannel from a takeover. The other agents expose no cancel events, so their stops are model decisions. H Offline–Online Association 

For four models with both offline and online mea-surements, model-level rank association is weak (Spearman ρ = −0.20 for EOT →START ; ρ =0.316 for INT →STOP ). Event-level STOP asso-ciation is stronger: behavioral correctness is .584 when the offline detector is correct versus .422 when it is wrong, OR 1.91 [1.34, 2.73], n = 506 .START shows no such association (.800 vs. .820; OR .89 [.53,1.50], n = 720 ). We do not interpret the pooled association because START and STOP have different base rates. 

Offline detector pass. The offline measurements come from a separate pass on the same 120 scenar-ios in which each agent acts as a detector: for EOT it hears the isolated channel of the speaker whose turn ends, for INT an agent-free mix, both with a detector instruction, and its response onset is read out as the commit time and scored with the MPTB window. Table 37 gives the event-level contingency counts behind the odds ratios.
