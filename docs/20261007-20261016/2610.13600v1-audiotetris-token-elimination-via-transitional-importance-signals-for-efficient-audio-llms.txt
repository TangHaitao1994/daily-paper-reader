## ABSTRACT 

Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) achieve strong performance on speech-text tasks, but standard audio encoders produce hundreds of tokens for even short audio clips. Because self-attention scales quadratically in sequence length, this in-flates inference-time compute substantially. Current training-free token compres-sion methods prune or merge tokens based solely on coarse attention or feature similarity. However, treating audio tokens as generic, isotropic features smooths over spectral transitions, destroying fine-grained phonetic transitions essential for decoding speech. In contrast, speech signals exhibit highly structured temporal patterns where information density peaks at acoustic transitions. We propose Au-dioTETRIS , a training-free, two-stage framework that uses the audio encoder’s representation geometry as an acoustic prior for token importance before applying attention-based compression. Instead of discarding encoder structure after the en-coding stage, AudioTETRIS propagates this prior directly into the LLM decoder to compute a per-token fused score. This score drives progressive top-k token selection across decoder layers without requiring additional training or auxiliary models. Evaluated on Qwen2-Audio-7B, Phi-4-Multimodal, and Audio Flamingo 3, AudioTETRIS retains near-uncompressed performance on several speech tasks using only 25% of audio tokens, matches or surpasses full-token accuracy at mod-erate compression rates, and consistently outperforms existing training-free base-lines. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Large Audio Language Models (LALMs) have achieved state-of-the-art results across several speech-text tasks like speech recognition, audio QA, and general audio understanding (Chu et al., 2024; Radford et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025a; Yang et al., 2024). Typically, these models use a pretrained audio encoder to map the input waveform into a sequence of continuous frame-level embeddings (audio tokens), which are projected into the Large Language Model (LLM) embedding space and prepended to text prompts. However, this approach creates a severe compu-tational bottleneck: standard speech encoders generate hundreds of tokens for just a few seconds of audio (Lin et al., 2025; Chu et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2025a). Because decoder self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length, this large token footprint drives up inference costs (Tay et al., 2022). Furthermore, uniform audio tokenization is inherently redundant (Radford et al., 2023; Chu et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025b; Zhang et al., 2026). It clashes with the nature of speech, where information is concentrated during rapid phonetic transitions rather than steady-state regions (Furui, 1986a; Stevens, 2002; Aylett & Turk, 2004; Zhang et al., 2025a). While recent methods apply token pruning or merging (Lin et al., 2025; Bhati et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2026; Behera et al., 2024), they share a substantial flaw: they rely solely on isolated decoder attention or local feature similarity, ignoring the structural phoneme transitions that could be already captured by the encoder (Behera et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025; Gong et al., 2026; Jung et al., 2026; Bhati et al., 2025). We hypothesize that speech token compression should be guided by the underlying acoustic struc-ture of the signal, rather than post-hoc decoder attention alone. Our approach is based on a simple insight: regions of rapid spectral transition in the encoder’s hidden states coincide with phonetic transitions, where essential acoustic details reside (Furui, 1986a; Stevens, 2002). We empirically validate this hypothesis in Section 4, showing a strong correlation between representation variance in encoder space and actual phonetic segment transitions. Compression methods that ignore this structure could risk discarding high-frequency acoustic details and degrading downstream perfor-mance (Zhang et al., 2026; Libera et al., 2026). To solve this, we introduce AudioTETRIS (Figure 1), a training-free framework that compresses au-dio tokens by jointly exploiting speech-specific acoustic structures and decoder-side attention. First, AudioTETRIS derives a phoneme-transition-aware prior from the temporal derivative of hidden states, isolating regions of high spectral change to protect information-dense transition segments. Second, this prior is propagated into the LLM decoder and fused with a depth-adaptive attention score derived from intra-audio attention in early layers and cross-modal text-to-audio attention in middle layers. By coupling acoustic salience with task-conditioned attention, token survival is driven by both structural and semantic relevance. We evaluate AudioTETRIS across backbones like Qwen2-Audio-7B, Phi-4-Multimodal, and Au-dio Flamingo 3 (Chu et al., 2024; Microsoft et al., 2025; Ghosh et al., 2025a) on tasks including Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), Speech Emotion Recognition, Audio Question Answering, and Speech Translation. AudioTETRIS retains near-lossless accuracy while keeping as few as 25% of tokens, outperforming other training-free baselines, and even surpassing full-sequence models at moderate compression rates. 2Preprint Our main contributions are: • Acoustic transition prior: We show that temporal variance in encoder hidden states serves as a reliable proxy for phonetic transitions, capturing high-information regions without fine-tuning or external alignments. • AudioTETRIS framework: We propose a training-free compression framework that prop-agates this acoustic prior into the LLM decoder, dynamically guiding self-attention to pre-serve both acoustic and semantic details. • State-of-the-art efficiency: Extensive experiments demonstrate near-lossless performance at aggressive compression levels (25% token retention), outperforming strong training-free baselines across diverse benchmarks. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Large Audio Language Models. LALMs typically couple a pretrained speech encoder, such as Whisper (Radford et al., 2023), with an LLM backbone (Chu et al., 2024; Ghosh et al., 2025b;a; Microsoft et al., 2025; Tang et al., 2024; Deshmukh et al., 2023), projecting audio features into the text embedding space. However, this paradigm yields audio token sequences that are orders of magnitude longer than the accompanying text, creating severe computational overhead during prefill and generation. Reducing this token count without sacrificing the dense lexical content of the underlying signal remains a key challenge (Shao et al., 2026; He et al., 2025). 

Training-Free Token Compression. Training-free compression methods eliminate redundant au-dio tokens without modifying model weights. One direction seeks to prune or merge sequence tokens prior to or during LLM processing (Bolya et al., 2023; Behera et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Jung et al., 2026). Across vision-language models (VLMs), methods like SparseVLM (Zhang et al., 2025b) leverage self-attention logits to select text tokens as ‘raters’ that adaptively prune vi-sual tokens across layers (see also Shang et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025; Xing et al., 2025). In the speech domain, SpeechPrune (Lin et al., 2025) compresses long-form speech ( ≥30s) tokens using approximated attention scores from the LLM’s initial transformer layer alongside speech-text fea-ture similarity. However, relying on early-layer LLM dynamics forces the model to evaluate query relevance before building a comprehensive contextual representation of the signal. Alternatively, encoder-level methods like Local Temporal Bipartite Merging (LTBM) (Luo et al., 2026) merge nearby tokens under a temporal window constraint (Li et al., 2023), relying purely on local feature similarity rather than task-specific or phonetic cues. 

Training-Based & Multimodal Token Compression. Rather than relying on training-free pruning, alternative strategies preserve performance by introducing extra trainable parameters or secondary modalities (Kavaki & Mandel, 2025). For instance, encoder-level pooling methods often require low-rank adapters (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2022) to fine-tune the backbone and recover downstream accuracy (Bhati et al., 2025). Although effective, this training dependency eliminates the mod-ule’s utility as a plug-and-play solution for unseen backbones or domain shifts. Other frameworks, such as EchoingPixels (Gong et al., 2026), achieve substantial token reduction by exploiting cross-modal alignment across visual and audio streams, but do not extend naturally to audio-language tasks. Across both directions, training-free methods bypass acoustic structure while structure-aware methods require fine-tuning or secondary modalities - a trade-off AudioTETRIS resolves with a training-free, acoustically grounded importance signal (§3). 

## 3 METHODOLOGY 

We present AudioTETRIS , a training-free framework for compressing audio tokens in LALMs. AudioTETRIS operates in two stages: Stage 1 (§3.2) extracts a phoneme-transition-aware acoustic prior directly from the audio encoder’s hidden states, and Stage 2 (§3.3) propagates this prior into the LLM decoder, fusing it with a depth-adaptive attention score to progressively prune audio tokens across decoder layers. ## 5 EXPERIMENTS & R ESULTS 

5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Backbones and Datasets. We evaluate AudioTETRIS on three LALM backbones with distinct encoder-decoder architectures: Qwen2-Audio-7B ( ∼8.4B) (Chu et al., 2024), Phi-4-Multimodal (5.6B) (Microsoft et al., 2025), and Audio Flamingo 3 ( ∼8.3B) (Ghosh et al., 2025a). Our eval-uation spans four core tasks: automatic speech recognition on LibriSpeech test-other (Panayotov et al., 2015), emotion recognition on IEMOCAP (Busso et al., 2008), audio question answering on MMAU-mini (Sakshi et al., 2025), and speech translation (English-to-Chinese) on CoVoST 2 (Wang et al., 2021). Together, these benchmarks span lexical transcription, cross-lingual gen-eration, paralinguistic classification, and complex audio reasoning, allowing us to test whether pre-serving phoneme-transition regions specifically benefits tasks that require precise lexical recovery. 

Baselines. We compare against SpeechPrune (Lin et al., 2025), SparseVLM (Zhang et al., 2025b), and LTBM (Luo et al., 2026), representing the two families of prior work discussed in §2. We additionally include a Random Pruning baseline that retains a uniformly random token subset at matched compression rates, providing a lower-bound sanity check. All baselines are implemented under matched compression schedules using released hyperparameters where available. 

Implementation Details. We evaluate four target token compression rates - 0.36 , 0.51 , 0.64 , and 

0.75 - representing the fraction of audio tokens removed by the final decoder layer. These correspond to uniform per-stage retention factors r ∈ { 0.8, 0.7, 0.6, 0.5} across two pruning layers ( 1 − r2) in our experiments, ranging from light reduction to aggressive 4× compression. Pruning is scheduled at layer 1 1 and at a backbone-specific later layer ℓ† (Eq. 4) via progressive top-k retention on the fused score s(ℓ) 

> t

(Eq. 5). The acoustic prior wt is computed from a single mid-network encoder layer per backbone: layer 16 for Qwen2-Audio, layer 14 for Phi-4-Multimodal, and layer 15 for Audio Flamingo 3. These layers are selected by the label-free kurtosis criterion of §4 (the depth maximizing κ of dt); forced alignments are used only to validate this choice post hoc and are not required to apply AudioTETRIS to a new backbone. The diagnostic prior bt is computed from the same layer. All experiments are executed on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU. Layer choices, calibration, and alternative schedules are detailed in Appendices A.2.2 and A.3.3.

6Preprint 5.2 MAIN RESULTS 

We report overall performance and efficiency across four compression rates on Qwen2-Audio in Table 1. Three key observations emerge from these results: 

AudioTETRIS maintains high accuracy even under heavy compression. At low compression (rate 0.36), AudioTETRIS matches or improves upon the uncompressed baseline on many bench-marks (4.52 WER, 58.04% IEMOCAP, 42.36% MMAU-mini vs. 4.61, 55.11%, 42.20% uncom-pressed). Even at rate 0.75, WER stays at 8.50 while IEMOCAP, MMAU-mini, and CoVoST2 remain within 2 points of uncompressed baselines, confirming that our method removes redundant tokens without disrupting essential lexical and semantic transitions. 

Performance gaps widen as compression grows aggressive. At rate 0.36, several baselines remain competitive; by rate 0.75, baseline methods degrade sharply (random pruning WER climbs to 31.59), lacking a mechanism to safeguard acoustic transitions. AudioTETRIS outperforms the best baseline by over 1.2 WER points at the highest compression setting. 

ASR is inherently more vulnerable to token pruning. Fine-grained transcription (WER) de-grades far faster under aggressive compression than high-level understanding (MMAU-mini) or emotion recognition (IEMOCAP): at rate 0.75, IEMOCAP and MMAU-mini stay nearly intact while WER roughly doubles. High-level tasks rely on coarse acoustic features and tolerate token dropout, whereas exact lexical recovery needs fine-grained transition preservation - a vulnerability that speech-specific methods (AudioTETRIS, SpeechPrune) mitigate far better than general-purpose vision baselines or non-speech heuristics. 5.3 EFFICIENCY ANALYSIS 

Pruning within the LLM backbone yields direct computational savings, dropping total TFLOPs from 7.89 to 4.32 at a 0.75 compression rate, with latency falling from 1.31 s to 0.62 s over the same range. In-LM methods (AudioTETRIS, SparseVLM) incur slightly higher TFLOPs than single-shot methods (SpeechPrune, LTBM) since early layers still process the full, unpruned sequence before the first pruning stage; this difference is minimal relative to AudioTETRIS’s accuracy gains. Real-world latency scales somewhat less than the FLOP drop due to RoPE reindexing and KV-cache trimming overhead at intermediate layers (Appendix A.1.4). 5.4 GENERALIZATION ACROSS BACKBONES 

To test whether AudioTETRIS’s gains are specific to Qwen2-Audio’s architecture, we repeat the main comparison on Phi-4-Multimodal and Audio Flamingo 3 at compression ratio 0.51 (Figure 4). On both backbones, AudioTETRIS remains within 1 point of uncompressed WER and attains the strongest or near-strongest accuracy on MMAU-mini, while baselines that match it on one metric (e.g., WER) fall sharply behind on others (e.g., SpeechPrune loses over 17 BLEU points on CoV-oST2); Full numbers are given in Table 4 (Phi-4-Multimodal) and Table 5 (Audio Flamingo 3). These results suggest the phonetic transition prior transfers across encoders with different subsam-pling rates and architectures (Phi-4-Multimodal’s Conformer encoder vs. the Whisper-based en-coders of Qwen2-Audio and Audio Flamingo 3), consistent with encoder-state transitions being a general property of speech representations rather than an artifact of one backbone. Extended results across all four compression ratios and benchmarks are in Appendix A.3.1, where similar patterns hold.

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We presented AudioTETRIS, a training-free framework for compressing audio tokens in LALMs. By leveraging the audio encoder’s hidden-state dynamics as a phoneme-transition prior and fusing it with depth-adaptive decoder attention, AudioTETRIS preserves acoustically rich and semantically critical regions without requiring auxiliary training or fine-tuning. Experiments across Qwen2-Audio, Phi-4-Multimodal, and Audio Flamingo 3, demonstrate that AudioTETRIS retains near-lossless performance at light compression, while widening its advantage over training-free baselines as compression gets aggressive. Furthermore, our findings highlight that ASR is highly sensitive to token loss making speech-aware structural priors essential for exact lexical recovery. Ultimately, this work highlights a broader principle for multimodal architecture design and token-compression methods: intermediate representations computed by modality-specific encoders contain valuable structural geometry that should be propagated forward rather than discarded at the LLM interface. LIMITATIONS 

AudioTETRIS’s acoustic prior assumes speech-encoder hidden states exhibit large-norm transitions at phonetic boundaries - validated for Whisper-based encoders (Qwen2-Audio, Audio Flamingo 3) and the Conformer-based Phi-4-Multimodal encoder, but not confirmed for encoders trained with different objectives or aggressive temporal downsampling (e.g., HTSAT-style audio-event encoders). Second, while the early pruning layer is fixed by construction, the later crossover layer ℓ† requires a short backbone-specific calibration pass (Appendix A.2.2) - a one-time cost, but the method is not fully zero-shot to unseen architectures without it. Third, because pruning happens progressively inside the decoder rather than once before prefill, AudioTETRIS carries a small TFLOP overhead relative to single-shot methods (§5.3), though this is largely offset by its accuracy gains. Finally, our evaluation spans four English-source benchmarks; results on noisier, or multilingual audio remain untested, and pushing compression beyond the rates studied here would likely erode lexical accuracy first, given ASR’s sensitivity to aggressive compression. 9Preprint ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS

A.1.3 SPARSE VLM A DAPTATION TO FIXED -R ATE SPEECH PRUNING 

SparseVLM (Zhang et al., 2025b) originally proposed an adaptive, dynamic pruning schedule based on the rank of the visual-text attention matrix. However, its public release 2 replaces this dynamic controller with static token-retention schedules. Because the dynamic rank-based ratio is absent from all usable releases ( main , v1.5 , v2.0 ), we could not evaluate SparseVLM’s originally de-scribed adaptive deployment directly. Instead, we adapt its public attention-guided scoring mecha-nism to our fixed-rate setup for a direct, controlled comparison. 

Adaptation. We retain SparseVLM’s core scoring mechanism - using the text raters, and text-token attention to rate audio token importance - and apply it at layers {1, 15 } (Appendix A.2.2). At each stage, we keep the top fraction r of audio tokens by score to match our evaluated compression targets ( 0.36 , 0.51 , 0.64 , 0.75 ). Because the eager fallback is confined to exactly two decoder layers per backbone (layer 1 and that backbone’s calibrated ℓ†;§5.1), its contribution to total latency is small in practice, and it could be eliminated entirely in a production deployment with a custom fused kernel that computes only the row/column marginal attention sums our scoring requires, without materializing the full attention matrix - a one-time kernel-engineering cost rather than a potential structural limitation of the method. Finally, the 11-stage dense schedule underperforms at all rates, showing that frequent, fine-grained pruning could accumulate more errors for the ASR task. Table 10 highlights the efficiency trade-offs at rate 0.51. We observe that the two-stage compression schedule offers one of the most favorable balance between accuracy and computational cost. In contrast, three-stage and dense schedules introduce a slight overhead ( 1.04 × and 1.21 × latency, re-spectively) without consistent downstream gains, confirming the 2-stage compression is an effective overall configuration.
