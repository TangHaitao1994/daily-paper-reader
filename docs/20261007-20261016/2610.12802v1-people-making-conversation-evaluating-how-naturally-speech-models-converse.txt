## Abstract 

A conversational speech model has to decide, at every pause, whether to speak. Existing evaluations test boundary detection, policy adherence, or aggregate statis-tics, not this decision. We place real-time speech systems at 427 pauses and 482 turn ends from 176 unscripted conversations in Seamless Interaction, give each the conversation so far, and score what it generates against what the person did: whether and how soon it speaks at a pause; its latency, length, and wording at a turn end; and whether it can answer a question about the preceding 30 to 120 seconds. Across six systems, the hosted APIs (GPT Realtime, Gemini 3.1 Flash Live, Grok Voice) respond at almost every pause whichever endpointer they use; only their latency, about 1 to 4 s with silence-based endpointers and about 8 s with semantic turn detection, decides whether the words land inside the pause. The full-duplex models (PersonaPlex, Moshi) are the only systems that regularly stay silent and the only ones that interrupt within a human reaction time. Hosted systems reply longer than the person. Given the history as text, hosted systems recall 41 to 60% of conversation facts under a Claude Opus 5.5 judge and 19 to 26% under a strict word matcher; hearing it as audio, full-duplex models recall 17 to 22% and 12 to 15%. We release the labels, points, harness, and every output. 2

## 1 Introduction 

A conversational model must know when to say nothing. A listener can take the floor, acknowledge without taking it, or keep listening. The choice depends on the conversation so far, not only on whether the other person has stopped speaking. A short response latency does not establish whether the system should have spoken. Natural here means doing what the person did at that moment: silent where they were silent, replying where they replied, with similar timing and length; no listener rates the outputs. PEOPLE MAKING CONVERSATION does that. From 176 casual conversations we select two kinds of moment. A pause point is a silence inside a speaker’s turn that a system might mistake for its cue; the person listening did not speak, and the speaker went on. A turn-end point is a boundary where the floor passed and the person replied. The system receives the conversation up to the moment, then hears the other speaker continue in real time (Figure 1). At a pause we score whether it stays silent or takes the floor, and how quickly. At a turn end we score how soon it begins, how long it speaks relative to the person, and how close its words are to theirs. After 30 to 120 seconds of history we also ask a spoken question about something said earlier, and a judge scores the answer. ## 2 Related work 

Moshi reports aggregate timing statistics [1]. The Gemini 3.1 Flash Live and GPT-Live releases report audio reasoning, function calling, and human preference, but no boundary-level account of which decisions the model gets right [2, 3]. Model papers such as BayLing-Duplex and SALMONN-omni score scenario success [4, 5]. Table 1 lists the corpora of two-party conversation and Table 2 the benchmarks. Turn-taking is measured in three ways. Detection benchmarks ask whether a system marks the events a human annotator marked: end of turn, pause, backchannel, interruption. Aggregate evaluations ask whether generated dialogue resembles human dialogue in its statistics, or whether people prefer it [3, 15]. Each answers its own question; none asks ours. The detection family is the nearest to ours. What it scores is whether a boundary event was emitted at the right time. What we score is what a generative system does at a boundary a person actually faced, in a conversation nobody staged, and what it retains from the minutes before. Their stimuli are elicited or synthetic by design, which buys controlled conditions; ours are casual by design, which buys the decisions we want to measure. The two are complementary, and a system should be run on both. Our hosted pause results differ from benchmarks where hosted systems appear to hold at pauses. Those evaluations let the provider’s turn detector decide when a turn ended and score the detection; we requested a response once a silence-based endpointer fired, so what we measure is that configuration’s behaviour and latency, not whether the model could hold under a different turn detector (§6). 

## 3 The benchmark 

3.1 Corpus 

The corpus is the naturalistic subset of Seamless Interaction [21]: 1,395 prompted, casual sessions of at least seven minutes, in which two participants were given a topic card and talked. We select by liveliness alone. Six traits are computed from the released transcripts and voice activity (speech-time balance, speaker alternations per minute, short-utterance rate, question rate, one minus the longest single-speaker share, overlaps per minute); a conversation’s score is the mean of its six percentile ranks among the 1,395. The 176 at or above 0.75 are the corpus (35.9 hours, mean 12.2 minutes, median 8.7; Appendix A). No model selects conversations; the LLM judge of §3.3 selects points within them. The score favours lively conversation, so rates reported here are not population estimates. 

3.2 Labels 

Each speaker’s channel is labelled with speech onsets and offsets, turns, pauses, backchannels, and overlaps. Voice activity and transcripts are the corpus’s own; voiced stretches of at least 350 ms with no transcript are transcribed and aligned by Qwen3-ASR and Qwen3-ForcedAligner. A deterministic rule engine places every label and records the rule and confidence behind it. Gaps of at most 250 ms inside one speaker’s activity are closed. A listener segment of at most 1 s and two words during the other speaker’s turn is a backchannel. At every offset the engine looks ahead 5 s: if the same 3speaker resumes it is a pause; if the other speaker starts substantively it is an end of turn; if the other speaker starts and yields it is a pause and a failed take; if nobody speaks it is an end of turn. A voice activity projection model [24] and three heuristics flag labels for review: a turn ended while the projection keeps the speaker on the floor ( P ≥ 0.65 ), a backchannel where it hands the floor over ( P ≥ 0.60 ), a turn with half its words from gap-fill, and a lexical turn under 400 ms; 19,933 turns produce 5,447 flags (27.3%, Appendix B). The points in this paper are built on the engine’s labels. Every pause and turn-end window in the run was played with its context to two annotators and accepted, an accept-or-reject check with no per-window log. 

3.3 Evaluation points 

The 176 conversations contain about 46,000 candidate boundaries; the point set is curated from them in two stages. Hard gates reject events that cannot be scored cleanly: pauses shorter than 600 ms or overlapped by the listener, active turns shorter than 1 s or three words, turn ends whose reply starts more than 3 s later or has fewer than five words, and events whose audio fails the scorer’s speech gate. Survivors are ranked by a heuristic over pause length, turn context, and the last words before the boundary, keeping at most three per type per conversation. A GPT-4o judge then reads a transcript window around each survivor. It rejects incoherent windows, pauses that hand the turn to the listener, interrupted fragments, and unresponsive replies, and ranks the rest by how tempting the pause is. An event the judge scores zero is never selected. This gives 427 pause points and 482 turn-end points across 157 and 171 of the 176 conversations; the run reported here draws a seeded random 300 of each. At a selected pause the engine found no listener words and at a selected turn end the person replied substantively, so the human reference is hold or take, never backchannel. Appendix A gives the funnel. Recall points test memory. For each prefix of 30, 60, 90, and 120 seconds ending at a completed turn, a question is spoken to the system in the user’s voice, cloned by Qwen3-TTS from 20 to 40 s of their speech. Conversation recall asks, in the user’s words and addressed to the system, about a specific fact either speaker stated in the first 30 seconds. The system plays speaker 1; the question is spoken in speaker 2’s cloned voice, with “I” and “me” referring to speaker 2 and “you” to speaker 1. A correct answer states the fact from speaker 1’s position, whichever speaker originally said it (example in Table 10). GPT-4o extracts it, a judge checks it for specificity, and it is dropped if its spoken form leaks a speaker label. Answers are scored by Claude Opus 5.5 (temperature 0); GPT-4o scores of the same transcripts are kept for comparison (Table 12). The same question is asked at every history length. Fact recall is the control. It asks one general-knowledge question from a fixed list of twelve, assigned per conversation, whose answer does not depend on the history; a system that fails it did not answer the question it was asked. A judge-approved conversation fact exists for 46 conversations, giving 184 conversation-recall points; fact recall has 669, a prefix being skipped when no turn completes before its target. The run uses all 184 and a seeded random 300. 

3.4 Protocol 

A rollout has two phases: history preload, then a live stream that is scored. History is capped at 164 s before the active turn, PersonaPlex’s context window. Full-duplex models receive it as two audio channels, the other speaker’s and their own, teacher-forced with word timestamps. Hosted models receive completed turns as text and the trailing user audio; their APIs accept nothing else. Every system also receives a short identity prompt, written once per speaker by GPT-4o from the transcript: who it is, how it knows the other person, and that the assistant history is its own prior speech. At a pause point every system is streamed the active turn, the recorded pause, and the speaker resuming, as real-time frames, so it hears the pause as real silence; a full-duplex model steps through it frame by frame, and a hosted model’s server-side endpointer fires on it. The endpointer is the system’s turn-taking policy. At a turn-end point the trigger differs by family: a hosted model receives an explicit end-of-turn signal and a requested response, with its endpointer off so a mid-turn pause cannot interrupt it; a full-duplex model must detect the end from the audio itself and then receives silence frames until it stops or reaches the cap. The person’s reply is not played back. Drain lengths, caps, and audio format are in Appendix C. We score the first rollout of each point; a second rollout from the same run is released but not reported. Every output is recorded with its input timing, so every score can be recomputed from the rollout. 43.5 Metrics 

Speech intervals are found by an energy gate calibrated against Whisper word timestamps on the systems’ own output (Appendix D). The gate alone is a weak classifier of speech, so a response 

requires transcribed words; above-gate audio with an empty transcript is wordless phonation , reported as its own category. 1. Pause recognition. On the stream of §3.4, a takeover is a word-bearing interval overlapping the labelled pause; otherwise the system held , either silently or by speaking after the person resumed. We report the shares silent, takeover, and after, the wordless rate, and takeover latency from pause start to the first words, zero if the system was already speaking. 2. Turn-end latency. Time from the turn end to the onset of the system’s first transcribed word, on the playback clock; a word that began before the turn end gives zero or less. 3. Response length. Duration from first to last speech interval and word count, each as a ratio to the human reply. 4. Response similarity. Token F1 and cosine similarity of all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings be-tween the system’s transcript and the human reply. 5. Recall. Whether the spoken answer is correct, judged by Claude Opus 5.5 against the gold answer or the conversation, reported by history length. 

## 4 Experiments 

4.1 Systems 

We evaluate six real-time speech systems in two families. Self-hosted full-duplex models listen and speak at once and run on Modal: PersonaPlex-7B [25] and Moshi [1]. Hosted real-time APIs take audio in and return audio out, with the provider’s server-side voice activity detection deciding when the user’s turn has ended: GPT Realtime ( gpt-realtime-2025-08-28 ) [23], Gemini 3.1 Flash Live (preview) [2], and Grok Voice ( grok-voice-latest ) [26]. GPT Realtime is run at two silence settings, 300 ms and 500 ms, which isolates the endpointer’s contribution within one model. The 500 ms setting is OpenAI’s documented server-VAD default; 300 ms is the faster variant [27]. For pause points only, GPT Realtime is also run with OpenAI’s semantic turn detection at eagerness low ,the setting most willing to wait; turn-end and recall evaluations turn detection off and commit the turn explicitly, so this configuration has no results there. GPT Realtime and Grok use a detection threshold of 0.5, Gemini’s automatic detection closes on 500 ms of silence, and every hosted endpointer uses 300 ms of prefix padding. Versions, evaluation dates, and settings for every system are in Appendix C. 

4.2 What is held fixed 

Every system sees the same points, the same history cap, the same identity prompt, the same live audio frames, and the same speech gate, and is scored by the same code. One thing differs and is not hidden: full-duplex models hear the history and hosted models read it, so cross-family recall comparisons carry that caveat (§6). 

4.3 Reference points 

Two policies bound the pause and turn-end metrics without running anything: never speak holds every pause and takes no turn; always speak takes every pause at zero latency. The human, by construction of the point set, held every pause and took every turn end, with a length ratio of 1 and a similarity of 1. Similarity has a measured floor, the human reply from a different point in the same conversation (median token F1 0.08, cosine 0.12 over 240 pairs), and a measured ceiling, the human reply against its own label transcript (0.88, 0.89), the transcription noise every system is scored through. Recall has a ceiling too: a text model reading the transcript answers 97% of the conversation-recall questions under the GPT-4o judge (Appendix E), so a score is a fact about memory, not about the questions.

## 5 Results 

Pause points (Figure 2, Table 3). The hosted systems almost never stayed silent (0 to 2% of points): their silence-based endpointers request a response once speech ends. Silent shares and no-audio outcomes are reported separately (Table 15). Hosted speech began a median 1.0 to 1.1 s after the pause began for GPT Realtime, 2.7 s for Gemini, and 3.9 s for Grok over all rollouts (Table 3 gives the median over takeovers only), against a median labelled pause of 0.97 s. Whether the words land inside the pause, a takeover on 7 to 26% of points, or after the person has resumed, on 72 to 86%, is set by the latency, not by a decision. Grok’s low takeover rate is its slowness, not restraint: its words arrive after the person has resumed on 86% of points. The full-duplex models are the only systems that stay silent, on 22% (PersonaPlex) and 39% (Moshi) of points, because nothing forces a full-duplex model to respond. They are also the only ones that take the floor within a human reaction time, a median 216 and 373 ms in; three in ten of PersonaPlex’s takeovers and a quarter of Moshi’s were speech already under way at the pause. Raising GPT Realtime’s endpointer from 300 to 500 ms cuts its takeover rate from 26 to 17% and delays its speech by 80 ms: the endpointer is the policy. Semantic turn detection at low eagerness changes the timing more than the outcome: on the 173 pauses shared with the main set it produced no words on 5% (9 points, several with wordless audio), took the floor inside the pause on 6% (500 ms: 17%; 300 ms: 26%), and spoke after the person resumed on 88%, its first words a median 6 s after the pause ended (Table 3, Figure 2). Wordless phonation is a full-duplex phenomenon, 6% of PersonaPlex’s points and 1% of Moshi’s.

## 6 Limitations 

Hosted models read the history as text and full-duplex models hear it, so the cross-family recall gap is partly a gap between channels; streaming audio history to the hosted models is future work. The hosted systems’ near-zero silence is a property of the silence-based endpointer configurations we tested, which request a response once speech ends, not a decision their models failed to make. Semantic turn detection was tested at one setting, low eagerness, on pause points only. A harness audit found that scoring could stall the shared audio stream and the drain could outlast queued playback, so reaching the 30 s cap does not establish continued speech, full-duplex durations can include later resumptions, and latencies can reflect stalls. GPT-4o still selects the points and extracts the recall facts, sharing a provider with two systems under test. Turn-end comparisons depend on the family-specific response triggers of §3.4; reply playback remains untested. The gate is an energy threshold, no score has a human-human variance, and we report one rollout per point. Labels are rule-based and only spot-checked: on 42 re-annotated windows, 86 to 95% of boundaries fall within 250 ms of a human’s, a 250 ms shift can reorder individual systems but not families (Appendix E), and humans hear a backchannel in 7 of 20 pause windows labelled silent. Finally, the corpus is English and dyadic.

Review. The labels used for the evaluation points are the engine’s, and every evaluated window passed the two-annotator accept-or-reject check of §3.2. Separately, four annotators are re-annotating conversations in full in a review tool, and an administrator approves each conversation. At submission, 11 conversations (143 minutes, 1,260 engine turns) are approved; Table 6 compares them with the engine. We match each engine turn to the human turn of the same speaker that overlaps it most, and call it matched if the overlap covers more than half of it. Three things follow. First, humans merge: 984 of the 1,260 engine turns match a human turn, and the humans’ 928 turns are fewer than the engine’s. Second, humans call more backchannels: 226 engine turns (18%) became backchannels, because the engine’s rule (at most 1 s and two words) is stricter than a person’s judgement. Third, boundaries move: of the matched turns, 49% have a start or end moved by more than 100 ms, 39% by more than 250 ms, and 22% by more than 1 s, the last being merges. The flagger is informative but not sufficient: 81% of flagged engine turns were changed, against 55% of unflagged ones. For the benchmark these differences cut in a conservative direction. A listener response the engine called a turn blocks a pause point (a listener word inside the hold), so the mislabel removes points rather than corrupting them, and a reply that a person would call a backchannel is already excluded from turn-end points by the five-word gate. ## D Gate sensitivity 

The speech gate has three parameters, threshold 0.01, release ratio 0.5, and minimum duration 80 ms, each recorded in every pause score so a score can be recomputed from its own artifact. They were calibrated against Whisper word timestamps over the systems’ own output audio, a label source independent of any energy threshold: each response is tiled with 2 s windows at a 1 s hop, each window is peak-normalised before decoding so the labeller cannot inherit the level bias being measured, and a word counts only if Whisper’s no-speech head, mean log-probability, and repetition guard all pass and every window covering the instant agrees. On human reference speech the labeller validates 49 of 62 windows, the misses being real pauses; on digital silence, floor noise, broadband noise at 0.05, and a 120 Hz tone it labels nothing. Table 8 gives the sweep. An Otsu split of the 21,580 envelope windows lands at 3.2 × 10 −4; against the labels it opens intervals a median 840 ms early and moves the pause score of the calibration run from 0.544 to 0.228. Onset error keeps falling above 0.01 (mean absolute error 282 ms at 0.01, 169 ms at 0.05) but only by dropping labelled speech; 0.01 is the highest threshold that still matches every labelled utterance and the one at which the onset bias crosses zero (median 0 ms on pause rollouts, 

−140 ms on turn-end rollouts). A missed utterance is the error that matters, because it turns a takeover 13 into a hold. The same rule fixes the release ratio: 0.75 improves onset error (308 against 410 ms) but loses a labelled utterance. Minimum durations from 40 to 80 ms are indistinguishable. The gate remains a mediocre classifier of lexical speech (AUC 0.80 on pause rollouts, 0.85 on turn-end rollouts): labelled speech runs down to 8 × 10 −4 and unlabelled audio up to 1.8 × 10 −2, which is the wordless phonation of §3.5, speech-like sound (dominant frequency 110 to 135 Hz) that Whisper cannot decode and that is not an echo of the user channel.
